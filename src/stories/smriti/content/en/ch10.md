*"Yes. Ananya. Not Gauraiya. I promised to tell you on the last day, didn't I? So here. A-na-nya. Ananya Rao. I told you it was a terrible name. You know what it means? 'The one without a second.' Meera sat on the bed and let the tape run on into silence for a long time before she pressed STOP herself.

She did not cry. She was a little surprised by that. She sat very still with her hand on the recorder, the plastic warm from the motor, and found that what she felt was not grief, or not only grief. It was something closer to recognition. The girl on the tape had been more completely herself in forty minutes than in seven years of Meera's remembering. Funny, and frightened, and furious, and careful of other people, and a little vain, and brave in the specific, unglamorous way of a person who checks the fire doors in every building she enters because once she could not open one.

*I'm keeping that one.*

So Session 2 had not been recorded on the seventh of September. It had been recorded on the night of the fourteenth, in Room 6, on the other side of the wall from Meera's bed, starting a few minutes before eleven and ending a minute or two after five past eleven, when the mains failed and the generator coughed three times and died, and Sister Annamma came down the corridor with her lantern, and Ananya Rao said *chalo* and pulled on her yellow raincoat.

The cassette lay in the recorder. Meera opened the little door and took it out and held it under the bulb.

The label. *HIMR · VOICE LETTERS*, printed. Then, in blue gel pen, round and quick and leaning slightly backwards, the hand that had made a colony newspaper at nine and sold it for fifty paise: *Meera ke liye — G.*

And below it, in black ballpoint, a date. *7.9.19.*

She had noticed on the very first night, in her flat in Shyam Nagar, that the date was in a different pen. She had thought, then, of a librarian dating a book. Now she took the photograph out of its envelope and turned it over, and laid it on the bed beside the cassette, the black-ink caption beside the black-ink date.

*Cohort 4 — Farewell — 13.9.19.*

*7.9.19.*

Full stops between the numbers. The same firm black ballpoint. The same nine, beginning with a small loop at the top before it came down.

She did not need the magnifying glass. She did not need a document examiner, not for this. It did not prove whose hand it was. But it proved, as plainly as anything on a page can prove anything, that the hand that had written *farewell* on the back of a photograph taken during the power cut on the fourteenth had also written a date on this tape. And that date was the date of the first recording session. The afternoon in Therapy B, with the barbet and the pressure cooker. The seventh.

Not the fourteenth. Not the night.

Whoever had dated this cassette had known about the voice-letter session. They had probably looked it up in a register, as Meera would have, and written down the day Ananya had sat in Therapy B and recorded her letter. They had labelled it, the way the institute labelled everything. And then they had put it away with the photograph, a tape from a harmless afternoon and a picture from a harmless farewell. As if neither of them had anything to do with the night Ananya Rao disappeared.

Either they had never listened far enough to hear the rain, or they had heard it, and dated the tape a week early anyway.
