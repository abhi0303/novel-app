The first page Shibu had photographed was not the fifteenth. It was the twenty-ninth of August, and Meera read it sitting up straight on the edge of the bed with her bare feet flat on the cold floor, as she had been told to.

*29.8.19 — 02:10 — Round. R7 (Meera mol) not in bed. Door open. Found at end of corridor at E door, standing, facing door, eyes open. Right hand flat on the glass. Did not answer when I said her name. Did not look at me. Took her arm and walked her back. She came like a child, no trouble. In bed she slept at once. Morning: asked if she slept well. "Like the dead, Sister." No memory. Informed Madam (phone) 07:00. Madam: "Observe. Do not tell her. It will frighten her. Keep the E door key-switch locked."*

Meera read it twice. Then she put the phone face down on the quilt and sat for a while with her hands pressed together between her knees.

*Best sleep since February,* she had written in her diary the next morning. *It's the tablet.* And on the same page: *Woke up very cold. Feet like ice. G. says I'm a restless sleeper, she can hear me through the wall.*

She turned the phone over.

*6.9.19 — 01:40 — Round. Heard voices at E end. R7 at E door again, standing, hand on the glass. G. (R6) already with her, holding her other hand, talking to her very softly. G. said: "She was patting the glass with her palm, Sister. I heard her door." Walked her back together. In the corridor G. asked me: "What is she taking? Tell me what it is." I said I cannot tell you, it is the doctor's order. G. very angry. Said: "Then somebody should tell HER." Informed Madam 07:00. Madam: "Continue."*

*11.9.19 — 02:30 — R7 in corridor, walking slowly towards E door. Before I reached, G. came out of R6 and turned her round and brought her back to bed. G. was dressed. Raincoat on the hook by her door, I saw. I think G. is sitting up at night now. I think she is not sleeping. Informed Madam. Madam: "Dose to continue until end of programme. Two more weeks. Then stop."*

Meera put the phone down again.

She did not need to look at the black stubs in the night register in her photographs. She knew now what had been on the three pages cut out between the twenty-eighth of August and the twelfth of September. Three nights. Three *events*. Room 7, standing at the red door at two in the morning with her eyes open and her hand on the glass, while the magnet held because the power was on, and a nurse led her back to bed and a director on the telephone said *do not tell her*.

*Meera, tum raat ko—*

She heard it, in the voice on the cassette, the sentence that had been recorded over by the rain. She did not have to imagine the end of it. It was written here, in a nurse's round hand, three times, with dates.

*Meera, tum raat ko chalti ho.*

Meera, at night, you walk.

---

There were two pages for the night of the fourteenth.

The first was short and the handwriting shook.

*14.9.19 — 23:05 — Power gone. Generator not starting. Lanterns. All residents (6+G) to common room. G. in raincoat. 23:32 photo (G. asked). 23:36 all to rooms, I took them down with the lantern. G. took R7 in herself. 23:39 Madam phoned from Dehradun. On the phone till 23:53. Lights came 23:48 while I was talking. NO ROUND AFTER. I sat in the duty room. I was tired. It was the last night. God forgive me.*

The second was the morning.

*15.9.19 — 06:00 — Round. R6 (G.) not in room. Bed not slept in, only sat on. Recorder on the bed. Raincoat, outdoor shoes, torch gone.*

*15.9.19 — 06:10 — R7 (Meera mol). Asleep in bed on top of the blanket. Nightdress wet through, completely, cold. Mud on the nightdress up to the knees. Both feet: mud to the ankles. Left sole: many small cuts, gravel in the cuts, one deep. Right foot: cuts on the toes and heel. Left slipper missing. Right slipper on the floor by the bed, full of mud. In her hair: wet deodar needles, small leaves. Woke her. Very confused. Did not know where she was. Said, in Hindi, half asleep: "Gauraiya bol rahi thi andar jao." Then: "Darwaza band karna hai." Then slept again at once. Cleaned feet, removed gravel, dressed with povidone and gauze, bandaged. TT given 06:15. She did not wake again properly. 06:20 phoned Madam. Told her everything. Madam: "Put her in the car with Rawat-ji before anyone wakes. Pack her bag. Say her mother is ill. Do not write this in the register."*

Underneath, in the same hand but smaller, as if added later the same day:

*I am writing it here.*

---

Meera did not know how long she sat there.

*Gauraiya bol rahi thi andar jao.* Gauraiya was saying, go inside.

*Darwaza band karna hai.* I have to close the door.

Not a recovered memory. Not a voice at a glass that she could not place in time and had refused to write on the right-hand side of her notebook. Her own words, spoken aloud at ten past six on the morning of the fifteenth of September 2019, half asleep, with gravel in her feet, and written down within minutes by a nurse who had been told not to write anything, and had written it anyway in a cheap blue notebook and hidden it under her wedding sari for seven years.

She made herself do it properly. She made herself go back through it the way she had gone through everything else.

*Mud to the knees.* The iron landing outside the red door was a grid of bars; rain fell straight through it to the terrace below. There was no mud on it. There could not be.

*Gravel in the cuts.* Iron does not leave gravel. The cliff path was stone-paved, but she had walked it on Saturday with Dev, and the paving was broken and gritty, with gravel washed onto it by seven monsoons, and in 2019 Dev's own log had recorded *water seepage* from the wall above, running across it.

*Wet deodar needles in her hair.* The landing was hung on a bare stone wall. Nothing grew over it. But the cure-walk, beyond the landing, ran along the face of the cliff under the overhanging deodars of the ridge, the same trees whose needles had been on the path on Saturday, and whose smell she had found in her own trunk in Bani Park.

*Left slipper missing.* A blue institute slipper, found by Dev at six-thirty that morning on the broken edge of the path, thirty-five or forty metres from the door, and given to the nurse, and given to Ira, who had put it in a plastic bag.

She had not stood on the landing watching someone go. She had been out there. Along the path. In the mud and the gravel and the rain, under the deodars, as far as the broken wall.

She had not followed Ananya out.

Ananya had followed her.

---

She knocked on Kabir's door at a quarter past ten. He opened it at once. He had not been asleep either; he was fully dressed, and the canvas bag was on the bed, and his face when he saw hers changed.

"Page three," she said. "Now."

He did not argue. He took the envelope out of the bag and slid the single missing sheet out of it, the blue inland-letter paper with *3* in a circle in the corner, and put it in her hand.

She read it standing in his doorway.

*K., this is the part I can't tell anyone here.*

*M. travels at night. That's what I call it, to make it sound less frightening. She gets up out of bed and walks, with her eyes open, and she doesn't see you, and she doesn't remember in the morning. Twice now I've heard her door at two a.m. and found her at the end of our corridor, at the red door, standing with her hand flat on the glass, like she wants to go out into the rain. The magnet holds when the power's on, thank God. The night nurse finds her too. They know. It's in their EVENTS column. They haven't told her, and last week they put her dose UP.*

*She thinks she's sleeping better than she has in her life. She told me so, so happily. I didn't have the heart.*

*So I sit up now. I listen through the wall for her door. I'm her chowkidar, I told her, as a joke, and she laughed.*

*Here's the thing I keep thinking about, K. If the power ever goes at night, that door opens. It's fail-safe; I asked. And on the other side there's a little iron landing, and a stair, and a path along a cliff that the electrician has taped off because the wall is falling down. I keep my raincoat on the hook by my door, and my torch in the pocket, every night. You'll laugh. Don't laugh.*

*I'll tell her everything on the last day. I can't now. If I tell her now, she'll stop sleeping, and she'll be so ashamed, and they might send her home early with nobody to watch her. Two more weeks. I can do two more weeks.*

Meera lowered the page.

"You've had this for seven years," she said.

"Yes."

"You knew I sleepwalked. You knew she was sitting up for me."

"I knew she wrote that she was." Kabir's voice was very low. "And I knew she'd gone out of that door in a storm and never come back. And I knew there was a girl called M. on the inside who walked at night and didn't remember." He looked at her. "Can you understand what I thought, Meera? For seven years? I thought you'd walked out there in your sleep, and she'd gone after you, and something had happened between you on that path. I thought you'd come back and she hadn't. I didn't know what. I didn't let myself know what. When I read the note in your bag that you closed the door…" He stopped. "I thought, she shut it on her. Without knowing. Asleep. And I couldn't tell you that, because if I told you, you'd remember it whether it happened or not." He put a hand on the door frame. "So I gave you everything else and kept this, and watched you build it yourself. And on Monday, in Dev's quarter, you built it the other way round. She was leaving, and you shut the door behind her. And I sat on the floor and I didn't know which was worse."

"Neither," said Meera. "Neither is what happened."

She held up her phone with the nurse's page on it.

"There was mud to my knees, Kabir. Gravel in my feet. Deodar needles in my hair. My slipper was on the broken edge of the path. I wasn't standing at the door watching. I was out there, at the wall." Her voice was perfectly steady. "And at six in the morning, before anyone had told me anything, before Ira, before the station, before a single beautiful sentence, I said *Gauraiya bol rahi thi andar jao.* Gauraiya was telling me to go inside."

Kabir looked at the screen for a long time. Then he put his hand over his eyes and stood in the doorway of a homestay room in Dunda and did not make any sound at all.

---

Ira Sen came at half past ten.

They heard the car on the stony road and saw its lights swing across the verandah, and Bisht-ji went down in his vest and shawl to see who it was, and came back up the stairs with a face of enormous disapproval to say that there was a lady, an old lady, a doctor, who would not go away.

She was standing on the verandah in the dark beside Bisht-ji's blind mother's empty cane chair, alone. No lawyer. She had a cardboard file under her arm, the old-fashioned kind with cloth tapes, and she held it against her chest with both hands.

"I couldn't wait for the morning," she said, when Meera came down. "I couldn't sleep in that hotel with this in my bag and you up here." She held out the file. "The SP can have it tomorrow. You should have it first."

Meera took it. They went into the homestay's front room, where the family kept a sofa with lace on its arms for guests, and Ira sat down on it without being asked, and Meera untied the tapes.

Three sheets of ruled paper, cut close to a binding: the missing pages of the night register, the twenty-ninth of August, the sixth and the eleventh of September, in Annamma's round blue hand. The same words, near enough, as the photographs on Meera's phone. *R7 at E door, standing, hand flat on the glass. G. already with her.*

A single sheet of Ira's own notepaper, in her sloping black ballpoint, dated *15.9.19 — evening*: *Dev: torch on cure-walk going E ~23:43. Crash ~23:45. M. behind E door glass ~23:46, closing. Sister: R7 06:10 — wet, mud to knees, gravel, deodar needles, L slipper missing. "Gauraiya bol rahi thi andar jao." Slipper on edge = 7.* And under it, underlined once, in a hand that for once was not perfectly controlled: *She went after her.*

And at the bottom of the file, in a clear plastic bag gone yellow with age, sealed with brown tape, a blue rubber slipper.

It was stiff, and the mud had dried on it to a pale grey crust and flaked away in places. Meera did not open the bag. She did not need to. Through the plastic, on the inside of the heel, where every slipper in the box in the linen store had its number, a white painted numeral showed through the dried mud.

*7.*

---

"You knew," said Meera. "On the Sunday. On the first evening."

"I knew by seven o'clock that evening," said Ira. She sat very straight on the lace-covered sofa. "I had the slipper in my hand and the nurse's account and Dev's, and three pages I'd had cut out of my own register because they recorded you standing at that door in the night with your hand on the glass. I'm not a stupid woman. I didn't need a policeman to tell me what had happened."

"Tell me."

"You walked." Ira's voice did not shake. "On ten milligrams of a drug I had told the nurse to give you, against a warning I had read in April, that I had decided did not apply to a young woman under my care. The power failed. The magnet let go of the door that it had held shut on you three times before. You walked out of it into the storm and along the path to the place I'd been told on the second of September was about to fall. And Ananya Rao, who had been sitting up in her room for two weeks in her raincoat waiting for exactly that, because I would not tell you and would not stop it, went after you." She was silent a moment. "She reached you at the tape. She turned you round. She told you to go inside and close the door. You did. And the wall went, with her on the outside edge of it, holding the light for you."

The front room was very quiet. Somewhere in the house a clock ticked.

"And you told me I slept," said Meera.

"I told you that you slept. I told Dev he'd seen nothing. I told the police she walked out of the gate." Ira looked at her. "Do you want to know why? I've had seven years to make the reason sound well. I made it sound like this: that you could not have survived it. Ashok's daughter, five months after him, sedated, injured, told in a railway station that the girl who had made you laugh had died bringing you back from a walk you didn't know you'd taken. I told myself that knowledge would kill you. I told myself it was mercy." She paused. "It was mercy. For me. The true story was the one that ended my life's work. A girl dead because of a drug I gave without consent to a guest who wasn't on any register. Dev's story was dangerous too, but it was about you. The true one was about me."

"And when I asked you this afternoon if I'd shut her out?"

"I said you were asleep." Ira's mouth moved. "Which was true. You were. Every second of it. Even when you closed the door."

---

There was one thing left, and Meera did it because she had to.

She went upstairs and brought down the recorder and put it on the low table in front of the lace sofa, and rewound the tape to the click where the afternoon in Therapy B gave way to the rain.

"You told the SP you never listened past the beginning," she said.

"I didn't."

"Then you've never heard this."

She pressed PLAY.

The rain on the tin roof of Room 6. The fan ticking. The whisper.

*Meera. Me again. Fourteenth. Night. Tomorrow's the last day.*

*I didn't take my tablet tonight. Or for two weeks, really. I'm awake.*

*Tumhara darwaza abhi tak band hai. Main sun sakti hoon.*

*So jao, Rajkumari. Main hoon.*

The fan winding down. *Arre—* The three thuds of the generator failing. *Light gayi.* The nurse's voice far down the corridor. And then, quick and decided:

*Chalo. Raincoat.*

The zip.

The click.

Ira Sen sat on the lace-covered sofa in the front room of a homestay in Dunda with her hands in her lap and her eyes on the small grey recorder, and Meera watched her hear it, the way she had watched Kabir hear the beginning in her clinic a week ago, and Dev hear his own log in the generator room. She watched it reach her.

Ira did not cry. She sat absolutely still. After a long time she said, very quietly, to no one:

"*Your door's still shut. I can hear it.*"

And then: "She was listening for you. Through the wall. With her raincoat on." She closed her eyes. "The power went, and she knew what that meant, and she put her coat on. Before anything had even happened." She opened them. "I had that tape in my desk for seven months and in my cabinet for six years, and I never once listened far enough to hear her get ready."

---

It was nearly midnight when Meera went back up the hill.

She went because there was one thing that was still on the left-hand side of her notebook, and she knew, now, where it belonged, and she wanted to see it with her own eyes before she moved it.

Dev was at the gate in the dark with his torch, because the SP's order had made him the guardian of a site of interest and he had taken it more seriously than anything in his life. He did not ask why she had come. He took one look at her face, and at Kabir behind her, and unlocked the gate, and led them down the service ramp in the rain and along the lower terrace, past the mortuary and the generator room, to the foot of the iron stair.

"You go first," said Meera to him. "Take the light up. Then come down again and stand here, where you stood. And shine it up at the glass."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he did it.

She climbed the stair after him in the rain, twenty-two iron treads, with her hand on the cold rail. On the landing she waited while Dev went back down, and then she turned and faced the red door from the outside, the way she had never once in seven years let herself imagine facing it.

Red. Fire red, blistered, wet, in the beam of Dev's torch from below.

The small wired window at head height. Beyond it, dim and cream, the corridor where Room 7 was.

She lifted her right hand and put it flat on the glass.

Cold. Wet. The rain ran down over her knuckles and between her fingers and onto the iron at her feet. And through the glass, behind her hand, under it and around it, lit pink by the torch, were the red letters of the old sticker on the inside of the pane. She was looking at them from the wrong side. They were back to front. Every letter reversed, the E's and the R's and the S's all facing the wrong way, the words running from right to left, so that her eye slid across them and would not make them hold.

Letters she could see the shape of and could not read. *They were wrong somehow. They would not make words.*

She had stood on the other side of this glass on Sunday night, inside, in the corridor, with her hand in the same place, and read EMERGENCY EXIT — PUSH BAR TO OPEN without the slightest difficulty. She had written *dream-logic?* in her notebook, because letters in dreams cannot be read, and it was the oldest observation in sleep research, and she taught it to second-year students.

It had not been a dream. It had been the last thing she saw before she went in. She had stood here, on this landing, with the rain on her and the mud to her knees and one slipper gone and gravel in her feet, and put her hand on this glass from the outside, and looked through it at a sticker she could not read, at a corridor she could not reach until she found the edge of the door and pulled. Because a voice behind her on the cliff had told her: *go inside.*

She took her hand off the glass. She found the edge of the door, where it had been left a few inches ajar for her, and pulled it towards her, and stepped through, and turned, and with both hands on the inside bar she drew the red door shut behind her on the rain.

It closed with its soft double clank.

She stood in the dark corridor with her back against the cream steel and her eyes closed. Two metres to her right, Room 7. Under her feet, the floor, which in a moment, seven years ago, had shuddered three times as a man in a generator room below turned an engine over by hand. And then the lights. All at once. Everywhere.

*Tumne hi darwaza band kiya tha.*

You were the one who closed the door.

It had always been true. Every word of it. She had closed it. Not on Ananya. Not behind a girl walking away into the rain to a car on the road. She had closed it on herself, from the inside, as she had been told to, by a girl who had come out after her in the storm with a torch she was afraid to hold over a drop she was afraid of, who had turned her round at the broken wall and pointed her home and stood on the outer edge to light the way, and told her, *andar jao, darwaza band karna*. And had been standing there, still holding the light, when the mountain went.

---

Someone was knocking on the other side of the door. Softly, with a knuckle, on the glass.

She opened her eyes and pushed the bar, and the door swung out, and Kabir was on the landing in the rain with his face wet in the torchlight from below.

"I heard you shut it," he said. "From the stair. I heard the bar." He stopped. "Meera."

"She didn't leave me," said Meera.

She heard her own voice say it, quite calm, in the doorway, with the rain blowing past her. Then she heard it again, as if from somewhere else, as if it were a sentence in a case file she had been reading for a very long time and had only now understood.

"I thought I was looking for where she went," she said. "All week. I thought I was looking for Ananya." She looked past him, along the cliff path, at the pale wet stone running east into the dark and stopping in the air. "I was the one who went missing that night, Kabir. She was looking for me."

Below them, at the foot of the iron stair, Dev Malik had lowered his torch. In the faint light it threw up from the wet terrace Meera could see his face turned up towards them, and she saw him understand. He had stood in that exact place seven years ago and watched a woman in white close a red door, and had believed for seven years that she was shutting someone out.

She had been coming home.
