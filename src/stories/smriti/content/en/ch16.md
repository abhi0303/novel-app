The SDRF found the torch on the Thursday, a little after two in the afternoon, a hundred and forty metres below the gap in the cliff path, where the long tongue of rubble from the old collapse ran out into a thicket of thorn and scree a stone's throw above the river.

Meera was not there. The SP had asked her not to be, and she had agreed, and she had sat instead on the verandah of the homestay in Dunda beside Bisht-ji's mother in her cane chair, listening to the river and not to her phone. When the phone rang, it was Kabir, who had been on the slope with the team since dawn, and for a long moment he did not say anything at all, and she heard the wind and the water behind him and knew.

It was a small aluminium torch, the cheap kind that every room at the institute had been given for power cuts, crushed flat on one side and so corroded that the switch had fused. The lens was gone. On the barrel, under seven years of rust and silt, when the team leader rubbed it with his thumb, was a number painted in white, the way Dev had painted a number on every torch in every room in 2016 so that they would not wander.

*6.*

Near it, caught in the roots of a thorn bush, they found a strip of yellow PVC the length of a hand, with a toggle still stitched to it.

They did not find anything else. The team leader, a quiet Garhwali with grey hair cropped to his skull, came up to the homestay in the evening to tell her so himself, standing at the foot of the verandah steps with his helmet under his arm. They would go down again in the morning, he said, and the morning after, as far as the river let them. But she should know what the river was. It was seven years and seven monsoons. It was the Bhagirathi in September.

"*Nadi sab wapas karti hai,*" said Bisht-ji's mother from her chair, without turning her blind face. The river gives everything back.

"*Haan, Amma,*" said the team leader gently. "*Par sab kuch nahi.*" Yes, Amma. But not everything.

The east wing of St Luke's Sanatorium came down on the following Tuesday, a week later than planned, after the SP lifted her order.

Meera did not watch that either. Dev did. He rang her afterwards, from the gate, with the noise of the excavator still grinding behind him, and told her that it had taken the machine eleven minutes to take down the end of the corridor, Room 7 and the landing and the red door all together, in one long collapse of stone and iron and slate, and that the dust had hung over the ridge for an hour.

He had taken two things out before it came down. He had not asked anyone's permission, and he did not ask hers.

The first was the clock from the common room, with the crack running across its face through the four. He had wrapped it in a towel and taken it home to his quarter.

The second was the small wired-glass panel from the red door. He had unscrewed its steel frame from the inside on the Monday night, with the same screwdriver he had used on Ira's cabinet. The old sticker was still on it, its red letters faded to pink at the edges.

"I'll send them to you, didi," he said. "If you want them. If you don't, I'll keep them." A pause, full of the excavator. "I don't know why I took them. I couldn't let the machine have them."

"Keep the clock," said Meera. "Hang it in your quarter. Put a new battery in it, set it by the radio." She was quiet for a moment. "Send me the glass."

The case went to the magistrate in Uttarkashi in October, with a report that Ananya Rao, aged twenty-four, of Secunderabad, had died on the night of 14 September 2019 in the collapse of the retaining wall of the cure-walk on the Himgiri Institute premises, while attempting to bring back another resident who had left the building in a state of drug-induced sleepwalking. That she had not left the institute voluntarily. That the account given to the police at the time had been false in its material particulars, and that the false account had been given knowingly.

Who was responsible, and for what, was for a court.

A case was registered against Dr Ira Sen for giving false information to the police and for causing evidence to disappear. The State Medical Council opened an inquiry into the administration of zolpidem to research participants and to a non-participant without consent, outside any approved protocol. Mr Mehra issued a statement on his client's behalf that she would cooperate fully and would make no public comment. She made none.

Meera saw her once more before she left Uttarkashi, on the steps of the SP's office, the day after the torch was found. Ira was coming out from a second statement with her lawyer, in the same grey shawl, smaller than ever in the hard mountain light. She stopped when she saw Meera. The lawyer stopped too, and then, at a look from her, went on down to the car.

"I'm told they found her torch," said Ira.

"Yes."

Ira looked out over the valley for a long moment.

"Your father would have reported me," she said. "In 2019. The day after. He'd have driven up from Jaipur and walked into that police chowki and told them everything, and he'd have lost thirty years of friendship doing it, and he'd have done it anyway." She turned back. "You've done it properly. I'm glad it was you."

"I didn't do it for you, Ira-di."

"No. I know." Ira's mouth moved. "I spent my whole life, Meera, trying to make the worst things people remember hurt them less. I never once asked any of them whether that was what they wanted. I thought it was obviously what they wanted." She paused. "I gave you a kinder story than the true one, and you've spent a week tearing it down with your hands. I'm not going to ask you to forgive me. I wouldn't know what to do with it." She drew her shawl round her shoulders. "Tell it right. That's all. She asked you to."

She went down the steps to the car without looking back. Meera stood on the steps of the SP's office and watched it go down the hill, and did not say anything, because there was nothing in her that she could have said that would have been true.

The loose threads tied themselves off one at a time, the way they do in real cases, untidily and without ceremony.

Gaurav Thapliyal's wife rang Kabir from Pauri in the second week of October and said that her husband had seen the news, and wanted it known why he had sent the message about the electrician. He had smoked on the terrace at night, all eight weeks, against the rules. Many nights he had seen Dev come up the outside iron stair from the generator and go in through a door that was not supposed to exist. In September 2019 he had told the policeman so, in his statement. But the statement had been typed in the office from Madam's notes, and he had been tired and frightened and on his way home, and he had signed it without reading it. Seven years later, when a journalist he would not speak to rang again, he had remembered the door.

Sister Annamma Varghese's notebook went to the SP in Uttarkashi by registered post from Kottayam, in a biscuit tin wrapped in brown paper, with a note in her son's careful English saying that his mother was ready to come and give evidence in any court, whenever she was called, and that she had not been so well in years.

Neelam Bisht began a letter to Ananya's mother, in Hindi, and wrote eleven drafts of it, and in the end sent all eleven, because she could not decide which was the right one.

Farhan Ali posted, on the bakery's Instagram page, a photograph of a single loaf of bread with a sparrow drawn on its crust in flour, and no caption.

Ritika Sharma had the photograph of the seven of them framed at a shop in Toronto, and sent Meera a picture of it hanging in her kitchen, above the stove, where she would see it every morning.

Kabir asked her in the third week of October.

They were sitting on the steps of the SP's office again, as it happened, though by accident; he had come up to Uttarkashi to collect his letters, which had been copied for the record and returned, and she had come to sign the typed version of her statement.

"The SP says I can publish," he said. "After the charge sheet. Not before." He was holding the envelope of letters on his knees. "I won't, if you don't want me to. Your name, I mean. Your nights. The door. I can leave you out of it. I've had seven years of writing around a hole in the middle of this story. I can do it again."

"No," said Meera.

He looked at her.

"Put me in," she said. "All of it. Ten milligrams. The red door. The mud to my knees. That I closed it. That I said what I said at six in the morning. That I was sent home and told I slept and believed it for seven years, because the woman who told me was the person I trusted most after my father, and because I was a clinical psychologist who knew everything there is to know about how memory fails and it still never once occurred to me to check." She was quiet for a moment. "She asked you to ask me what I remembered. Not them. Me. So write what I remember. And write how I know it, every piece, every source. Write it the right way."

Kabir looked down at the letters on his knees for a long time.

"She'd have written it better than me," he said.

"Yes," said Meera. "She would. She'd have made it funny in the middle and you'd have hated her for it."

He laughed, a short startled sound, the first she had heard from him since Dunda. Then he put the heel of his hand to his eyes and sat there on the steps with the letters on his knees, and she sat beside him and did not say anything, and after a while the mountain light moved off the steps and the evening came up the valley.

She went to Secunderabad at the end of October, with Kabir, on the overnight train from Delhi, because Ananya's mother had written to the SP's office to ask whether the woman in Room 7 would come.

The railway quarters behind Bolarum station were exactly as Ananya had described them on the tape: two long rows of yellow-washed buildings with green doors, a neem tree at the end of the lane, a handpump, washing on the lines, children on bicycles. Number 14 was on the ground floor. The door was open.

On the train back to Delhi, very late, with Kabir asleep on the upper berth and the dark country going past the window, Meera took out her notebook for the last time on this case and wrote down the thing she had been asked most often that month, by the SP, by the inquiry officer from the Medical Council, by her own mother in the study in Bani Park with the four-minutes-fast clock: why she had not remembered.

She wrote it plainly, for herself.

*I was on 10 mg of zolpidem, which I did not know I was taking. Zolpidem can cause complex behaviour during sleep, including walking, with little or no memory of it afterwards. That accounts for most of the gap.*

*I was partly roused on the path and at the door, by cold, by rain, by a voice, by the generator and the lights. Partial arousal can leave fragments: images and sensations without a story. That accounts for the five things I kept.*

*Within hours, someone I trusted told me that I had slept, and gave me a single kind sentence about Ananya to hold instead. I believed her, and I repeated it, and so did six other people. That is suggestion. It is ordinary. I have taught it.*

*Afterwards I filed the fragments in the only drawer I had: my father. A door I didn't open in time; a clock; a knock I didn't hear. They fitted, and because they fitted I stopped asking. That is misattribution. It is ordinary too.*

*And I didn't want to know. I can't prove that one. I'm putting it in the right-hand column anyway.*

*None of this erased anything. Nothing erased anything. The memory was not taken from me. It was never fully made, and the little that was made was given a different name by someone I loved. The truth was not in my head. It was in a log in a generator room, and a nurse's notebook under a wedding sari, and a pharmacy register nobody could alter, and a slipper in a plastic bag, and a tape that nobody listened to the end of. It was in the photograph from the very first night: everyone looking at the camera, and Ananya looking at me.*

She closed the notebook.

The glass came in November, by courier, in a wooden crate packed with straw.

She unpacked it on her dining table in the flat in Shyam Nagar, in the evening, with the lamp on and the neem tree outside the window, where seven weeks before she had cut the tape on a parcel with kitchen scissors and lifted out a recorder painted *HIMR 3*. The panel was heavier than she expected: a rectangle of thick glass with a fine wire mesh in it, in a narrow steel frame with four screw holes. The old sticker was still on one side, EMERGENCY EXIT — PUSH BAR TO OPEN, its red letters faded to pink.

She stood it on the table against the wall, and looked at it from the side with the sticker, where the words read properly, and then she walked round the table and looked at it from the other side.

Back to front. Letters she could see the shape of and could not read.

She left it there.

---

That night she did what she had done every night for seven years.

She went round the flat and switched off the lights. She drank a glass of water standing at the kitchen counter. She went into the bedroom and closed the door behind her, and took the key from the lock, and turned it from the inside, and heard the small, solid sound the lock made when it closed.

She had always thought it was the sound of a woman who lived alone in a city, making herself safe. And it was. It had simply never been her idea.

Her mother had turned this key from the outside for three months, because a doctor on the phone had told her to. Then one night Meera had turned it herself, from the inside, without knowing why, and had gone on turning it, every night, for seven years: a habit her body had kept long after her mind had been told there was nothing to keep it for. In a cheap blue notebook in Kottayam, in a nurse's round hand, under a date, were the words she had said aloud at ten past six on the morning after, with gravel in her feet and deodar needles in her hair. *Gauraiya bol rahi thi andar jao. Darwaza band karna hai.*

She would never know whether she remembered that voice on the cliff, or whether she had rebuilt it, the way she had rebuilt a torch going the wrong way along a path, out of other people's words and her own wanting. She knew too much about memory to pretend otherwise. But she knew it had been said. It was written down, by someone who had been there, in the morning, before anyone told her what to remember.

On the dresser, under the lamp, lay a page torn from a cheap ruled notebook, in Dev Malik's slow, careful Hindi.

*Tum us raat wahan thi. Tumne hi darwaza band kiya tha.*

She had been there. She had closed the door.

She put the key in the saucer on the top shelf of the wardrobe, where she could not reach it in the dark, and turned off the lamp, and lay down. Through the wall there was nothing. There had been nothing through that wall for seven years. Outside, a dog barked once in the lane and stopped.

"Band kar diya," said Meera, to the dark. I've closed it.

Then she slept.
