Dunda was a single street of shops along the highway, a bus stand, a government inter college painted ochre and green, and an army of rhesus monkeys sitting on the electricity wires. A narrow road climbed away from it to the north, broken tarmac giving way to rubble and then to two stony ruts with grass between them. Bhuvan took it in first gear, with his lips moving.

Four kilometres. Pine first, then deodar, tall and dark and dripping. A shrine to the local devta at a bend in the road, with bells hung from every branch of the tree beside it. A last turn.

And there it was.

She had remembered it as larger. Memory does that; it enlarges the places where something happened to us. It was a long, low building of grey stone with a slate roof, built along the crest of the ridge like a wall. A row of tall windows faced the road, many of them now boarded. The man who came out of the guard hut was in his early forties, broad and short and solid, with a thick moustache, a grey wool cap and a maroon sweater that had been darned at both elbows. He came down to the gate with the stiff, important walk of a man who has been told to keep people out, and opened his mouth to tell them so. Then he saw Meera's face through the bars.

He stopped.

It was not the ordinary surprise of a watchman at an unexpected visitor. Meera knew the difference; she spent her working life watching faces at the moment something reached them. All the colour went out of his face at once and came back too fast, a dark flush up his neck. His hand on the gate bolt closed and did not move. For a second he looked, absurdly, like a man who has seen someone come back from the dead.

It was smaller than in the photograph. All the furniture had gone except a single broken sofa with its stuffing showing, pushed against the long wall. The tall south windows were uncurtained, and through them the valley was full of moving cloud, so that the room seemed to float. On the far wall, where the banner had hung, there were four rusted nails in a row. And beside them, still hanging slightly crooked, was the clock.

It was the same clock. Plain white face, black hands, a thin black rim. It had stopped at ten past four on some afternoon nobody had noticed. Across its glass, running diagonally from the edge of the rim to the centre, was a long hairline crack, and the crack passed straight through the four.

Meera stood in front of it for a long time.

She had a clock in her list. *2. A wall clock.* For seven years she had said to herself, and to her therapist, and once to a psychiatry resident who had asked a kind question at the wrong moment: my father's clock. The one I looked at when I found him. Four minutes fast.

She did not know whether this was the clock in her list. That was the honest answer, and she made herself stay with it. There are only so many ways to make a clock. White faces and black hands are in every school and railway station and office in India. The mind does not file memories like a librarian; it files them like a child, by feeling, under whatever drawer happens to be open. She knew that.

But she also knew that her heart was going far too fast for a woman looking at an ordinary clock.

She took a photograph of it. Then, because she was who she was, she took out the print from its envelope and held it up beside the clock on the wall. The same clock, at eleven thirty-two, above seven lantern-lit faces. In the photograph the crack was there too: a fine pale line across the glass, catching the flash, running through the four.

So the photograph had been taken in this room, under this clock, with the crack already in it. That did not tell her when. It only told her where.

The east corridor was forty metres long and almost completely dark. The windows that should have lit it were on the north side, behind the therapy rooms, and the doors to the therapy rooms were shut. Her torch beam went down it like a hand down a sleeve.

Therapy A on her left. Then a narrower door with a brass plate that said OBSERVATION, and beside it, set into the wall, a long dark rectangle of glass that she remembered: the one-way mirror. From inside the therapy room, you saw yourself. From in here, you saw them. Then Therapy B, where they had recorded the voice letters. Then a records room, a linen store.

On her right, the residents' rooms. Small varnished doors, each with a number painted on it in white. 1. 2. 3. The paint was flaking.

She walked slowly. She was aware of trying to do something she taught her clients not to do, which was to make a memory come by wanting it. It would not come that way; it never did. Memory answers to cues, to smell and light and the angle of a floor, not to effort. So she stopped trying and simply walked, and let the corridor do what it would.

It did nothing. It was a corridor. Mouse droppings along the skirting. A calendar from a Dunda hardware shop on a nail, still showing September 2019.

The door of the linen store on the left hung open. Inside, cardboard cartons had been stacked to the ceiling and then abandoned. Wind came in, and rain, cold and sudden, straight into her face. And the noise of the river, no longer far below and filtered through stone, but enormous and close and everywhere.

A railing ran round the landing at waist height, red-brown with rust. To her right an iron staircase went down steeply, close against the wall, towards the terrace. To her left, eastwards, a narrow paved path began at the edge of the landing and ran along the face of the mountain, perhaps a metre wide, with a low parapet and a broken railing on its outer side, and beyond the railing nothing at all. They stood in the corridor, the three of them, with the door swinging and banging on its hinges in the wind until Dev reached past her and pulled it shut. It closed with the same double clank. He did not lock it. There was nothing to lock it with; she could see the empty steel plate above the frame where something electrical had once been fixed, with two cut wires hanging from it.

"There was a magnet," Dev said, following her eyes. "A magnetic lock. With current, it holds. No current, it opens." He wiped the rain off his face with the back of his hand. "Fire rule. In a fire, the current goes, the doors open, people get out."

"And when the current came back?"

"Then it held again." He looked at her. "From inside you could always push the bar. Always. From outside—" he shrugged, the big shoulders lifting under the darned sweater. "From outside there is no handle. From outside, if the current is on, you cannot open it."

Kabir had said nothing at all. He was standing with his back against the cream wall opposite Room 7, looking at the door, and his face in the torchlight was the colour of paper.

Somewhere below them, in the bowels of the building, there was a sound.
