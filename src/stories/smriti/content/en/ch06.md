"Madam told me to burn them," said Dev Malik. "In 2020, when they closed. She said, *Dev, there's nothing in there anyone needs. Make a fire on the terrace and finish it.*"

He was standing at the bottom of the service ramp on the south side of the building, with a ring of keys in one hand and the rain running off the peak of his wool cap. Behind him the lower terrace stretched along the foot of the main building: a long stone platform, mossy and puddled, hemmed in on its outer edge by a low wall and then by nothing but cloud. A row of doors along the back of it led into the hillside, into the lower floor of the old sanatorium that you could not see from the road. At the far east end, under the iron landing they had stood on the day before, squatted the generator room with its tin roof, humming.

"And you didn't," said Meera.

"No." He chose a key. "I don't know why. I told her I did."

He unlocked a door that had once been painted white. Above it, carved into the stone lintel and almost worn away, a single English word: MORTUARY.

"From the old days," Dev said, seeing her read it. "TB hospital. When someone died up there," he tipped his head at the building above, "they brought them down the outside stair at night, so the other patients wouldn't see. Then the jeep took them from here to the road." He pushed the door open. "The institute used it for the files. It's dry. Nobody likes to come in."

---

It was a long, low, vaulted room, colder than outside. Steel almirahs lined both walls, grey government issue, a dozen of them, some with their doors hanging open. On a stone slab in the middle of the floor that Meera did not let herself think about, someone had left a stack of empty cardboard file boxes and a dead pigeon.

Dev switched on a work lamp clamped to the door frame, running from a cable that disappeared towards the humming generator, and the room jumped into flat yellow light.

"Everything from 2016 to 2020," he said. "Accounts. Food. Staff attendance. Maintenance, mine. Medical, the nurses'. Madam's own files she took. The rest is here." He looked at her, and then at Kabir, and then back at her. "The company will throw it in the river on Monday with the plaster. Nobody will read it. So read it."

Kabir did not move. He was looking at the almirahs the way, Meera thought, a man might look at a locked door he had been knocking on for seven years and found, one afternoon, simply standing open.

"Thank you," he said to Dev. His voice was not quite steady.

Dev did not answer him. He said to Meera, "One hour. Before dark. The light on this side goes early," and went back out into the rain, and they heard his boots go away along the terrace towards the generator.

---

They found Cohort Four in the third almirah on the left.

It took a while. The institute had kept its records the way most Indian institutions do: carefully, in registers, by category, and then had stopped caring halfway through and thrown everything into the nearest box. There were bills for gas cylinders and requisitions for toilet paper. There were staff leave applications, a stack of *Smriti Patrika* newsletters, a box of blank voice-letter labels. And on the third shelf, held together with jute string, a row of tall cloth-bound registers with typed paper labels on their spines.

NIGHT DUTY REGISTER — 2019 (II).
PHARMACY — DISPENSING — 2019.
MEDICAL ROOM — 2019.
MAINTENANCE LOG — DG / ELECTRICAL — 2019.
GATE — VISITORS & MOVEMENT — 2019.
ACTIVITIES — P4.

Kabir took them down one at a time, as if they might break.

They worked at the stone slab, because it was the only flat surface, and Meera made herself not mind. She had brought the RTI report and a pad of paper, and she drew a line down the middle of a page and wrote *Official* on one side and *Records* on the other. Then, under the yellow work lamp in the old mortuary of St Luke's Sanatorium, with the rain on the terrace outside and the generator thudding under her feet, she began to build the night of the fourteenth of September.

---

The night duty register first.

It was a wide ledger ruled into columns: *Date. Time. Event. Residents. Action. Sign.* The handwriting in it was the same all through Cohort Four's eight weeks: a nurse's quick, round, upright hand in blue ballpoint, with the Malayali curl to its r's that Meera now knew she would recognise anywhere. *A.V.* in the sign column, night after night.

The page for the fourteenth was neat.

*14.9.19. 21:30 — Night medication given (see Pharmacy). 22:00 — Lights out. All residents (6+G) in rooms. 23:05 — Power fluctuation (rain). Generator took load. Residents assembled common room as per SOP. 23:15 — All residents to rooms. Brief. 15.9.19. 06:00 — P4-05 not in room. Raincoat, shoes, torch missing. 06:20 — Director informed by phone.*

It matched the report almost word for word. *Brief.* *Generator took load.* *23:15.*

"*6+G*," said Kabir quietly, with his finger beside it.

"Six and the guest." Meera wrote it down. "Me."

She turned back through the register, page by page, to see what an ordinary night looked like. Most were nothing. *21:30 meds. 22:00 lights out. 02:00 round — all asleep.* Now and then a line in the *Event* column: *P4-03 nightmare, sat with her 20 min.* *P4-04 smoking on terrace, counselled.* *P4-02 found in kitchen 02:10, eating; returned to bed; no recall.* That was Farhan. *Events*, he had said, laughing without humour. *That's what I was.*

Then, between the pages for the twenty-eighth of August and the twelfth of September, she noticed something with her fingers before she saw it with her eyes. The register was thinner there. She bent the binding back under the lamp. Close to the stitching, three pages had been cut out, neatly, with a blade, leaving only narrow stubs of paper a few millimetres wide. On one of the stubs, a fragment of blue ink survived: the tail of a letter, and below it what might have been a *7*.

She showed Kabir. He looked at the stubs for a long time.

"Three nights," he said. "Somebody wanted three nights gone."

Meera wrote on her pad, in the *Records* column: *Night register: 3 pages cut out, between 28.8 and 12.9.* Then, after a moment: *Stub — "7"?* And she moved on, because it was not the fourteenth, and the fourteenth was what she had come for.

---

The pharmacy register was a different kind of book altogether, and Kabir's hands, when he opened it, were shaking.

"I've asked for this," he said. "RTI to the Health Department. RTI to the Mental Health Authority. A court application. Three letters to Ira Sen's lawyer." He turned the first page. "Seven years."

It was ruled into columns too: *Date. Time. Room. Drug. Dose. Given by. Remarks.* The daytime pages were what Meera expected: *Propranolol 40 mg* against each room number an hour before each memory session, signed and dated, exactly as an approved trial should be. Paracetamol. Antacids. Cough syrup in August when everyone caught the same cold.

The night pages were different.

From the twelfth of August, three room numbers appeared every night at 21:30, and only three. *R2. R6. R7.*

*R2 — Zolpidem — 5 mg.*
*R6 — Zolpidem — 5 mg.*
*R7 — Zolpidem — 5 mg.*

Against R7 on the twelfth of August, in the *Remarks* column, in a different hand from the nurse's, a sloping, educated black ballpoint: *SCA — per Dir.*

Meera stopped breathing for a second, and then made herself breathe.

Zolpidem. She knew it the way any clinician knows it: a hypnotic, the most widely prescribed sleeping pill in the world, the white tablet that half of urban India's middle-aged insomniacs kept in their bathroom cabinets. She knew its literature, too, because sleep and memory were her field. She knew that in the hours after a dose, people sometimes did things they would not remember. They ate, made phone calls, cooked, walked about the house. Very occasionally they drove. She knew that regulators in several countries had cut the recommended dose for women in half, because women cleared the drug more slowly and woke still impaired. She knew that in the spring of 2019, months before this page was written, the American regulator had put its strongest warning on the label because of exactly these behaviours.

She turned the pages. On the twenty-sixth of August, against R2 and R7, the dose changed. *10 mg.* The same hand in the *Remarks* column: *↑ per Dir. tel. order.* R6 stayed at five.

R7. Ten milligrams. Every night from the twenty-sixth of August to the fourteenth of September.

"SCA," said Kabir, reading over her shoulder. "What's SCA?"

"I don't know." That was true. "Something consolidation, probably. Sleep consolidation." She heard her own voice, flat and professional, the voice she used in case conferences. "There's research on it. Zolpidem increases sleep spindles. Some studies suggest it might strengthen how memories are stored overnight. If you'd just loosened a traumatic memory with propranolol in the afternoon, you might want to strengthen how the softer version was stored that night." She stopped. "It's not in the trial. It's not in anything I've seen. Nobody asked me."

"Nobody asked anyone," said Kabir.

She kept turning. Every night, R7, ten milligrams, *A.V.* in the *Given by* column. Every night R6, five milligrams, *given*. Every night R2.

"She wasn't taking hers," said Kabir. His finger was beside R6, on a page at the end of August. "Not after the thirtieth. She was putting it under her tongue."

Meera looked up.

He was not looking at her. He was looking at the register. And his face, for a second, before he heard what he had said, was the face of a man looking at a photograph of someone he loved.

"How do you know that?" she said. "The thirtieth?"

Kabir's eyes came up. She watched him hear it.

"Farhan," he said, after a beat. "Farhan said she told him to spit them out. So she must have been spitting hers out too. I'm guessing. Sorry. I'm guessing at the date."

It was a perfectly reasonable answer. Meera wrote nothing down. But she put it away, the way she had put away the marigolds on the gate and his footsteps going straight down the dark corridor to the end, in the place where she kept the things she would think about later.

She turned to the fourteenth.

*14.9.19 — 21:30 — R2 Zolpidem 10 mg. A.V. — R6 Zolpidem 5 mg. A.V. — R7 Zolpidem 10 mg. A.V.*

Then the next line, the next morning.

*15.9.19 — 06:15 — R7 — Povidone-iodine sol., gauze ×4, crepe bandage ×2 — A.V.*
*15.9.19 — 06:15 — R7 — Inj. Tetanus toxoid 0.5 ml IM — A.V.*

Meera read it three times.

Room 7. Six fifteen in the morning. Antiseptic, four gauze dressings, two bandages. A tetanus injection, the kind you give for a wound that might have dirt or rust in it.

She put her pen down on the stone slab.

---

"There'll be an entry in the medical register," she said, a little too evenly. "There has to be. You don't give tetanus toxoid without a note. Who, what, where, how it happened."

Kabir was already opening it.

The medical room register was smaller, a doctor's book: *Date. Time. Name/Room. Complaint. Findings. Treatment. Sign.* The visiting psychiatrist's signature appeared once a week in it; the rest was the nurses'.

Kabir found the fourteenth. He turned the book so Meera could read it.

*14.9.19 — 16:00 — P4-01 (Neelam) — Slipped on wet steps near kitchen. R wrist painful, swollen. No deformity. Crepe bandage applied, cold compress. Analgesic. — A.V.*

Neelam's wrist. The white thing in the photograph. *I'd hurt my wrist that day. In the afternoon, in the rain.*

Meera put her finger on the line and held it there for a moment. Then she wrote it down, carefully, with the time.

Then the fifteenth.

*15.9.19 — 07:30 — P4-03 (Tenzin) — Headache. Paracetamol 500 mg. — A.V.*
*15.9.19 — 08:10 — P4-06 (Ritika) — Anxiety, tearful. Reassured. — A.V.*
*15.9.19 — 19:40 — P4-02 (Farhan) — Insomnia, distress. Seen by Director. — I.S.*

That was all.

Nothing at 06:10. Nothing at 06:15. Nothing for R7, or for *G*, or for anyone with a wound that needed four dressings and a tetanus shot. The page was clean and unbroken. There was no gap where an entry might have been, no erasure, no crossing out. It was simply not there.

"The pharmacy gave out dressings and a tetanus injection for Room 7 at six-fifteen in the morning," said Meera. Her voice sounded very far away. "And the medical register doesn't know it happened."

Kabir said nothing.

"Somebody was hurt in Room 7 that night," she said. "Badly enough for a TT shot. And the only record of it is in the pharmacy book, because the pharmacy has to account for every ampoule it gives out." She looked at the page. "Whoever cleaned up the medical register forgot the pharmacy."

"Or couldn't touch it," said Kabir quietly. "Stock registers get audited."

Meera wrote, in the *Records* column, in capitals, as though the capitals might hold it still: *15.9 06:15 R7 — DRESSINGS + TT. NO MEDICAL ENTRY.* And under *Official*, opposite it, she wrote: *Ira: "You slept. You were asleep by eleven."*

The maintenance log was the last book, and the only one in a hand she had not seen yet.

It was a cheap hard-bound account book with a red cloth spine, the kind sold by the kilo in Dunda bazaar, and on its cover someone had written in marker pen, half in English and half in Hindi, *DG / BIJLI — 2019 — D. MALIK*.

The log was all short lines, in a slow, careful hand that switched between English and Hindi as the words came to it. *Diesel 40 L received. Starter battery charged. Geyser R3 element changed. Water pump R side seized, repaired.* And on the second of September, in the same hand, a longer entry that made her stop:

*2.9.19 — Cure-walk (E), 35–45 m from landing: retaining wall bulging, water seepage. Unsafe. Path closed — barricade tape fixed. Madam informed.*

*Dev the electrician put tape across the cliff walk because the wall is leaning*, she had written in her own diary that day. *G. is afraid of heights.*

And on the twelfth: *E corridor emergency lamp (near E door) — battery dead. Indent raised.*

*E door.* She looked at the two words. On the architect's plan in the hall, the east end of the corridor was a sealed store with no door. In the maintenance log, written by the man who maintained it, it was simply the E door, as if there had never been any question.

Then she turned to the fourteenth.

*14.9.19*
*23:05 — Mains fail (storm). AMF auto-start: 3 attempts — FAIL. Lockout.*
*23:06 — To DG room via E door.*
*23:20 — Phoned Madam (mob). Battery weak. Bleeding fuel line.*

The next line had been scratched out. Not erased; scratched, with a ballpoint pressed so hard that it had nearly gone through the paper, back and forth, back and forth, the way you scribble out a word you wish you had never written. Under the yellow lamp it was a black ridge of ink.

*23:47 — Manual start — 3 cranks — OK.*
*23:48 — On load.*
*01:10 — Mains restored. Changeover OK.*

Meera held the book up to the work lamp and tilted it, the way she had held the RTI page the night before in Dehradun. This time something came through. Where the pen had dug into the paper, the original words had been pressed into it, and with the light raking across the page from the side, she could see the shapes of them under the scribble, the way you can read the ghost of a letter on the page beneath the one it was written on.

A time, first. A tilde, a two, a three, a colon. *~23:4–*. The last digit was gone.

Then a word that might have been *loud*. Then *crash*. Then, quite clearly, two letters at the end of the line, *E side*, and a question mark.

She put the book down very carefully on the stone slab.

The light had gone. When she looked up from the log, the narrow windows high in the mortuary wall were grey, and the yellow lamp seemed brighter, and the generator under their feet had a steadier, deeper note, as if it knew the evening had come and it was needed.

She looked at her pad. Two columns, *Official* on the left and *Records* on the right, and between them a story that had come apart in her hands like wet paper.

*Official: Power fluctuation, brief. Generator took load. Residents dispersed 23:15.*
*Records: Mains failed 23:05. Generator FAILED to start, three attempts, lockout. No power until manual start at 23:47. Load 23:48. In the dark: 43 minutes.*

*Official (Neelam): "Short. Ten minutes. In bed by quarter past eleven."*
*Official (Annamma): "It was short."*
*Records (Tenzin): "Lights came on by themselves. 11:48. My watch."*

Tenzin's steel watch. Tenzin, who did not say things she was not sure of. In the canteen at noon, Meera had believed her and had not been able to prove it. Now a man who had never met Tenzin, writing in a cheap account book on a stormy night seven years ago, had proved it for her to the minute. *23:48 — On load.* The lights in Room 3 had come back on at 23:48 because the generator had taken load at 23:48. Not ten minutes. Not brief. Forty-three minutes of darkness, in a building whose east door opened, whenever the power failed, onto an iron landing above a gorge.
