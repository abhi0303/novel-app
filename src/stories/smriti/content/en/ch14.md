The unredacted report came at ten past three, as an attachment to an email from the Member Secretary of the State Mental Health Authority, with an apology for the delay and a note that the original had been retrieved from a sealed cabinet in the Authority's record room in Dehradun.

The SP printed page seven and laid it on her desk without a word, and Meera read it standing up.

*…The east end of the residential corridor is a sealed store with no external access. A search of the premises and grounds was conducted.*

*One non-enrolled resident (guest of the Director, Room 7) was sent home on medical grounds on the morning of 15.09.2019, before the arrival of police.*

*The Director informed the police at Dunda at 18:30 on 15.09.2019…*

So that was the black bar. Eighteen words. *On medical grounds.* Meera read it twice and found that it made her want to laugh, a short, dry, unhappy laugh. Ira had told the truth, in the one place she must have known almost no one would ever read it. In a report to her own regulator, she had written down that the guest in Room 7 had been sent away before the police came. And then a clerk at the regulator, protecting the guest's privacy exactly as the law required, had blacked it out before anyone outside could see it. Every part of it had been done correctly. That was what she would remember.

"Medical grounds," said the SP.

"Bandaged feet," said Meera. "And a tetanus shot."

---

Ira Sen arrived at four exactly, in a white Innova with Dehradun plates, with a lawyer.

He was a large, soft-spoken man in a grey suit who introduced himself as Mr Mehra and said, before anyone else had said anything, that his client was attending voluntarily, that she was fifty-eight years of age and had a heart condition, and that she reserved every right she possessed. Ira said nothing at all. She came into the SP's office in a dark handloom sari and a grey shawl, with her silver hair perfectly in place, and sat down in the chair in front of the desk, and then she turned her head and looked at Meera, who was sitting by the window.

"She stays," Ira said to the SP. "If you want me to say anything, she stays. I'll say it once, and I'll say it to her."

Mr Mehra murmured something. Ira lifted one hand very slightly from her lap, and he stopped.

The SP looked at Ira for a long moment. Then she nodded to her reader, who opened a laptop, and to a woman sub-inspector by the door, who took out a notebook.

"Go on, Doctor," she said.

---

Ira did not tell it the way Dev had told it, out of order, falling back on itself. She told it the way she must once have presented at conferences in London: in sections, with headings, as if she were walking a room of colleagues through a study that had gone wrong. And she did not look at the SP once. She looked at Meera.

"The door first," she said. "Since you've seen it."

It had been her decision. The contractor had quoted a price for taking down the old stair that would have cost her a year's budget for staff, and Dev had needed a fast way to the generator. The approving authority in Uttarkashi would never have passed an exit from a residential corridor onto an iron landing above a cliff, so the plan had said *store*, and the stair *to be dismantled*, and she had told herself that a door only Dev used, with a magnetic lock that only Dev could release, was not a lie. "The fire rule meant it had to open when the power failed. I knew that. I chose not to think about what it meant on a residential corridor in the monsoon." Her voice did not change. "That is the first thing."

"The power. When Dev rang me at twenty past eleven I told him to keep trying. When I learned the next morning that the generator had failed for forty minutes, with that door open and a cliff path that I had been told on the second of September was unsafe and had not had repaired, I understood exactly what a court would make of it." She paused. "So the generator *took load*. So it was *a brief fluctuation*. I wrote those words for the police myself, on the Sunday night, in my office. Sister Annamma copied them into the night register."

"The night register," said Meera. "The page for the fourteenth."

"Is not the page she wrote that night. She wrote the true one. Lantern, the common room, the time she sent you all back down the corridor. And no headcount afterwards, because at twenty to twelve I telephoned the duty room to find out what was happening, and I kept her on the line for fourteen minutes asking about the generator and the residents and whether she had enough kerosene. So there was no round. Nobody looked into any of the rooms between twenty to twelve and six in the morning." Ira's hands lay perfectly still in her lap. "I took that page out on the Sunday night and she wrote a new one at my desk, copying from my draft. *All to rooms 23:15. Brief.* It's very neat. You'll have noticed."

Meera had noticed, in the mortuary, without letting herself know it. The page for the fourteenth had been the cleanest page in the book.

"The three pages before it," said Meera. "Cut out between the end of August and the twelfth of September."

The smallest pause.

"They recorded adverse events in the sleep consolidation arm," said Ira. "Events at night in rooms 2 and 7. They would have shown that I had been told, more than once, that the medication was causing complex behaviour at night, and that I had increased the dose anyway." She did not look away. "I removed them."

"What events?"

"Farhan's, you know about. He ate flour."

"And Room 7?"

"Others," said Ira Sen.

Mr Mehra stirred. The SP's eyes went from Ira to Meera and back. Meera waited. Ira did not say anything else.

---

"The medical register," said Ira. "Room 7, on the morning of the fifteenth. Sister wrote it at six-ten, as she should. I had her rewrite that page too, on the Sunday night, without it. You were not on any register, Meera. An unregistered guest with injured feet the morning after a resident disappeared, on a drug she had not consented to: that would have brought every question there was. Who you were. Why you were there. What you had been given and by whom." A small movement of her shoulders. "I forgot the pharmacy. Or I didn't forget it. I could not touch it. The drug inspector audits the stock register; every ampoule of tetanus toxoid has to be accounted for. I thought nobody would ever put the two books side by side." She looked at Meera with something that might, in another woman, have been pride. "You did it in a mortuary in an afternoon."

"And you sent me home."

"I sent you home." For the first time, something came into Ira's voice: not apology, something harder and older. "At six-twenty in the morning Sister rang me in Dehradun and told me Ananya was gone, and that you were in your bed soaked to the skin with your feet cut to pieces and no memory of anything. At six-forty Dev rang me and told me about the path. And about you at the door." She held Meera's eyes. "He was quite certain. He had seen you shut that door, with your hand on the glass, at a quarter to midnight. I sat on the edge of my sister's bed in my nightdress and I understood that by Monday evening, if the police heard what Dev had seen, there would be a drugged girl in a police lock-up in Uttarkashi for the disappearance of a young woman whose door she had closed. Ashok's daughter. Five months after I had stood at his cremation." She drew a breath. "So I told Sister to put you in the car with Rawat-ji before anyone else was awake. I met you at the station. And I told you that you had slept."

"You told me she'd flown off into her own sky."

"Yes."

"Gauraiya apne aasmaan mein ud gayi."

"Yes." Ira's mouth moved. "It was the kindest sentence I could think of. I said it to you at the station, and to them that evening in the common room, and to the journalists who rang in October. Joshi-ji typed their statements from my notes, so it went into those as well. I heard Neelam say it on a television programme in 2021, word for word, and I turned the television off." She was silent a moment. "I'm a memory researcher, Meera. I know exactly what happens when you give a frightened group of people one beautiful sentence about something none of them saw. I've published on it."

Kabir, by the door, had stopped writing.

---

"The photograph," said Meera.

"I took the memory card out of the institute camera on the Sunday evening. I had the whole card wiped except one frame, which I copied first. On the twentieth, in Dehradun, I had that one frame printed at the shop by the clock tower where I'd had every institute photograph printed for four years." Ira looked at the print lying on the SP's desk. "I wrote *farewell* on the back, and the date of the farewell, so that if anyone ever found it in my things they would see seven people saying goodbye. Not lanterns. Not the clock."

"Why keep it at all?"

"Because it was the last photograph of her," said Ira simply. "I couldn't."

"And the tape."

"Sister found the recorder on Ananya's bed at six in the morning, with the tape in it, and kept it for me. I listened to it on the Sunday night in my office, after the police had gone." Her voice was very steady. "I heard her say your name, and *you still haven't been told the truth*, and *I'm Ananya, your sister*. And I switched it off. I didn't listen to any more of it. I have not listened to any more of it since." She paused. "I labelled it the way we labelled every voice letter, from the session register: the seventh. I put it in an envelope with the photograph. In March 2020, when we closed, I took my files home. I left the envelope in the cabinet in my office."

"Why?"

Ira looked at her for a long time.

"I couldn't destroy her voice," she said. "And I couldn't live in a house with it." Her hands moved once in her lap and were still again. "So I left her where she was."

---

The SP's reader had stopped typing. The room was very quiet. Through the window, the afternoon light had gone gold on the far side of the valley, and somewhere below in the town a temple bell was ringing for the evening aarti.

"Dr Sen," said the SP. "The slipper."

Ira did not move.

"Mr Malik found one institute slipper on the broken edge of the path on the morning of the fifteenth. He gave it to the night nurse, who gave it to you. Where is it?"

"I have it."

"Where?"

"In my house in Dehradun." A pause. "In a file. With the pages."

"Then it comes to this office tomorrow," said the SP, "with the file and the pages. In a sealed envelope. Or I'll send someone for it."

"It will come," said Ira.

Meera was watching her face. She had watched faces all her working life, at the exact moment something reached them, and she saw it now, very small and very brief: when the SP said *slipper*, something in Ira Sen's composed face had closed, the way it had closed on her doorstep on Thursday at the sight of Kabir in the rain.

"Ira-di," said Meera.

Ira turned to her.

"Did I shut her out?" Meera's voice was perfectly level. "On the cliff. Did I close that door on her, while she was still out there?"

Ira Sen looked at her for a long, long time. Her eyes behind the rimless glasses were wet and her voice, when it came, did not show it at all.

"You were asleep, Meera," she said.

Then she turned to the SP and said, "I've said what I came to say," and folded her hands, and did not say anything more, however the SP asked, for the remaining twenty minutes of the interview.

---

Dev was waiting in the corridor outside, on a wooden bench under a poster about cybercrime, with his wool cap in his hands and his sealed envelope of maintenance log on his knees. When Meera came out he stood up.

He did not wait to be asked this time.

"I sent it, didi," he said.

Meera stopped.

"The box. The recorder. The tape. The photo." He looked at the floor and then made himself look at her. "And the paper. *Tum us raat wahan thi. Tumne hi darwaza band kiya tha.* I wrote it."

Behind her she heard Kabir come out of the SP's office and stop.

"In August the company told me to clear the building," said Dev. "Everything. Madam's office also. Madam had given back all the keys last year except one, the steel cabinet, so I broke it with a screwdriver. Inside there was nothing. Some old files. And one big envelope, in Madam's writing. *Cohort 4 — 14/15.9 — G. / M. — personal.*" He swallowed. "I opened it. The photo. And the recorder with the tape in it. I put new batteries. I pressed play, I heard *Meera*, and I stopped it, like Madam must have." He looked at her. "And I looked at the photo and there you were. Next to her. With your eyes half closed. The same face I see every night behind that glass."

"How did you find me?"

"On the phone. Google. *Meera psychologist Jaipur.*" He almost smiled. "There are many. I looked at all the photos. One was you. Older. The same face."

"And the note."

"I wanted you to come." His voice cracked. "Before they knocked it down. Before the door was gone. I wanted you to stand there and remember what you did. I thought, she has had seven years in Jaipur with a nice clinic and no memory, and I have had seven years with my generator and that door and that girl's light going out on the path, and Madam's voice telling me I saw nothing." He wiped his face with his wrist. "I thought you shut her out. I've thought it every night. I wanted you to know it, too. That's why I wrote it. *Tumne hi darwaza band kiya tha.* So you would come and stand there and know."

"Why didn't you sign it?"

"Because I was afraid." He said it simply, the way he had said everything since Monday. "Of Madam. Of the company. Of the police, for what I said in 2019. Of you." He looked at the floor. "I posted it from Dehradun on the thirteenth, when the company sent me down for papers. On the fourteenth I came back and the marigolds were on my gate again." He glanced past her at Kabir, and away. "And on Friday you came to the gate, and I saw your face through the bars, and I couldn't breathe."

Meera stood in the corridor of the SP's office under the poster about cybercrime and looked at Dev Malik.

She had noticed, in the mortuary, that the Hindi in his log was written slowly and carefully, each letter finished before the next began. She had noticed it on the note on the first night, in her flat, before she knew anything: *the letters of someone who writes more numbers than words.* She had put it away, because handwriting is the thing people are most confidently wrong about, and because she had not wanted to know.

"I'm sorry, didi," said Dev. "For the note. For seven years. For not going up the stair when I heard the door."

Meera found that she could not answer him. She put her hand on his arm, briefly, as the SP had put her hand on hers that morning, and he looked at the hand as if it surprised him very much.

---

They drove back up to Dunda in the dusk, the three of them in Bhuvan's Innova, and nobody spoke. At the bend below the institute they passed the SDRF team coming down: a truck with orange ropes coiled in the back and six men in helmets and harnesses, muddy to the waist. Kabir rolled down his window and asked. The team leader leaned out of the truck and shook his head. The slope was steep, loose, full of rubble from the old collapse and seven years of monsoons since. They had roped down sixty metres below the gap and found nothing yet. They would go again at first light, further down, towards the river.

At the homestay, Bisht-ji's mother was sitting on the verandah in the dark with a shawl over her head, listening to the river.

"*Aa gaye,*" she said, as they came up the steps. You've come back. "*Kuch mila?*" Did you find anything?

"Not yet, Amma," said Kabir.

"*Milega,*" said the old woman with great certainty. It will be found. "*Nadi sab wapas karti hai. Der se. Par karti hai.*" The river gives everything back. Late. But it does.

---

Meera's phone rang at a quarter to nine, while she was sitting on her bed under the wall of papers with her statement's carbon copy on her knee.

A Kerala number. Not the one she had called on Friday.

"Hello? Meera madam?" A man's voice, young, careful English. "My name is Shibu. I am Annamma Varghese's son. In Kottayam. My mother wants to talk with you. Please wait one minute."

A rustle. A door. Then the voice she had heard on Friday night, older and softer and shaking.

"Meera mol."

"Sister."

"Mol, a police lady rang me this evening. From Uttarkashi. She asked me about the register. I told her." A breath, ragged. "I told her everything. I wrote the page again for Madam on the Sunday night, at her desk, from her paper. Both pages. Night register, medical register. She was standing behind me. I was fifty-four and I had a loan on my house in Kottayam and my son was in engineering college. I wrote what she told me to write."

"I know, Sister. It's all right."

"It's not all right." Annamma's voice rose. "It has not been all right one day for seven years. I told you on Friday I didn't remember. I remember everything. I lied to you on the phone." She was crying now. "Mol. I told you about my little book."

"Yes."

"I kept writing in it every night. As well as the register. Everything. Every round, everything I saw, my own words. When Madam took the register pages away I still had my book. I never told her. I put it in my tin trunk when I came home and I told myself I would burn it, and I didn't." A long breath. "Shibu found it today. After the police lady rang. In the trunk, under my wedding sari."

Meera did not move.

"I have written it all there," said Annamma Varghese. "Everything I saw that morning in your room at six o'clock. And before. The other nights. I wrote it for you, mol. I knew one day somebody would ask. I was waiting for somebody to ask."

"Sister," said Meera. Her voice was not steady. "Will you send it to me?"

"Shibu is taking photos now. With his phone. Every page from August. He'll send on the WhatsApp." A pause. "Read it slowly, mol. Read it sitting down."

The line went quiet. Then Shibu's voice again, apologetic: "It is many pages, madam. I am sending now. The network here is slow."

The call ended.

Meera sat on the bed in the homestay at Dunda with the phone in her hand and the wall of her own careful evidence behind her: every line with its source, every source agreeing, her own signature at the bottom of it in a police file in Uttarkashi. *At approximately 23:46 on 14 September 2019 I was standing inside the east service door of the Himgiri Institute and I closed it.*

The phone buzzed in her hand.

A photograph. A page of a small cheap notebook, ruled in blue, written close in a round, upright, curling hand in blue ballpoint, the hand she had first seen in the night register in the mortuary, with the Malayali curl to its r's. At the top, underlined twice, a date.

*15.9.19 — 06:10 — R7 (Meera mol)*

The phone buzzed again, and again, and again. Page after page arriving, slowly, over a bad network from the other end of the country, filling the screen one by one.

Meera put her feet flat on the cold cement floor, as she had in Room 7, and sat up straight, as Annamma had told her to, and began to read.
