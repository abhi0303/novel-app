The homestay at Dunda belonged to a retired schoolmaster called Bisht-ji and his mother, who was ninety and blind and sat all day in a cane chair on the verandah listening to the river. In the evenings Bisht-ji's wife cooked for whoever was staying, and that night it was rajma and red rice and a mountain spinach Meera did not know the name of, eaten at the family's own table under a calendar of Lord Shiva and a framed certificate for thirty years of service to the Government Inter College.

Meera ate without tasting anything. Kabir ate a great deal, which she had noticed was what he did when he was upset. Bisht-ji talked about the institute.

"A strange place," he said. "Good people worked there. My cousin's son was the cook for two years. But strange. All those young people from the cities, nobody allowed to go to the bazaar, nobody allowed a phone. Like a gurukul, but for the mind." He shook his head. "And then the girl. The whole of Dunda talked about it for a month. They searched the road and the bus stand. Nobody saw anything." He looked at Meera with a schoolmaster's mild curiosity. "You were there, beta?"

"I was there," said Meera.

"Then you know more than the whole of Dunda," said Bisht-ji kindly, and passed her the rice.

When they had finished, Kabir paid for both of them, over her protests. He took a worn brown leather wallet from his back pocket, and as he opened it under the bare bulb above the table Meera saw, tucked into the clear plastic window where people keep a driving licence, a small square photograph. A woman laughing, with a short black bob, a pen behind one ear.

He saw her see it. He closed the wallet, quite slowly, and put it away, and counted out the notes for Bisht-ji's wife from his shirt pocket instead.

Meera said nothing. She thanked Bisht-ji's wife for the rajma, and went upstairs, and laid out everything she had on the bed.

Her room had one bulb, one window over the dark valley, and a quilt so heavy it felt like being buried. She sat cross-legged on the quilt with the photograph in the middle of it and arranged the evidence around it in a circle, the way she arranged case material for supervision: the RTI report, her pad from the mortuary with its two columns, her phone with the pages of the registers photographed one by one, the brown diary, Ira's folded note with the phone number. And she began, as she had been trained to begin with any document a client brought into the room, by assuming nothing.

*A photograph is a fact about light,* her supervisor in Bengaluru used to say. *It tells you where the light was. Everything else, you add.*

So: where had the light been?

There were two kinds, she decided, peering at the print under the bulb with the magnifying glass she had borrowed from Bisht-ji's mother's sewing basket. The first was low and warm and came from the left and below: two sources, one on the coffee table in front of the sofa, the edge of its glass chimney just visible at the bottom of the frame, and one higher, on a shelf. Lanterns. Kerosene, from the colour, yellow-orange, the kind that every house in the hills keeps for the monsoon. It lit the faces from beneath, so that the eye sockets were dark and the cheekbones gold.

The second light was the flash: hard, white, flat, from the front. It had bleached the near faces and caught everything reflective in the room. The rain on the black window behind them, turned into silver streaks. The glass of the clock face, with its fine pale crack. The zip of Ananya's yellow raincoat. The white of the crepe bandage on Neelam's right wrist.

You do not light a room with kerosene lanterns, in a building with a generator and electric lights in every room, unless the electricity has gone.

She wrote that down. *Lit by lanterns → power out.*

Then she took her phone and found the photograph of the maintenance log. She had photographed every page from the twenty-ninth of August to the end of September, and now she read them all again, slowly, looking only for one thing: every time the power had failed.

*31.8 — 14:10 — Mains fail 4 min. AMF start OK (1st attempt).*
*9.9 — 16:20 — Mains fail 2 min. AMF OK.*
*14.9 — 23:05 — Mains fail (storm). AMF 3 attempts — FAIL. Lockout… 23:47 Manual start… 23:48 On load.*

That was all. Three outages in the last two weeks of the programme. Two of them in daylight, both under five minutes, both with the generator taking over on the first or second try, before anyone would have had time to fetch a lantern, let alone gather seven people on a sofa. And one at night, in a storm, with forty-three minutes of darkness.

On the thirteenth, the night of the farewell dinner, there was nothing in the log at all. The power had not failed.

*13.9 — no outage. 14.9 — 23:05–23:48, dark.*

---

The clock next. She held the magnifier over it until her eyes watered.

The hands were sharp in the flash: the hour hand halfway between eleven and twelve, the minute hand two small marks past the six. Eleven thirty-two. The window behind was black, and there had been no outage after dark on any night but the fourteenth. Eleven thirty-two at night.

She had photographed a line in the maintenance log that afternoon without paying attention to it. Now she found it again. *10.9 — Common room wall clock — new cell. Set by radio time.*

So four days before the photograph was taken, Dev had put a new battery in the common room clock and set it by the radio. A battery clock does not care whether the mains have failed. At the moment of that flash, the clock on the wall had been telling the truth, give or take a minute.

Eleven thirty-two on the night of the fourteenth, with the power out. Right in the middle of the forty-three minutes. Seventeen minutes after the institute's report said every resident had been in bed.

She wrote it down. Then she wrote, under it, the line from the RTI report that she now knew by heart: *P4-05 was observed by the night nurse entering her room at 23:15.*

And in the photograph, at 23:32, P4-05 was sitting on a sofa in the common room in a yellow raincoat with her hand on Meera's back.

She went through the rest of it like a checklist, because it calmed her to do it that way.

*Neelam's wrist.* A crepe bandage, wound from the knuckles to halfway up the forearm, very white in the flash. The medical register: *14.9.19 — 16:00 — P4-01 (Neelam) — slipped on wet steps near kitchen. R wrist painful, swollen. Crepe bandage applied.* Neelam herself, in the staff room in Uttarkashi, unprompted, rubbing the wrist with her thumb: *I'd hurt my wrist that day. In the afternoon, in the rain.*

On the evening of the thirteenth, Neelam's wrist had been perfectly well. It had not yet been sprained.

*The banner.* COHORT 4 — ALVIDA NAHI, PHIR MILENGE, hand-painted on a white cloth. She had photographed the last page of the activities register in the mortuary because it was there, and now she found the line. *14.9.19 — 15:00–17:00 — Banner for common room (P4 cohort). On the evening of the thirteenth, there had been no banner.

*Clothes.* Seven people in pyjamas and track pants and old sweaters, with shawls round their shoulders. Gaurav in a vest. Ritika in a T-shirt that said NO. Meera in her grey shawl over a cotton nightdress. Ananya in her raincoat, zipped to the chin, over pyjamas with small blue elephants on them. Nobody dresses like that for a farewell dinner with a speech.

And then the thing that settled it, the thing that did not come from the photograph at all.

"Kabir," she said, when he knocked on her door at ten with two glasses of the homestay's tea, "do you have anything from the farewell dinner? The real one. The thirteenth."

He looked at the photograph in the middle of the quilt, and at the circle of papers around it, and at her face.

"Yes," he said, and put the tea down, and sat on the floor with his back against the wall and scrolled through his phone. "The institute had a Facebook page. Ira took it down in 2020, but I'd saved everything on it in 2019, after." He turned the phone to her. "Posted on the fourteenth, at nine twelve in the morning."

It was a screenshot of a post. The institute's page, its little logo of a stylised mountain. *Farewell dinner for Cohort 4 last night! So proud of these brave, brilliant people. Eight weeks of courage. Thank you all. — Dr I.S.* And below it a photograph, taken in the dining hall in the warm, even light of electric bulbs: a long table with a white cloth, steel thalis, a cake. Ira at the head of the table in a dark silk sari, smiling her composed smile. The six participants and Meera arranged along both sides in their best clothes, in kurtas and a borrowed jacket, washed and combed.

Meera enlarged it with two fingers until it blurred.

Neelam, at the far end, holding up a glass of nimbu-paani in her right hand. Her right wrist bare. A thin silver bangle on it. No bandage.

Behind them, the dining hall wall. No banner.

Ananya, beside Meera, in a green kurta, laughing at the camera.

"That," said Meera, "is the thirteenth."

She held the phone beside the print on the quilt. Two photographs of the same seven people, taken a day apart. One in electric light at a dinner table with a cake, wrists bare, Ira at the head. One in lantern light on a sofa in a storm, in pyjamas, with a bandage and a banner and no Ira at all.

"And this one," she said, and put her finger on the print, "is not. It's the fourteenth. It's eleven thirty-two at night on the fourteenth, in the middle of the power cut that the institute told the police lasted ten minutes and was over by quarter past eleven. And somebody has written on the back of it that it's the farewell, on the thirteenth."

Kabir was very still against the wall.

"I can prove that," she went on. Her voice was steady. She was glad of the checklist. "Not from the clock alone. Clocks can be wrong. Not from the bandage alone, or the banner. But the maintenance log says the only dark outage was on the fourteenth, from 23:05 to 23:48. The medical register puts the bandage after four on the fourteenth. The activities register puts the banner after five on the fourteenth. The clock had a new battery on the tenth and says 23:32. Your screenshot shows the thirteenth with no bandage and no banner. And Neelam and Ritika and Tenzin, separately, all describe a lantern-lit gathering on the last night with a photograph taken by Sister on the institute's camera." She looked up. "Five independent records and three witnesses. It's the fourteenth. There's no other night it could be."

"Rajpur Road," said Kabir. "Near the clock tower. It's been there for thirty years."

A sloping, educated hand, quick and confident. Black ballpoint, firm. A capital *C* with a small hook at the top. A date written the way doctors write dates, with full stops between the day and the month and the year, and a nine that began with a loop at the top before it came down.

She unfolded Ira's note from the Rajpur Road study, the phone number with the date above it, and laid it on the quilt beside the photograph.

*18.9.26.*

Full stops between the numbers. A looped nine.

She put the two side by side under the bulb and looked from one to the other until the letters stopped looking like letters. She was careful. She had sat through a two-day course in Bengaluru on the reliability of eyewitness evidence, and half of one afternoon had been about handwriting. Untrained people, the lecturer had said cheerfully, are about as good at matching handwriting as they are at matching faces of strangers: much better than chance, and much worse than they think. Doctors write dates with full stops all over India. Loops on nines are taught in half the convent schools in the country.
