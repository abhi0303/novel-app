The excavator came up the valley in the dark, on a flatbed trailer behind a truck with one headlight. Meera heard it before she saw it: a deep labouring growl on the switchbacks below Dunda at a quarter to five, then the hiss of air brakes, then the truck's horn, three long blasts at the bend by the shrine to tell anyone coming down to wait.

She was standing at the window of her room in the homestay, still dressed, with the wall behind her covered in paper.

She had not slept at all. After the photograph, after Ananya's eyes, she had sat on the bed until one in the morning, and then she had got up and taken every sheet of paper she had, her mortuary columns, her Room 7 notebook, the pages she had copied from Dev's log, the RTI report, the backs of three homestay bills, and stuck them to the whitewashed wall above the bed with Bisht-ji's wife's masking tape, in order, from left to right. Then she had stood back and looked at it, the way she looked at a case formulation on the whiteboard of a hospital ward round. Not to decide what it meant. To see whether it held.

It held. That was the worst of it. Every line she had stuck up had a source beside it, and every source was a different person or a different book, and they agreed.

At five Kabir knocked. He had a phone in one hand and two glasses of tea in the other, and he stopped in the doorway and looked at the wall.

"You did that in the night," he said.

"Yes." She took the tea. "Who were you calling?"

"Ankita Bhandari. The SP. I interviewed her in 2021 when she was commandant of the SDRF battalion in Dehradun, about the Chamoli flood. She gave me her number and said, *if you ever have something real, not a story, call me.*" He looked at the wall again. "I told her I had something real. She'll see us at eight, at her office in Uttarkashi, before her ten o'clock. She said bring everything and everyone."

"Everyone."

"Dev's already gone down to the gate. He says the demolition crew won't get his keys until someone in a uniform tells him to give them." Kabir almost smiled. "He's sitting in the guard hut with the door bolted. Neelam's taking a half-day. Tenzin's on duty at the hospital, three hundred metres from the SP's office; she'll walk over." He hesitated. "I didn't call Ira."

"No," said Meera. "The SP will."

---

The office of the Superintendent of Police, Uttarkashi, was a two-storey building of pale yellow plaster on a ridge above the town, with a garden of marigolds and a flagpole and a view straight down the valley to the river. At eight in the morning the corridor outside the SP's room was already full: constables with files, a man with a land dispute, two women from a village up the Assi Ganga valley with a complaint about a missing goat.

Ankita Bhandari saw them at eight exactly. She was in her mid-forties, small and square-shouldered, in uniform, with her hair pulled back and a pair of reading glasses pushed up on her head. Behind her desk hung a framed photograph of an SDRF team on a rope across a flooded river. She did not stand up. She looked at the five of them filing into her office, Meera, Kabir, Dev with his wool cap crushed in his hands, Neelam in her school cardigan, and a constable bringing extra chairs, and said: "Mr Qureshi. You said something real."

"Dr Sanyal will tell you," said Kabir.

The SP turned her eyes to Meera. They were tired, intelligent, unimpressed eyes, the eyes of a woman who had pulled a great many bodies out of a great many rivers and had stopped expecting anything.

"Twenty minutes, Doctor," she said. "Then I have the district magistrate. Tell me what happened, and tell me how you know."

Meera had thought, in the car, about how to do this. She had decided to do it the way she had been taught to present a case to a consultant who did not have time: the conclusion first, in one sentence, and then the evidence, in order, every piece with its source, so that the listener could stop her at any point and check.

"Ananya Rao didn't leave the Himgiri Institute on the night of the fourteenth of September 2019," she said. "She died on the cliff path behind it, about four hundred metres from the gate, when the retaining wall collapsed under her at about a quarter to midnight. Her body went into the gorge. The search in 2019 never looked there because the institute told the police the path couldn't be reached from the building, and that she'd walked out of the main gate. Both of those things were false." She put the first sheet of paper on the SP's desk. "Here's how I know."

---

She went through it in order. She would remember afterwards that her voice did not shake once, and that she had felt, the whole time, like a woman reading out her own medical report in a language she happened to speak.

*The power.* The maintenance log, in Dev's hand, written that night: mains failure at 23:05. The generator's automatic start, three attempts, failed, lockout. No power to the building until a manual start at 23:47 and load at 23:48. Forty-three minutes. The institute's report to the State Mental Health Authority said *a brief power fluctuation* and *the generator took load*. She laid the log and the report side by side.

"I wrote that," said Dev from his chair, without being asked. "On the night. In the generator room. It's true."

*The door.* The 2016 approved plan, which showed the east end of the residential corridor as a sealed store with no external access. The east door itself, which existed, which was steel with a push bar and a wired-glass window, red on its outside face, and which opened onto an iron landing with a stair to the generator room and the cliff path. The maglock on it, wired to the mains, fail-safe: unlocked whenever the power failed. Dev's log, on the night: *23:06 — to DG room via E door.* Photographs she had taken on Friday of the door, the landing, the stair, the path, the gap.

The SP picked up the photograph of the gap and looked at it for a long time.

"The path," said Meera. "Dev's log, second of September: the retaining wall thirty-five to forty-five metres from the landing bulging, seepage, path closed, barricade tape. My own diary, the same day: *Dev put tape across the cliff walk because the wall is leaning.* On the night of the fourteenth, Dev's log has a line scratched out at about 23:45. Under raking light you can read *loud crash E side*. Dev scratched it out the next evening, on the director's instruction."

"On the director's instruction," the SP repeated.

"Yes, madam," said Dev.

*The photograph.* Meera put it on the desk, face up, beside the log. Seven people in lantern light. The clock at 11:32. Neelam's bandaged wrist, matched against the medical register, *14.9.19, 16:00, P4-01, crepe bandage*. The banner, matched against the activities register, *14.9.19, 15:00–17:00*. The clock's new battery, from the maintenance log, the tenth. The institute's own Facebook post of the real farewell dinner on the thirteenth, from Kabir's archive: no bandage, no banner, electric light, Ira at the head of the table. And on the back of the print, in black ballpoint, *Cohort 4 — Farewell — 13.9.19*, and the Dehradun lab's machine backprint: *20 09 19*.

"That's my wrist," said Neelam quietly. She was looking at the photograph with both hands pressed together in her lap. "I'd forgotten that was the same day. I'd have sworn the farewell and the power cut were the same night. I'd have sworn it." She looked up at the SP. "I signed a statement that said everyone was in bed by quarter past eleven. That's a quarter past eleven, madam?"

"That's eleven thirty-two," said the SP, looking at the clock in the photograph.

*The people.* Tenzin had come in during the photograph, in her white uniform, and stood just inside the door, and when the SP looked at her she gave her account in four sentences, as precisely as she had in the canteen: the common room, the photograph, the walk back down the corridor at about 23:36, Ananya taking Meera into Room 7 and coming out with her finger on her lips and going into Room 6. And the lights coming on by themselves in Room 3 while she lay on her bed, and her watch saying 11:48.

"Matches the log," said the SP. She did not say it to anyone in particular.

*The tape.* Meera put the recorder on the desk and played thirty seconds of the second session, the rain on the tin roof, Ananya whispering, *Tomorrow's the last day*, the fan winding down, the three thumps through the floor, *light gayi*, the far-off call, *chalo, raincoat*, the zip. The SP sat with her chin on her fist and listened without moving. When it stopped she said, "Again," and Meera played it again.

"The three sounds," said the SP.

"The generator's automatic start. Three attempts. 23:05." Meera put Dev's log beside the recorder. "It's the only time in the last two weeks of the programme that the generator failed three times in a row. That part of the tape was recorded in Room 6 on the night of the fourteenth, a few minutes before she went to the common room. She'd put her raincoat on. Ritika Sharma, another participant, says she wore it indoors all through the gathering, and when Ritika asked her if she was going somewhere, she said *maybe*."

"And the institute's report?"

"Says her raincoat, her outdoor shoes and her torch were missing in the morning." Meera turned a page of the RTI copy and put her finger on the line. "Which is true."

---

The SP's ten o'clock came and went. A constable put his head round the door and was waved away. Someone brought tea in thick white cups that nobody drank.

*Dev.*

The SP turned to him at last, and Dev Malik stood up out of his chair, because that was what you did in front of a uniform, and told it again: the push bar at about 23:41; the single small torch moving fast along the cliff path at about 23:43, east, away from the landing; the crash at about 23:45, and the light spinning and going out; his own torch pointed up at about 23:46, and behind the wired glass of the red door a woman with long loose hair, in white, her hand flat on the glass, pulling the door shut. The three cranks at 23:47. The lights at 23:48. The empty landing at 23:55, the locked door, the path ending in air. The slipper on the broken edge at six-thirty in the morning, which he gave to the nurse, who gave it to the director, who took it away in a plastic bag.

"And the woman at the door," said the SP.

Dev looked at Meera. Then at the floor. Then at the SP.

"Meera didi," he said.

The SP turned her eyes back to Meera, and Meera met them.

"That's right," she said. "It was me."

She put the last sheets down. The pharmacy register for the fourteenth: *R7, zolpidem, 10 mg*. Room 7 was hers; she had been the director's unregistered guest; she was not on any list. The pharmacy register for the morning of the fifteenth: *R7, 06:15, dressings, bandages, tetanus toxoid*. The medical register for the same morning, with no entry at all for R7. Her own fragments, written down before she had heard most of the tape or any of Dev's account: a red door seen from its outer face; a hand flat on wet glass; three knocks after the door; the lights coming on while she stood in the corridor. Each one beside its time in the log.

"I don't remember it as a story," she said. "I have pieces. I've tested every piece I have against something that isn't me. They all fit Dev's account. I was at that door at about 23:46, on ten milligrams of zolpidem I didn't know I was taking, and I closed it. Two minutes later the generator locked it. A minute before that, the wall had already gone. At dawn I was put in a car and sent to Dehradun before your officers arrived, and I was told I'd slept through the night."

"By whom?"

"By Dr Ira Sen. At the railway station in Dehradun. And everyone else at the institute was told the same by her that evening. That Ananya had chosen to leave. That she'd walked out of the gate." Meera put down the RTI report, open at page seven, the black bar across it. "This sentence was redacted when Mr Qureshi got the report under RTI, as third-party information. I think it's about me. I think it says the institute had a guest in Room 7 who was sent home before the police came. Your office can ask the Mental Health Authority for the unredacted copy. They won't refuse a Superintendent of Police."

The SP looked at the black bar for a long time. Then she picked up her desk phone, pressed a button, and said, "Get me the Member Secretary at the State Mental Health Authority. And find out who was in charge at Dunda chowki in September 2019."

---

The man who had been in charge at Dunda chowki in September 2019 was now an inspector at a police station in Haridwar, and he came on the SP's speakerphone at ten forty, a careful, tired voice with the hum of traffic behind it.

Yes, madam, he remembered the case. A girl from Hyderabad, an adult. The director reported her missing on the evening of the fifteenth. No, not that morning; the evening. They'd said they were searching the grounds themselves. When he went up on the sixteenth the director gave him the plan of the building and a typed timeline. Lights out at ten, a short power fluctuation, everyone in bed by quarter past eleven, the girl seen going into her room. Raincoat and shoes and torch gone. The gate guard had been sheltering. She'd told the director she wanted to leave. There was a boyfriend in Delhi, there were his letters in her room. The family were saying elopement and then not saying it. It looked like what it looked like.

"Did you search the slope?" said the SP. "Below the cliff path at the east end?"

A pause.

"Madam, the director said the east end was sealed. No access from the building. The path there had been closed for weeks; there was a collapse that week, there were collapses all over the district that week, the Gangotri highway was blocked in three places. We searched the road, the bus stand, the jeep stands, the river at Dunda bridge. We circulated her photo." Another pause, longer. "Nobody told us anyone could get onto that path from inside."

"Nobody told you," said the SP.

"No, madam."

She thanked him and cut the call.

She sat for a while looking at the papers covering her desk: the log, the plan, the photograph, the recorder, the RTI report, Meera's handwritten columns, Kabir's copy of Ananya's pharmacy table in its plastic sleeve. Outside the window the valley had cleared, and the river at the bottom of it was the colour of milky tea, fast and full after the rain.

"Seven years," she said. "A missing adult. A cliff path that fell into a river during the worst monsoon week in a decade. A lawfully permitted demolition, starting," she looked at her watch, "two hours and forty minutes ago. A developer with very good lawyers. A former director with a defamation injunction and friends in Dehradun." She looked at Meera. "And you want me to stop it."

"I want you to look," said Meera. "Below the path. Before they bring the east end down on top of it."

The SP did not answer at once. She picked up the photograph and looked at it again: the seven faces, the lanterns, the clock.

"You understand what you're telling me about yourself, Doctor," she said. It was not unkind. "You're a clinical psychologist. You know how this sounds. You're telling me you were drugged, without consent, by the director of a research institute, and that in that condition you were the last person to see a missing woman alive, and that you shut a door behind her on a cliff in a storm. You're telling me you've reconstructed this from records and fragments and other people's accounts, seven years later." She put the photograph down. "A defence lawyer would say every one of your memories was suggested to you this week. By a note you received, by a journalist, by a caretaker with a guilty conscience."

"Yes," said Meera. "A defence lawyer would be right to say it. That's why I haven't asked you to believe a single thing I remember. I've only asked you to believe the log and the registers and the photograph and the tape and the plan and the door." She held the SP's eyes. "They don't have memories. They can't be suggested to."

The SP looked at her for a long moment. Then she picked up the phone again.

---

It took forty minutes and four calls.

At 11:52 the SP's reader typed an order on a laptop at the side table, printed it on a wheezing printer, and brought it in for her signature. *Demolition of the east wing of the property known as Himgiri Institute / St Luke's Sanatorium, Kothiyal Dhar, Dunda, stayed for seventy-two hours pending a search of the slope below the collapsed cliff path and preservation of the east service door, landing and stair as a site of interest in the missing person inquiry registered at Dunda in September 2019, now reopened.* A second order, to the SDRF post at Uttarkashi, for a team with ropes. A third, a notice under her own signature to Dr Ira Sen, Rajpur Road, Dehradun, to present herself at this office at four that afternoon to give a statement.

Dev took his keys out of his pocket and looked at them as if he had forgotten what they were for.

The SP stood up for the first time that morning.

"Dr Sanyal," she said. "I'll need your statement in writing. Today. Everything you've told me, everything in that notebook, with times. You'll sign it." She paused. "You understand what you'll be signing."

"Yes."

"Mr Malik. The same. And the original log comes to this office in a sealed envelope. Today." Dev nodded, his wool cap crushed in both hands. "Ms Bisht, Sister Dolma, I'll send someone this afternoon." She looked at Kabir. "And you, Mr Qureshi, will not publish one word of any of this until I tell you that you can."

"No, madam."

At the door, the SP touched Meera's arm, briefly, the way doctors do on ward rounds, to stop her.

"Why was she on that path?" she said. "Ananya Rao. At a quarter to midnight, in a storm, on a path she knew was closed. If she was leaving, why not wait for the morning? She was leaving in the morning anyway."

Meera looked at her.

"I don't know," she said.

It was the only line on the wall of her room in the homestay that did not have a source beside it.

---

She wrote her statement that afternoon in a small room on the ground floor of the SP's office, at a steel table with a constable at the door, in her own handwriting on long sheets of ruled paper. It took two hours. She wrote it the way she had presented it: conclusion first, then evidence, in order, every line with its time and its source. When she came to the part about the door she stopped for a while with the pen above the paper, and then wrote it plainly, in one sentence, because there was no other way to write it.

*At approximately 23:46 on 14 September 2019 I was standing inside the east service door of the Himgiri Institute and I closed it.*

Then she wrote the rest. At the end she signed it, and dated it, and the constable witnessed it, and it went into a file with a number on the cover.

It was the official truth now. Everything in it was correct. She had checked every line herself, against every record there was.

And underneath it all, like a stone under a quilt, she could feel the photograph in her bag. The seven faces in the lantern light, all turned to the camera except one. And that one turned to her.
