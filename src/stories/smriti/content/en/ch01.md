The parcel was waiting with the guard.

Prakash had kept it on the shelf inside his cabin at the gate of Vishnu Apartments, between the visitors' register and a steel tiffin. When Meera came in from the road at twenty to nine, damp from the drizzle that had followed her all the way from C-Scheme, he stood up and held it out with both hands. He did that with anything that looked official.

"Courier, didi. Came at four. He wanted a signature, so I signed."

"Thank you, Prakash-ji."

It was the size of a shoebox and heavier than a shoebox should be, wrapped in brown paper and a great deal of brown tape. Someone had wanted it to arrive whole. Her name was written on the front twice, once in English capitals and once in Hindi, in the same careful, rounded hand: DR. MEERA SANYAL, FLAT 304, VISHNU APARTMENTS, SHYAM NAGAR, JAIPUR. A courier slip was stuck in the corner. Booked at a counter in Dehradun on the thirteenth. Paid in cash. Sender: *S.* Below that, a mobile number.

Meera stood under the bare bulb outside the cabin and read the slip twice. She did not know anyone in Dehradun. That was not quite true, and she knew it was not quite true as she thought it. But she did not know anyone in Dehradun who would send her a parcel and sign it with one letter.

"Everything all right, didi?"

"Yes," she said. "Probably a book."

It was not a book. She knew that from the weight, and from the way something inside shifted against the cardboard with a small hard knock as she climbed the stairs.

Her flat was three rooms on the third floor, facing a neem tree and the back of a girls' hostel. She had lived there alone for four years. It was quiet in the way she had made it quiet: no television, plants on every windowsill, a shelf of journals in the order she had read them. She put the parcel on the dining table, switched on the lamp, took off her sandals and stood for a moment on the cool floor with her eyes closed. She did that every evening, like a full stop.

When she opened her eyes she was looking at her own feet. There were faint white lines across both soles, thin as threads, visible only when the skin was pale from cold or wet from rain. She had had them as long as she could remember. A childhood thing, she had always assumed. Glass on the terrace at Bani Park, or a broken diya. She had never asked.

She fetched scissors from the kitchen.

Professionally, Meera Sanyal believed in doing things in order. She saw people in her consulting room who had lost a parent, a child, a marriage, a version of themselves, and the first thing she taught all of them was that the mind under stress will happily skip steps, and that you have to put the steps back. So she put the steps back. She photographed the parcel on all six sides with her phone. She photographed the courier slip. She dialled the number on it and listened to a recorded woman tell her, in Hindi and then English, that the number did not exist. Then she cut the tape along one edge and lifted the lid.

Newspaper first. Old copies of *Amar Ujala*, Dehradun edition, crumpled into balls for padding. Under them, three things laid side by side as carefully as cutlery.

A portable cassette recorder, the kind she had not seen since school. It was grey plastic with a carrying strap, a row of piano-key buttons along the top and a small round speaker grille. Someone had painted *HIMR 3* on its side in white, in neat stencil letters.

A cassette, in a clear plastic case.

A photograph, face down, with a folded sheet of lined paper on top of it.

She knew the letters on the recorder before she had finished reading them, the way you know your own name in a crowded room. *HIMR.* Himgiri Institute for Memory and Recovery. A long, low stone building on a ridge in Uttarkashi, with a river at the bottom of the valley so far down that you heard it only at night.

She sat down.

Seven years. She did the arithmetic without wanting to. July to September 2019. She had been twenty-four, five months out of the worst week of her life, and unable to finish a sentence in her M.Phil seminars without forgetting how it ended. Dr Ira Sen, who had been her father's friend for thirty years, had telephoned her mother and said, in the voice that made people do things: *Send her to me. Not as a patient. As my guest. Mountains, quiet, a room with a view. She can sit in on whatever she likes.*

So Meera had gone. Eight weeks. She remembered it the way she remembered most of that year: clearly in places and not at all in others, like a house where someone had switched off half the lights. She remembered the cold of the mornings and the smell of deodar resin on the path. She remembered group sessions in a room with a long mirror on one wall, and a girl who laughed too loudly at the wrong moments and called her *Rajkumari*. She remembered the farewell dinner, and Ira making a speech. She remembered being driven down the mountain at dawn in the rain.

And she remembered being told, at a railway station, that the girl who laughed had gone.

She unfolded the sheet of paper.

It had been torn from a cheap ruled notebook, the kind sold in every village shop. One line, in Hindi, in the same careful rounded hand as the address. The letters were formed the way someone forms letters who writes more numbers than words: slowly, each one finished before the next began.

*Tum us raat wahan thi. Tumne hi darwaza band kiya tha.*

*Darwaza.* The door.

It was a four-by-six print, glossy, a little curled at the corners. Seven people were squeezed together in a room lit by something warm and low, lanterns maybe, so that their faces were gold on one side and lost on the other. The flash had caught them all mid-laugh or mid-blink. Behind them was a window so black it might have been painted, with rain running down it in streaks the flash had turned to silver. On the wall above them hung a banner of white cloth, painted by hand in uneven letters: COHORT 4 — ALVIDA NAHI, PHIR MILENGE. Not goodbye, see you again. Next to the banner was a round wall clock with a plain white face. Its hands said eleven thirty-two.

She found herself first. You always do.

She was sitting on the arm of a sofa on the right of the group, in a grey shawl she had forgotten she owned, with her hair loose and too long, the way it had been that year because she had not been able to make herself go to a salon. She looked thin. She looked, she thought with professional detachment, sedated: her eyes were half closed, and her smile came a fraction later than everyone else's. She did not remember this moment. She did not remember sitting on that sofa arm, or the lanterns, or the banner, or anyone saying *smile*. She looked at her own face for a long time, waiting for it to become familiar from the inside, and it did not.

Then she let herself look at the others.

Gaurav at the back, tall, unsmiling, with his army haircut. Farhan in front of him, making a face. Ritika, the youngest, kneeling at the front with two fingers up behind someone's head. Neelam in a cardigan, holding her right arm awkwardly against her body; there was something white wrapped around her wrist. Tenzin with her round glasses, laughing.

And on the sofa next to Meera, so close their shoulders touched, a girl with a short black bob and enormous dark eyes, her head turned a little from the camera as if she had missed the moment, wearing a yellow raincoat over her night clothes as if she had just come in from somewhere, or was about to go out.

Gauraiya.

Meera's chest did something she had spent years teaching other people to notice and name. *There it is,* she thought. *That's grief. And under it, that's anger.* She had been angry for a long time after the hills. Gauraiya, who had sat on the end of her bed every night for six weeks, who had made her laugh for the first time since February, who had said *we're sisters now, Rajkumari, that's final*, had walked out of the institute in the middle of the last night without a word to anyone. Without a note. Without even the name she had promised to tell Meera on the last day.

*She flew off into her own sky,* Ira had said at the station, holding both of Meera's hands. *You were asleep, beta. Everyone was asleep. Let her go.*

So Meera had let her go. Or she had done what people do instead, which is to stop looking.

She turned the photograph over again. On the back, in black ballpoint, in a sloping, educated hand quite different from the note's: *Cohort 4 — Farewell — 13.9.19.*

The thirteenth. She remembered the farewell dinner on the thirteenth. Ira's speech, the dal makhani the cook had made specially, everyone dressed up a little. She did not remember lanterns. She did not remember rain on that window.

She turned the photograph face up and looked at the clock again. Eleven thirty-two.

It was ten past nine. The drizzle had become rain. Somewhere below, the hostel girls were watching something loud on a phone.

Meera did not play the tape.

She sat with the recorder in front of her and made herself do something first, the thing she would have told a client to do. She took a fresh page from the notepad by her phone and wrote at the top: *What I remember of 13–15 September 2019. Written 16.9.26, 9.12 pm, before listening.* Then she wrote it down, quickly, without stopping to judge it, because the order mattered. Memory is not a recording. Every time you take it out and look at it, you put it back a little different. If she was about to hear something that would change what she remembered, she wanted a record of what she had remembered before.

*13th (Fri): farewell dinner. Ira-di's speech. Dal makhani. G. did impressions of everyone, including Ira-di. Ira-di laughed.*

*14th (Sat): rain all day. Packing? Don't remember packing. Don't remember the evening. Took the night tablet (the nurse gave it every night; "for sleep"). Slept.*

*15th (Sun): woken very early. Dark. Rain. Car with Rawat-ji, the driver. Down the mountain. Dehradun station, Ira-di waiting. "You were asleep, beta. Everyone was asleep. Gauraiya flew off into her own sky." Train to Delhi. Home.*

*G. had left in the night. Didn't say goodbye. Never told me her real name.*

She looked at what she had written. It took up less than half a page. The whole last day of the most important two months of her twenties, and it fitted on half a page.

Underneath, after a pause, she added a list she had written many times before, in other notebooks, in her own therapy, on the backs of envelopes at three in the morning.

*Recurring images (since 2019):*
*1. A red door.*
*2. A wall clock.*
*3. A dark corridor.*
*4. A hand pressed flat on glass. Behind the hand, letters I can never read.*
*5. Three knocks.*

Her father had died on a Sunday afternoon in February, in his study, of a heart that had simply stopped. Meera had been in the next room with headphones on, preparing a seminar. She had not heard him fall. She had found him an hour later, when she went to ask whether he wanted tea, and the first thing she had looked at, stupidly, was the clock on his wall. So: a door she had not opened in time. A clock. A corridor. A knock she had not heard. It fitted. It fitted so well that she had stopped asking whether it fitted.

The cassette was a TDK, sixty minutes, the brand her father had used for his lecture recordings. A white label had been printed with the institute's stamp: *HIMR · VOICE LETTERS*. She remembered the voice letters suddenly and completely, the way some memories come back whole. Ira's idea. Each of them had sat alone in Therapy B with this same kind of recorder and talked to someone, living or dead, for as long as they liked. Meera had spoken to her father for twenty minutes and cried for ten of them. The tapes were to be handed over on the last day.

For Meera. From G.

Below it, in black ballpoint, in a different hand, a date: *7.9.19.* Not the same pen. Not the same pressure. As if someone had come along afterwards and dated it the way a librarian dates a book.

She sat looking at the two inks for a long time.

Then she checked the recorder. The batteries were new: four AA cells, the copper tops still bright, the printed expiry 2034. Someone had made sure that it would play. She slid the cassette in, side A facing out as the label said, closed the little door, pressed rewind and listened to the whir run down until it clicked. She put her phone beside the speaker grille and started a voice recording on it, because she was the kind of person who did that. Then she pressed PLAY.

Hiss. A long, soft, old hiss, like rain heard through a wall.

Then birds. A barbet calling somewhere outside a window, the two-note call that had woken her every morning in the hills. *Kutroo, kutroo.* A jeep horn, far away and below, the way sound came up from the road. A small click and a rustle, as if someone was settling closer to the microphone.

And a voice.

"Meera, agar tum ye recording sun rahi ho, toh iska matlab hai ki tumhe abhi tak sach nahi bataya gaya."

Meera, if you're listening to this recording, it means you still haven't been told the truth.

A pause. A breath, close to the microphone. Then a laugh, a small one, embarrassed, quickly swallowed. Meera knew that laugh. She knew it the way she knew the barbet: before she knew that she knew it.

"Ya phir maine himmat nahi ki, aur seedha ye tape pakda diya. Dono mein se ek."

Or I didn't have the nerve, and just handed you this tape. One of the two.

Another pause. The barbet again. Then the voice came back, lower and steadier, as if the person speaking had decided to stop joking.

"Main Ananya hoon. Tumhari behen."

I am Ananya. Your sister.

Meera pressed STOP so hard that the recorder skidded on the table.

The room was very quiet. Rain on the neem tree. A girl laughing in the hostel. Her own breath.

She had never had a sister. She had never known anyone named Ananya.

But she knew that voice. She had heard it through a bedroom wall for six weeks, and at the end of her bed every night, and in a lantern-lit room she could not remember, at eleven thirty-two on a night she had been told she slept through.

It was Gauraiya's.
