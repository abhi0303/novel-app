The generator room smelled of diesel and hot metal and wet stone, and it was so loud inside that Meera had to stand close to Dev Malik to be heard.

He did not want her there. She could see it in the way he kept himself between her and the machine, as if it were an animal that might bite: a squat green engine the size of a small car, bolted to a concrete plinth, shaking on its mountings and breathing out heat. Gauges on a grey steel panel. A battery on a wooden shelf with crocodile clips still attached. A single bare bulb hanging from a flex. One small window, set high in the east wall, with bars across it and a pane of glass so grimed with exhaust that the evening came through it brown.

She held out the red account book, open at the fourteenth of September.

"Is this your writing, Dev-ji?"

He looked at it for a long time without taking it.

"Haan."

"Will you tell me about that night?"

"I told the police." He raised his voice over the engine, flatly. "Seven years ago. The current went for a little while. The generator came on. I was here in the DG room. I didn't see anything. In the morning the girl was gone."

"The generator came on by itself?"

"Haan. Automatic." He gestured at the grey panel. "AMF. Auto mains failure. Current goes, it starts by itself, ten seconds. It took load. A little while, then the current came back."

"What time did it come on?"

He frowned. "Twelve. After twelve, maybe. Quarter past twelve."

Meera turned the book round so that he could read it under the bare bulb, and put her finger on the lines one at a time.

*23:05 — Mains fail (storm). AMF auto-start: 3 attempts — FAIL. Lockout.*

*23:47 — Manual start — 3 cranks — OK.*

*23:48 — On load.*

She did not say anything. She had learned long ago that the most powerful thing you can do with a piece of evidence is put it down in front of someone and wait.

Dev looked at his own handwriting.

She watched it happen to him, and it was not what she expected. She expected the face of a man caught in a lie: the flush, the anger, the quick new story. What she saw instead was confusion. He looked at the page the way people in her consulting room sometimes looked at a photograph of themselves at a party they would have sworn they never went to. His lips moved, reading. *Fail. Lockout. Manual.*

"I wrote this," he said slowly.

"Yes."

"That night. I wrote it that night, in this room. By torch." He touched the page. "Three times it tried. Three times, nothing. Lockout. The battery was weak, there was air in the fuel line. I had to bleed it by hand, in the dark, with the torch in my teeth." He looked up at her, and his face was genuinely bewildered. "Why did I say automatic?"

"Because you've been saying it for seven years," said Meera quietly.

He stared at her.

"You said it to the police," she said, "and to Dr Sen, and to whoever asked, and to yourself. *A little while. It came on by itself.* You said it so many times that it became what you remember. It isn't lying, Dev-ji. It happens to everyone. It happens to me." She took her finger off the page. "But this is what you wrote that night, before anyone told you what to say. This is the one I believe."

The engine roared on. Dev stood with his big hands hanging at his sides, looking at his own log as if it had been written by a stranger.

"Madam said," he began, and stopped.

"What did Madam say?"

He did not answer for a long time. Then he reached past her, switched off the engine at the panel, and in the enormous sudden silence that rang in their ears like a struck bell, he said, "Come outside. I'll show you. Then you can ask."

They went back down the iron stair in the dusk, one at a time, and stood on the terrace again at the foot of it. The generator room was a black box beside them with its high barred window. Above them the red door was only a darker shape in the dark wall.

Kabir had picked up the box of registers. Dev had gone ahead to lock the mortuary, and they could hear his keys along the terrace.

Meera did not move. She stood at the foot of the stair and looked at the generator room window, and then up at the landing, and then along the line of the path where it ran east above them in the dark.

She had the plan in her head. She had stood in front of it in the hall the day before and photographed it twice, and she could see it now as clearly as if it were drawn on the evening in front of her. The residential corridor, long and straight, with its seven small rooms on the south side. Room 7 at the end. Beyond Room 7, a small square marked STORE (SEALED). Nothing beyond that. No door. No landing. No stair. A wall.

And on a different sheet altogether, the services drawing, the one the architect had not bothered to frame: the lower terrace, the laundry, the mortuary, the pump room, the electrical room, and at its eastern end a box marked DG ROOM. A different floor. A different world. You reached it, according to the drawings, by going out of the kitchen, down the service ramp, and along the terrace. Six minutes, Dev said. Seven in the rain.

According to the drawings, there was no way at all from the corridor outside Room 7 to the generator room, or to the cliff path, or to the road below the gate. According to the drawings, a girl who wanted to leave the institute in the night had to go through the front hall, past the duty room, out of the front door and past the guard at the gate. She looked up at the barred window of the generator room. From in there, by the light of a torch in the dark, a man standing at the engine could look up through that glass and see the iron stair, and the landing, and the door. And beyond the door, the path.

On paper, Room 7, the generator room and the cliff path were three places in two different drawings, with a wall and a whole floor between them.
