Neelam Bisht taught Hindi and social science to classes six to eight at a government girls' school in Uttarkashi, and she had chosen to meet them in the empty staff room after the last bell, because, she said on the phone, it was the only room in her life where nobody would ask who they were.

She was thirty-six now. She had cut her hair short and wore a navy cardigan over her salwar kameez, and when Meera came in through the staff-room door she stood up so fast that her chair fell over behind her.

"Meera-di," she said. "*Meera-di.*" And then she crossed the room and put her arms round Meera, briefly and hard, the way you hold someone at a railway platform, and stepped back with her eyes wet. "They told us your mother was ill. You were gone before breakfast. Not even your bag, Sister packed your bag. Seven years. I thought about you so many times."

"I'm sorry," Meera said. She did not know what she was apologising for. "I didn't know anybody's surname. I didn't know how to find anyone."

"Nobody did. That was the rule." Neelam wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand and laughed. "First names only. No addresses. No phones. *Leave your old life at the gate.* Ira ma'am's rules." She looked past Meera. "Namaste, Kabir-ji."

"Namaste, Neelam-ji."

So they knew each other. Meera filed it. Neelam picked up her chair and they sat round the end of the long staff-room table, under a framed portrait of the President and a chart of the school's board results, with the rain coming down outside on the playground and somewhere a peon sweeping a corridor.

---

Meera did not show her the photograph straight away. She had thought about this carefully in the car. Show a witness a picture and ask what they remember, and they will remember the picture. So she asked first, in the way she had been trained to ask, open and slow, and let Neelam go where she wanted.

Neelam went first to the cold. Then the food, the rajma, the six o'clock chai. Then the sessions, which she described carefully and without detail, the way people do who have been somewhere that changed them and are not sure yet how. She had lost her brother in the floods of 2013, at Kedarnath, and had not been able to go near a river for six years. "Now I teach on the bank of the Bhagirathi," she said, and smiled. "So something worked."

Then, without being asked, she went to Gauraiya.

"She was the noisiest person I've ever met. In the good way. She came on the first day with a bag full of Parle-G and gave one to everyone, even Gaurav, who never smiled, and she said, *I'm Gauraiya, I nest in houses, don't worry, I don't bite.* And she did impressions of everybody. The cook. Sister Annamma. Ira ma'am." Neelam shook her head. "Ira ma'am's was perfect. The pause before every sentence. *And how… does that… make you feel.*" Her voice dropped into a dry, precise register, and for a second Meera heard Ira Sen in the room and laughed before she could stop herself.

"And you two," said Neelam. "From the second week. You'd sit on the end of the corridor terrace and talk till the nurse sent you to bed. We made jokes. Farhan put it in the newsletter."

"I saw it."

"The samosa." Neelam laughed again, then stopped. "She looked after you, didi. You were not well then. You'd lost your father. She used to say, *my Rajkumari doesn't eat enough*, and put her own roti on your plate."

Meera kept her face still. She was good at that.

"Tell me about the last night," she said.

Neelam's face changed. Not much. A slight withdrawal, as if a door had been drawn half closed.

"The last night was the farewell," she said. "We had dinner, Ira ma'am made a speech, Gauraiya copied the speech. Everyone was laughing."

"The farewell was the thirteenth," said Meera gently. "Friday. Ira-di left for Dehradun on Saturday morning. I mean the Saturday night. The fourteenth."

"Oh." Neelam frowned. "Yes. Saturday. Sorry, it's all one in my head." She looked down at her right hand on the table and rubbed the wrist with her thumb, absently, as if it hurt. "I'd hurt my wrist that day, you know. Slipped on the steps by the kitchen in the afternoon, in the rain. Sister bandaged it. I was feeling sorry for myself all evening."

Meera wrote nothing down. She looked at the hand.

"And that night?"

"It rained. There was a power cut, quite late, after lights out. Sister brought lanterns and made us all come to the common room. It was short. Ten minutes, maybe? Then the generator came on and we went back to bed." Neelam spoke more quickly now, more smoothly, the way people speak when they are on a path they have walked before. "Gauraiya was there. Everyone was there. I went to bed at quarter past eleven. And in the morning she was gone."

*Quarter past eleven.* The phrase sat on the table between them. It was the time in the RTI report, almost to the minute: *dispersed at approximately 23:15*.

"How do you know it was quarter past eleven?" Meera asked.

Neelam blinked. "Because…" She stopped. "Because that's when it was." Then, after a moment, more slowly: "We had to write statements the next day. For the police. Joshi-ji typed them in the office and we read them and signed them. I suppose I remember what I signed."

"Do you remember Gauraiya in the common room? What she was doing?"

"She was being Gauraiya. Singing. Badly." Neelam smiled. "And looking after you. You were so sleepy you could hardly sit up. You kept nodding off on the arm of the sofa. She had her hand on your back the whole time, like you'd fall off." The smile faded. "I didn't see her after that. In the morning Ira ma'am came… no, Ira ma'am came in the evening. In the morning Sister said Gauraiya had left in the night. And when Ira ma'am came she sat us all down in the common room and talked to us."

"What did she say?"

Neelam looked out of the window at the rain on the empty playground. When she answered, her voice had changed again. It was quieter, and certain, and very slightly formal, the voice of somebody saying a thing they had long ago decided was true.

"She said Gauraiya had told her she wanted to leave. She said some people need to fly." Neelam turned back to Meera. "And honestly, didi, I think that's right. She wasn't made to be kept anywhere. *Gauraiya apne aasmaan mein ud gayi.*"

She flew off into her own sky.

Meera felt the words go into her like a cold coin into a slot. She kept her face still. Beside her, Kabir did not move at all.

Then she took the photograph out of the envelope and put it on the table, and Neelam looked at it and put her bandaged-in-memory hand over her mouth and began, quite silently, to cry.

At nine that night, in the homestay at Dunda where they had taken two rooms, Meera rang the nurse.

Kabir had found the number. It had taken him three years, he said, and a cousin of a cousin of a ward boy at a hospital in Kottayam, and Sister Annamma Varghese had never once agreed to speak to him. "She might speak to you," he said. "You were a patient. Sort of."

The phone rang a long time. Then a woman's voice, older than Meera expected, with the soft Malayali music in its Hindi.

"Hello? Who is this?"

"Sister, my name is Meera. Meera Sanyal. I was at the Himgiri Institute in 2019. In Room 7. Dr Sen's—"

The silence on the line was so complete that she thought the call had dropped.

"Sister?"

"Meera mol." It was almost a whisper. "Room 7. Yes. Yes. How are you? Are you well? Your feet—" The voice stopped, abruptly, as if a hand had been put over it. "Are you well, mol?"

"I'm well. Sister, I'm trying to understand what happened on the last night. The fourteenth. Do you remember—"

"No." Very quickly. "I don't remember. It was a long time ago, mol. I'm an old woman now. I have sugar, I have pressure, I don't remember last week." A breath. "There was a power cut. It was short. Everybody went to bed. In the morning the girl had gone. That's all."

"Sister, you just asked about my feet."

Silence.

"I'm sorry," said Annamma Varghese, and her voice cracked. "I'm sorry, mol. I don't remember. I wrote everything in my little book in those days. Every night, everything, in my own book as well as the register. Ira madam took the register. But the book…" She stopped. "I don't know where it is now. My son cleared the house when we moved. I don't know. I'm sorry. Don't call this number again."

The line went dead.

Meera sat on the edge of the homestay bed with the phone in her hand and looked at the wall for a long time. *Your feet.* She looked down at them, bare on the cold cement floor. The white threads on the soles.

Then she wrote in her notebook, under *Annamma (phone)*: *Asked "Your feet?" before anything else. Then "I don't remember." Kept her own book. "Ira madam took the register."*

Ritika Sharma lived in Toronto now, and worked night shifts in a hospital pharmacy, and at three in the morning her time, which was twelve-thirty in Dunda, Meera's phone began to buzz with voice notes. Fourteen of them, one after another, each about a minute long, in a breathless mixture of English and Hindi and occasional Punjabi.

Farhan Ali's face filled the phone screen propped against the homestay's sugar bowl: thirty-three, round, bearded, with a hairnet over his hair and flour on one cheek. Behind him, steel racks of bread and the orange glow of an oven. A bakery in Okhla, he said, his own. Three employees and a van.

"Meera-di," he said. "I'm going to cry on a video call in front of my staff, so give me one second." He turned away from the camera. When he turned back, his eyes were red and he was grinning. "You look the same. You look like someone who's going to tell me I'm catastrophising."

"You're catastrophising," said Meera, and he laughed.

But when she asked about the last night, the laugh went out of his face completely.

"I don't remember it," he said. "That's the truth, didi. Not the power cut, not the photo, not anything. Ritika says I was there. If she says so. I remember dinner on Saturday and then I remember Sister waking me up on Sunday saying Gauraiya's gone." He leaned closer to the camera, and dropped his voice so the bakery would not hear. "You want to know why I don't remember? Because they gave me a pill every night, and I took it like a good boy. *For sleep, beta.*"

"I know you did. We all did. Some of us." He shook his head. "I didn't know anything was wrong for weeks. "That's what she said. *They write it down and they don't tell you.*" He shrugged, a big helpless movement of the shoulders. "So I stopped. They drove down to Uttarkashi at noon to meet Tenzin Dolma in the canteen of the district hospital, where she worked as a staff nurse on the women's ward.

She came down in her white uniform with her round glasses pushed up into her hair, a small, neat, composed woman of thirty-four with a steel watch on her wrist, and sat opposite them at a corner table under a poster about hand-washing. She ordered tea for all three of them without asking. She looked at Meera for a long moment before she said anything.

"You were very sick then," she said finally. "I didn't understand how sick, at the time. I do now. I see it on the ward."

"I'd like to ask about the last night," said Meera.

"Yes. Kabir-ji said." Tenzin turned her steel cup slowly on the table. She had the kind of precision Meera recognised from good nurses: she would not say anything she was not sure of. "The power went at about eleven. I was awake; I was reading by torch. Sister came round with the lantern and took us all to the common room. We sat. Gauraiya sang. There was a photo." She glanced at the envelope in Meera's hands. "That one, I suppose."

"How long were you in the common room?"

"Long. Half an hour, more. Long enough that the lantern was running out." Tenzin frowned. "People say the cut was short. It wasn't short. When Sister took us back to our rooms it was still dark, all of it. The generator hadn't come on. I lay down in my room, and the lights came on by themselves—I'd left the switch on—and I looked at my watch." She touched the steel watch on her wrist, the same one or one like it. "Eleven forty-eight. I remember thinking: forty-five minutes, and in the morning everyone will say it was a minute."

Meera was very still.

"Why did you think that?"

"Because that's what people do." Tenzin met her eyes. "In the morning there was a statement to sign that said ten minutes. Ira ma'am's office typed it. I signed it. I was twenty-seven and I wanted to go home." She looked down. "I've thought about that signature many times."

"When Sister took you back to your rooms," Meera said, "did you see Gauraiya?"

"Yes. We went down the corridor in a line with the lantern. You were in front of me, very slow. Gauraiya had your arm. She took you into your room, the last one, and she came out again after a minute and went into hers. Six. That was the last time I saw her." Tenzin paused. "She looked at me when she came out of your room. She put her finger on her lips. Like, *shh, she's asleep.* Then she went into her room and shut the door."

Meera made herself breathe slowly.

"And afterwards? In the night?"

"I slept. I never took anything at night. They offered me a tablet in the first week and I said no; I'm a nurse, I don't like things I can't account for." Tenzin hesitated, for the first time. "I think I heard a door bang, once. At the end of the corridor. But there was wind, and the old building, and doors banged all night in the monsoon. I didn't get up."

"What time?"

"I don't know. I'd fallen asleep. Before the lights came back, I think. Or after. I don't know." She shook her head, annoyed with herself. "I'm sorry. I don't say things I'm not sure of."

"That's all right," said Meera. "That's exactly right."

Tenzin drank her tea. The canteen around them was loud with lunch: ward boys and relatives and a doctor eating standing up. A woman at the next table was crying quietly into her dupatta, and nobody was looking at her, which was the kindest thing anyone in a hospital canteen can do.

"Ira ma'am talked to us that evening," Tenzin said. "In the common room. She said Gauraiya had wanted to leave, that she'd told her so. She said we must respect that. She said a lot of things." She put down her cup. "I've thought about it for seven years, Meera. I've looked at it from every side. People go. Patients go. I've had women leave my ward in the night against advice, with the drip still in their arm. Some people can't bear to be kept." She looked out of the canteen window, at the grey hospital wall and the green mountain above it. "Gauraiya apne aasmaan mein ud gayi."

She flew off into her own sky.

Meera sat very still in the loud canteen with her hands round her steel cup, and the tea going cold.

Two women. Two days. A schoolteacher in a staff room and a nurse in a hospital canteen, twenty kilometres and seven years apart, who had not spoken to each other, Kabir said, since 2019. Neither of them had been quoting. Each of them had said it as her own, in her own voice, as the thing she had finally come to believe.

The same sentence. The same words in the same order, with the same small poetic lift at the end, which neither a schoolteacher nor a nurse would ever have chosen for herself.

And Meera had heard it twice before. At a railway station in the rain, with her hands held. And in a study on the Rajpur Road, two days ago, from a woman sitting with her back to the window.

*Gauraiya apne aasmaan mein ud gayi.*

Not a memory, then. Not three people remembering the same thing.

Three people remembering the same sentence.
