"You'll have to interview me," Meera said. "I can't do it to myself. Nobody can."

It was Sunday, a little after seven in the evening, and they were sitting on the floor of Room 7.

Dev had let them in at dusk without a word, which was itself a kind of answer. The engineers were coming in the morning; on Tuesday the machines. He had brought two kerosene lanterns from his quarter, the old brass kind with glass chimneys, and set them down in the doorway of Room 7 as if he had done it before. Then he had gone back to the generator room to wait, as she had asked him to.

Rain again. Not heavy; a steady, patient mountain rain on the slate roof. Below the window, under the cloud, the river.

Meera had brought her own blanket from the homestay and spread it on the floor beside the iron bed frame, roughly where the bed had stood seven years ago. She had brought her notebook and two pens. She had taken off her shoes and socks and was sitting with her bare feet on the cold cement, because the floor had been cold and she had been barefoot, and context mattered.

Kabir sat opposite her with his back against the wall and his own notebook on his knee, closed.

"I don't know how," he said.

"Yes, you do. You've interviewed people for fifteen years. You just don't know the rules." She held up her fingers. "There are four. One: I tell you everything, even things that seem stupid or irrelevant or wrong. Don't let me edit. Two: you don't suggest anything. No *was it this*, no *did you see that*. If I stop, you say *what else*. Three: when I've told it once, you ask me to tell it again in a different order. Backwards. From the middle. Four: you ask me to tell it from somewhere else. From the doorway. From the window. From someone else's eyes."

"That's it?"

"That's the cognitive interview. It was invented for the police in America in the eighties. It's the best method anybody has for getting more out of a witness without putting anything in." She paused. "It isn't magic, Kabir. It gets you more true details. It also gets you a few more false ones. Everything I say tonight is something I *think* I remember. We test it afterwards against what we know. I'll write it in two columns." She opened the notebook and drew the line down the page, as she had in the mortuary. *Remembered* on the left. *Verified* on the right. "If it isn't on the right, it isn't true yet."

Kabir looked at the two words for a long time.

"And if I don't want to hear it?" he said quietly.

"Then you'll have to sit there and hear it anyway," said Meera. "That's the deal. That's what I'm asking you for."

He nodded once, and opened his notebook, and put the pen down on it without writing.

"All right," he said. "Close your eyes. Tell me where you are."

---

She closed her eyes.

"I'm in this room," she said. "Seven years ago. The fourteenth. It's night. I'm—"

*Don't edit.*

"I'm tired. Not normal tired. Heavy. Like my arms and legs are full of wet sand. I've had the tablet. I always had it at half past nine, with warm water, in a little paper cup. After it, there's about half an hour when I'm still me, and then it comes up like a tide." She breathed. "It's raining. There's the river. There's a fan, the ceiling fan, I always slept with it on its lowest, even when it was cold, because I liked the noise." She stopped. "That's new. I'd forgotten the fan."

"What else?"

"Gauraiya. Through the wall." The name came out of her before she could stop it, and she let it. "I can't hear her, but I know she's there. She was always there. She said she could hear me. I couldn't hear her."

Kabir's pen did not move.

"What else?"

"The fan stops." Meera frowned with her eyes closed. "It doesn't switch off, it winds down. *Rrrr*. Slower. And the little red light on the switchboard by the door goes out. And it's very dark, darker than I've ever—" She stopped. "And then the floor. The floor goes *thud*. Three times. Under the bed."

She heard Kabir breathe in and not say anything.

"I'm half asleep. Somebody knocks on the door. The room door, this one. A woman's voice. *Sab common room mein aa jao, torch lekar.* Everyone to the common room, bring your torch." She opened her eyes and looked at the doorway, at the two lanterns burning there. "Sister Annamma. I can hear her. That's real, Kabir, that's—"

"Rule one," said Kabir gently. "Just tell it. Test it later."

She closed her eyes again.

---

It came in pieces after that, and she let it.

A lantern swinging ahead in the corridor. Someone's hand under her elbow, firm, steering. The common room, the smell of kerosene, the sofa, the arm of the sofa, which was where she sat because the seats were taken and because she was so tired that sitting up straight was work. Laughter. Someone singing very badly. The flash, a white explosion that left a purple hole in her vision for a long time afterwards. A hand on her back, warm, flat, between her shoulder blades, staying there.

And the clock.

"There's a clock," she said. "On the wall over the sofa. White face. There's a crack across it, a thin line, from the edge to the middle. I'm looking at it because I can't keep my eyes open and I'm trying to. I'm looking at the crack. It goes through a number. The four."

She heard Kabir stop breathing for a moment.

"Then walking," she said. "Back down the corridor. Dark. Very dark. The lantern ahead, far ahead. My feet are bare in the slippers and the floor is cold. The hand is under my arm again. Someone saying very quietly in my ear, *chalo Rajkumari, almost there, almost there.*" Her voice did not change. She was proud of that, afterwards. "Then this room. The bed. Someone pulling the blanket up. Someone saying *so jao*. Go to sleep."

"What else?"

"Nothing." She sat with her eyes shut, listening to the rain. "Nothing. It's black. It's like a cut in a film."

"What else?"

"Nothing, Kabir." But she stayed, eyes closed, and let the nothing be there, because she had told a hundred clients to do exactly this: to sit in the dark place and not run out of it. And after a while, out of the nothing, came the other things, the five things, the ones she had carried for seven years, and they did not come in order and they did not come as a story.

Cold. Sudden and total, from the soles of her feet up.

A red door.

A hand pressed flat on glass. Wet. Water running between the fingers.

Behind the hand, letters. Red letters. She could see them, she could see their shape, and she could not read them. They were wrong somehow. They would not make words.

Three knocks. Through the floor, through her body. *Thud. Thud. Thud.*

And then light. All at once, everywhere, so bright it hurt, white tube-light flooding a cream corridor, her eyes squeezing shut against it.

"The lights came on," she said, and her voice did shake, a little, now. "I was standing in the corridor and the lights came on. All of them. All at once."

---

She opened her eyes.

The lanterns in the doorway. Kabir against the wall, with his notebook open and the pen still lying on it. He had not written a single word. His face in the lantern light was very pale.

"Now the other way," she said. "Backwards. From the lights."

He made her do it backwards, and then from the middle, beginning at the clock, and then from the doorway, as if she were standing where the lanterns stood now, watching the girl in the bed. It took an hour. Some things changed and some did not, and she made herself notice which. The fan stayed. The three thuds under the bed stayed. The hand under her elbow and the voice in her ear, *almost there*, stayed. The clock with its crack stayed. The five things stayed, but in a different order every time she told them, which was itself a kind of answer.

When it was done she sat with her back against the iron bed frame and her bare feet on the cement, and opened the notebook on her knee, and began to fill in the right-hand column.

"I need to test two things," she said. "Will you ask Dev to start the generator? Once. And give me two minutes first."

---

She went and stood at the red door in the dark.

Not outside it. She did not open it. She stood inside it, in the last two metres of the corridor, with Room 7 at her shoulder and one of Dev's lanterns on the floor behind her throwing her shadow up the cream steel. She put her hand flat on the glass of the small wired window, at head height, the way the hand in her memory was pressed. The glass was cold and dry. Through it, by the lantern's reflected light, she could see the night: the rail of the iron landing, the rain falling past it, and nothing else.

Below the hand, stuck to the inside of the glass, the old sticker. EMERGENCY EXIT — PUSH BAR TO OPEN. Red letters, faded pink at the edges. She could read every word.

She stood there and looked at her own hand and the letters under it and waited for it to be the same, and it was not. In the memory the glass was wet, and water ran between the fingers. In the memory the letters were *behind* the hand, and they would not make words. Here they were under it, and they did.

*Letters in dreams can't be read.* It was one of the oldest observations in sleep research; she taught it to second-year students. The brain asleep could make faces, voices, rooms, the smell of a grandmother's cooking, but it could not make writing hold still. Look at a page in a dream, look away, look back, and the words are different, or not words at all. Perhaps that was all this was. A real door, a real sticker, half-seen in a drugged state, and a sleeping brain that could not make the letters hold.

Perhaps.

She wrote in the notebook, by lantern light, against the door: *Hand on glass — this window? The sticker? Here I can read it. In the memory I can't. Dream-logic? Darkness? Not verified.*

Then she called down the corridor, "Kabir. Tell him now."

She heard Kabir's phone. She heard Dev, distantly, through it. And she stood with her palm flat against the cold glass of the red door, and closed her eyes, and waited.

*Thud.*

Through the floor, through the soles of her bare feet, into her knees.

*Thud.*

Her hand on the glass. Her heart, much too fast.

*Thud.*

And on the third, the roar catching and swelling below, and the whole east end of the building beginning to hum, and Meera Sanyal standing at the door at the end of the corridor with her eyes shut and her hand on the glass, while something rose up in her from very far down, from under seven years, and broke the surface for a second like a fish.

Not a picture. A voice.

Close. Urgent. Low. Through the rain, or through glass, or through a wall; she could not tell which, and she would never be able to. A voice she had last heard on a cassette five nights ago, saying her name.

*Meera. Meera, sun.*

Listen.

*Unhe ye galat tareeke se yaad mat karwane dena.*

Don't let them make you remember this the wrong way.

She opened her eyes. The corridor was dark except for the lantern on the floor and the faint hum of the generator through the walls. Her hand was still on the glass. Her face was wet.

At the other end of the corridor, Kabir had lowered his phone and was standing quite still in the lantern light, looking at her.

"What is it?" he said. "What did you remember?"

Meera took her hand off the glass. She looked at the notebook in her other hand, at the two columns, at the right-hand one where every true thing had to be written before it could count.

She did not know where the voice belonged. She did not know whether it had been said through this glass, or through the wall between Room 6 and Room 7, or on the terrace in the sun in August, or never. She knew how memory worked. She knew that a voice heard on a tape five nights ago could put itself into a memory of seven years ago and feel, from the inside, exactly as if it had always been there. She knew she had wanted, more than anything, for Ananya to have said something to her at the end.

She wrote it down anyway. On the left-hand side, where it belonged.

*Remembered: Her voice. "Meera, sun. Unhe ye galat tareeke se yaad mat karwane dena." Don't let them make you remember this the wrong way.*

On the right-hand side she did not write anything at all.
