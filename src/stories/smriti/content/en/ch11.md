She walked down the road from the homestay towards Dunda in the cloud, past the shrine with its bells, past a woman driving three cows and a boy on a scooter with a gas cylinder strapped behind him, and did not see any of them.

She had spent five days doing the thing she was good at. Testing every memory she had against the records. Putting every recollection in the left-hand column and refusing to move it to the right until something independent, a log, a register, a stranger's watch, said it was so. She had been careful. She had been rigorous. She had been proud of how rigorous she had been.

And all that time a man had walked beside her with a torch, deciding which way to point it.

The door he had gone straight to. The slipper he had asked about. The archive he had wanted for seven years and she had opened for him. The interview in Room 7, with his pen lying unused on his notebook. Every one of those things had been real; she had seen them with her own eyes, found them with her own hands. But who had led her to the place where she found them? Had she gone to the red door because her memory took her there, or because he had walked to it first? Had she remembered Ananya's voice at the glass because it was there, under seven years, or because a man who had read those words every night for seven years had stood at the end of the corridor and willed her to?

She stopped at the bend in the road where the shrine was, and stood among the hanging bells with the cloud wet on her face.

She had tested everything. She had tested the clock and the knocks and the door and the corridor and the light. She had tested the nurse and the caretaker and the schoolteacher and the photograph.

She had never once thought to test the man holding the torch.
