She did not know how long she sat there.

At some point Dev got up and lit the gas ring and made tea in a dented pan, with too much sugar, the way you make tea for someone who has had a shock, and put a steel glass in her hands and closed her fingers round it. At some point Kabir sat down on the floor of the quarter with his back against the door and his notebook face down beside him.

And at some point, without wanting to and without being able to stop it, Meera saw it.

Not remembered. She knew the difference; she taught the difference. She saw it the way you see a thing that has been described to you many times: built, assembled, put together out of other people's words and her own fragments and the iron and the stone she had touched with her own hands. She saw a corridor in the dark, and a red door standing half open on its landing, and rain blowing in. She saw her own hand on the cold wet glass. She saw, beyond the glass, out on the cliff path in the rain, a small light going away from her. Going east. Going away and away, small and bobbing, the way a torch goes when someone is walking fast with it on a narrow path. She saw the light swing and spin and go out. And she saw herself, in her white nightdress, with her hair loose on her shoulders, pull the door shut on the dark.

It felt like a memory. It had the weight of one, and the cold of one. It had the glass under her palm and the rain on her face and the light going out.

*That's how it happens,* she thought, very clearly, from somewhere a long way above herself. *That's exactly how it happens. You hear the story and you put yourself into it, and it feels like remembering.* She had said it to a hundred clients. She had said it to Kabir in the car. She had said it to Neelam, almost, in her head, in the staff room in Uttarkashi. *It isn't lying. It happens to everyone.*

She knew all that. And it did not matter, because every piece of it was on the right-hand side of her notebook. The torch, the crash, the door, the hand on the glass, the lock, the slipper. Not one of them came from her alone. It was the most thoroughly verified thing she had ever believed.

And Ananya. The yellow raincoat zipped to her chin over the elephant pyjamas. Dressed to go out. *Going somewhere? Maybe.*

Meera looked at her face.

She had looked at it a hundred times, and every time she had looked at it as you look at the face of someone you have lost: for itself, the shape of it, the crooked smile, the enormous eyes. She had not looked at where the eyes were looking.

Everyone else in the photograph was looking at the camera. Gaurav, unsmiling, straight into the lens. Farhan, pulling his face at it. Ritika, mid-shout, *SMRITI*. Neelam and Tenzin, laughing at it. Even Meera herself, drugged and swaying, had managed to lift her heavy eyes towards the flash.

Ananya had not.

Her head was turned a little, as Meera had noticed on the first night and thought nothing of, *as if she had missed the moment*. But she had not missed anything. Her eyes were not on the camera at all. They were turned sideways and slightly down, steady and wide awake, on the girl beside her on the arm of the sofa.

On Meera.

Not smiling at her, the way you smile at a friend in a photograph. Watching her. With a kind of fixed, quiet attention, at the exact moment the flash went off and every other person in the room was looking somewhere else. As if the camera did not matter. As if there was only one thing in the room worth keeping her eyes on, and it was not the lens, and it was not the future she was supposedly about to walk out into.

Fourteen minutes before Meera closed the door, Ananya Rao had been looking at her like that.
