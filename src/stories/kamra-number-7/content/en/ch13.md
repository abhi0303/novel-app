The State Forensic Science Laboratory, Dehradun: an old government building with a monkey sitting on the gate, eyeing everyone who came and went as though they too had a file pending.

The toxicology lab was on the second floor. Blue light, steel tables, glass tubes, and a machine that hummed quietly and drew mountain ranges on a screen.

Beside Dr. Anita Joshi stood a thin man in glasses: Dr. Mehra, the senior toxicologist. His voice was so quiet and so precise that he seemed to weigh every word before letting it out.

"I'll be direct, Inspector," he said. "A cardiac glycoside was found in Mr. Sethi's blood. A heart medicine. The same family he'd been prescribed."

"How much?"

"Many times more than there should be. A lethal level." Dr. Mehra held out a printout. Kabir saw a long table of numbers and one line circled in red pen. "In the stomach as well. In the vomit as well. And..." He pointed to the evidence bags. "In the teapot. In the cup. Both."

Kabir held his breath. "In the tea."

"In the tea. The medicine is dissolved in the tea left in the pot. That means it wasn't added to the cup afterwards. It was put into the pot, or into the tea in the pot, before it was poured."

Dev looked at the printout. "How does it work? In plain words."

"At a dose that large?" Dr. Mehra adjusted his glasses. "First nausea. Vomiting. Dizziness. Weakness. The person thinks it's acidity, or tiredness. Many people start seeing things strangely: the light looks dim, or the colours look wrong, yellowish or greenish. Then the heartbeat goes wrong. Sometimes fast, sometimes slow, sometimes with no rhythm at all. And in a weak heart..." He made a gesture with his hand, like something dropping.

"How long does it take?"

"It depends on the dose, on the person, on what's in the stomach. I won't give you minutes. But at this dose, vomiting and dizziness can start within half an hour of drinking it. And at around an hour, the heart rhythm can become dangerous."

"So if Vikram drank the tea after 10:05..."

"Then by half past ten he wouldn't have been feeling well. And by around eleven..." Dr. Mehra left the sentence unfinished.

Anita, who had been silent until now, spoke. "It matches what we saw in the body, Rathore. No clot. The rhythm broke. Vomiting. And death between half past ten and a quarter past eleven."

As they left the lab, a man was waiting for Dev at the gate. Sixty, black coat, white hair, an old leather briefcase in his hand. Advocate R.K. Sharma. Vikram Sethi's lawyer for twenty-five years.

"Inspector Rathore." He shook hands. "I think I should have come to see you. Before now."

They sat at a tea stall nearby. Sharma-ji put two spoons of sugar in his tea and stirred it for a very long time.

"On the tenth of October Vikram-ji phoned me," he said. "He said he wanted to change his will. On the fifteenth, at Silver Pine. I was supposed to come up that morning. If it hadn't been for the landslide, I'd have been there. And he..." He put the spoon down. "Well."

"What did he want to change?"

"He said, 'Sharma-ji, I need to add a new heir.'"

Kabir's cup stopped in mid-air.

"Who?" asked Dev.

"He didn't say. I asked. He said, 'Come up, I'll tell you myself. This isn't something for the phone.' In twenty-five years I'd never heard Vikram-ji so..." Sharma-ji searched for the right word. "...so shaken."

"What about Meera's share?"

"He said Meera-ji's share would stay exactly as it is. It wouldn't go down. But a large part of the money from selling the hotel would go to this new person. Some property as well."

"How large?"

"He said, 'More than I could have given thirty years ago.'" Sharma-ji shrugged. "I don't know what he meant by that."

On the road back to Mussoorie, Kabir wrote in his notebook so hard that the page almost tore.

"A new heir! Who could it be, sir? Arjun? No, Arjun was busy stealing from him. A mistress? An illegitimate child? Thirty years ago... 'More than I could have given thirty years ago.' So something happened thirty years ago."

"Hmm."

"And Meera. Meera was afraid. She phoned the lawyer. She thought there was another woman and her share would go. She hid that from us. Sir, the motive is completely clear now. On the fifteenth the will would change. On the night of the fourteenth, Vikram dies."

"And the poison?"

"The doctor." Kabir tapped the notebook with his pen. "The doctor had the medicine. Meera had the reason."

Dev said nothing. Outside, the hills were shining in sunlight, for the first time this season.

Meera was called to the library that evening. Dev put Sharma-ji's account to her directly.

Meera closed her eyes. For a moment. Then she opened them and said, "Yes. I phoned him."

"Why?"

"Because Vikram told me he would change the will on the fifteenth. That's all. I asked who for, and he said, 'You'll find out.' That was his way. Eight years of 'you'll find out'." There was no mask in her voice now. "I thought there was a woman. Someone new. I wanted to know."

"And you didn't tell us," said Kabir.

"Because you would think exactly what you're thinking now."

"What are we thinking?" asked Dev.

Meera looked him straight in the eye.

"That a wife who was afraid of being left with nothing poisoned her husband." She said every word clearly. "I didn't, Inspector. I must have prayed for him to die a good many times in eight years. But I didn't poison him."

She got up and left. This time she didn't pause at the door.
