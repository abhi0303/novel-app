On the night of 14 October, the rain in Mussoorie was different.

It wasn't the kind that drifts over the hills in the evening and moves on. It fell without a pause, as if the sky had decided to settle an old account that very night. Fog had swallowed the roads. Deodar branches thrashed against each other in the wind. And beyond Camel's Back Road, on a lonely slope, the old Silver Pine Hotel stood in the dark like a half-forgotten memory.

The hotel was a relic of British days. Its wooden stairs complained at every step, and the corridors still smelled faintly of old carpet. Late October is the off-season, so that night there were only a handful of people in the building: the owner, Vikram Sethi; his wife, Meera; the family doctor, Sameer Kapoor; and, in Room 10, an elderly couple named Negi who came every year at this time. Vikram's business partner, Arjun Malhotra, had already left after the evening meeting.

Neelam, the night receptionist, was alone at the front desk.

She had worked there for three years. In that time she had learned that the nights in an old hotel are made of sounds: wood creaking, pipes knocking, wind butting its head against the windows. A little earlier the generator had growled into life somewhere in the basement and then gone quiet again a few minutes later. The lights had flickered two or three times. On a stormy night in the hills, that was nothing unusual.

At around 10:54 the manager, Rohan Malhotra, crossed the lobby. His hair was wet and the shoulders of his maroon blazer had gone dark with rain.

"Bahadur dada!" he called towards the gate. "Come upstairs a minute. Sir isn't answering his phone in Number Seven."

Bahadur, the night guard, came in shaking out his raincoat, and the two of them went up the stairs. Neelam didn't think much of it. Vikram Sethi not answering his phone was hardly news. He often lost himself in work, and when he did, the rest of the world could wait.

She went back to her register.

At 11:07 the telephone on the reception desk rang.

Neelam picked it up. "Silver Pine Hotel, good evening."

For a few seconds nobody spoke. There was only a strange hiss on the line, like rain drumming on a tin roof somewhere far away. Then a man's voice came, frightened:

"Room number seven... go quickly."

"Sorry? Who's speaking?"

The line went dead.

Neelam stood there for a moment with the receiver in her hand. Something in that voice unsettled her. It wasn't the voice of someone playing a joke. It was the voice of someone running from something.

A drunk guest, she told herself. A prank. She had only just put the receiver down when a scream came from upstairs.

It came from the second floor: long, and breaking apart at the end. A woman's scream.

Neelam's hand went to the brass bell on the counter, as if a bell could fix anything right now.

A minute later there were fast footsteps on the stairs. Rohan Malhotra came running down. His face had gone white. His shoulders were still wet, and when he stopped at the counter Neelam caught a faint smell of diesel, the same smell that always drifted up from the basement.

"Neelam... call the police. An ambulance too." He was out of breath. "In Room Seven, Vikram sir... he's—"

He couldn't finish.

"Someone just phoned," Neelam said quietly. "A man. He said to go to Room Seven quickly."

Rohan looked at her as if she had spoken a foreign language.

"Who?"

"I don't know. He didn't give a name."

Rohan was silent for a moment. Then he picked up the receiver himself and began dialling the Mussoorie police station. His fingers were shaking.

---

The hotel's owner, the well-known businessman Vikram Sethi, had been found dead in his room.

The door had been locked from the inside. The old brass bolt was across, and it had taken two men throwing their shoulders against the door to break it open.

The window was shut, its latch fastened from the inside.

And on the desk lay a brass clock, knocked onto its face, its glass cracked. Its hands had stopped at 10:15.

Down below, the storm went on pounding the roads of Mussoorie. Beyond Kolhukhet a piece of the hillside slid onto the road, and the way to Dehradun was closed for the night. Everyone who was inside Silver Pine that night would stay there until morning, with a dead man and with one another.

Nobody knew how the murder had been done.

And nobody knew yet that the real secret lay not in Vikram's death, but in a life he had left behind a long time ago.
