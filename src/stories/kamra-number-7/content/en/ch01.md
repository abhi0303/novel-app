At 5:30 in the morning the debris was cleared from the road near Kolhukhet, and at 6:40 the white jeep of the Dehradun Crime Branch pulled up at the gate of the Silver Pine Hotel.

The rain had stopped, but the hills were still dripping. The fog was so thick that the building surfaced slowly, like an old photograph in a developing tray: first the roof, then the chimneys, then the row of windows.

"Sir, I swear," said Kabir Ansari, climbing out and winding his muffler twice around his neck, "if Lucknow got this cold in October, we'd give up our famous manners and just wear blankets."

Inspector Dev Rathore didn't answer. He took a cardamom pod from his pocket, bit down on it, and kept looking at the gate.

Dev was forty-two. He spoke little, but when he did, people remembered it. In the Dehradun Crime Branch he was known as the man who listened more to people's silences than to their answers. He liked to say:

"A liar doesn't always say something false. Sometimes he's only hiding something true."

Kabir was his opposite: twenty-nine, quick in the head and quicker with his tongue. On every case he came up with three theories in the first hour and knocked down two of them himself in the second. Two years ago Dev had saved him from an unjust suspension, and Kabir had been at his side ever since. Sometimes he was a disciple, sometimes a partner, and sometimes the younger brother who never misses a chance to prove the older one wrong.

At the gate stood SI Rawat from the Mussoorie police station, in a thick jacket, his eyes red. He hadn't slept all night.

"Jai Hind, sir." He saluted. "The call came from the hotel at 11:12 last night. The manager, Rohan Malhotra, made it. I got here by 11:40. The road below was blocked, but Mussoorie is above the slide. I sealed the room. Nobody has touched the body, except that the doctor tried CPR before we arrived. Everyone has been kept inside the hotel."

"Who's everyone?" Dev asked.

Rawat checked his notebook. "The wife, Meera Sethi. The family doctor, Sameer Kapoor. Rohan the manager. Staff: Neelam at reception, Mohan the waiter, Bahadur the guard, Thapa the cook. And an elderly couple in Room 10, Mr. and Mrs. Negi. They didn't know a thing, slept right through it."

"And the partner?"

"Arjun Malhotra. Business partner. Says he left at a quarter to ten. Got stuck behind the landslide at a dhaba above Kolhukhet. He rang this morning and said he's coming back up. He'll be here in half an hour."

Dev nodded.

Rawat hesitated. "Sir, can I say something? To me it looks like a plain heart attack. Vikram-ji had a heart condition, the doctor says so himself. It's just that phone call, and the broken clock..."

"Just?" said Dev.

Rawat said nothing more.

The lobby had a strange chill to it. Last night's ashes lay cold in the fireplace. On the walls, stag antlers and faded photographs of British officers looked down as if they had seen everything and meant to say nothing.

The staff had been put in the library. Meera Sethi was in her room with a woman constable. Dr. Kapoor sat alone in the dining room in front of a cold cup of tea.

Dev stood in the middle of the lobby and asked his question straight out.

"Who discovered the body?"

A man stepped forward and raised his hand.

"I did, sir. Rohan Malhotra. Hotel manager."

He was thirty or thirty-one. Neat, quiet, with a small brass name badge on his maroon blazer. Last night's rain had left marks on the blazer that still showed. His eyes were red, and his voice carried the kind of tiredness that comes from more than lost sleep. It comes from having seen something.

"Tell me," said Dev. "From the beginning."

"Yes, sir. Last night I was in the basement checking the generator. There was a storm and the power kept going. At about 10:52 I called Vikram sir's room on the basement intercom. He'd asked for breakfast at eight for the board, and I wanted to confirm it. Nobody picked up."

"And then?"

"Sir, Vikram sir often didn't answer when he was working. But that night... I don't know, it didn't feel right. I went up. On the way I took Bahadur dada from the gate. We reached Number Seven around 10:55. I knocked and called out. No answer. I opened the lock with the master key, but the door wouldn't move. The safety latch was on inside, sir. That old brass bolt."

"And then?"

"Then we broke the door down. It took three or four goes with our shoulders. Inside, Vikram sir was lying on the floor by the desk. I sent Bahadur to Room Nine for the doctor. The doctor came and did CPR, but..." Rohan lowered his eyes. "He said sir was gone."

Kabir had been writing fast in his notebook. Now he looked up.

"One minute. The phone call came at 11:07. So did you see the body before the call, or after it?"

Rohan paused.

Only for a moment. But Dev saw that moment.

"I don't know anything about the call, sir. I'd already gone upstairs. Neelam told me about it later, when I came down."

"So when the call came, you were in Room Seven? With the body?"

"Yes. The doctor was doing CPR. I was right there."

"And do you know who made the call?"

"No, sir." Rohan looked Kabir straight in the eye. "I honestly don't."

Dev looked at him, but said nothing.

The second-floor corridor was long and dim. The red carpet was worn thin down the middle. The brass wall lamps were still on, because the morning light didn't reach that far. On one side were the rooms: Five, Six, Seven. Then the glass-panelled door of the service staircase. Then Eight, Nine, Ten. On the other side ran tall windows, rain still clinging to the glass.

Police tape made a cross over the door of Room Number 7.

Dev stopped at the door.

Kabir knew what came next: Sir's "one minute". At every crime scene Dev spent one full minute looking, without touching anything and without saying a word. Kabir had once asked what he looked at during that minute. Dev had said, "Whatever changes later."

The minute ended. Dev pulled on gloves and shoe covers and went in.

The room was large and old-fashioned. On one side was the bed, its sheet perfectly smooth, as if nobody had slept in it at all. A fireplace in the wall hadn't been lit in years. By the window stood a heavy mahogany desk, and beside the desk, on the floor, lay Vikram Sethi.

He was on his back; the doctor's CPR had turned him over. A grey kurta, a brown shawl half slipped from his shoulders. His face was calm. Almost calm. At the corner of his mouth was a dried, whitish line.

There was no obvious sign of a struggle. No broken chair, no scattered belongings.

On the desk were a cup of tea, a pen, some papers, and a brass clock lying on its face.

"Signs of forced entry on the door?" Dev asked.

Rohan stood in the doorway on the far side of the tape. "Only from when we broke it, sir."

"The window?"

"It was shut."

Kabir went to the window. It was an old sash window, latched from the inside. He looked down through the glass. Below was wet earth and a narrow path through the hotel garden. Last night's rain had washed everything clean.

"Sir, there are no footprints here."

Dev looked around the room once more.

"Either the killer didn't leave this way... or we're looking for footprints in the wrong place."

Kabir raised his eyebrows. "Killer? Sir, we don't even know yet that it's murder."

"That's why we're looking."

Dev knelt a hand's breadth from the body. He touched nothing. He only looked.

Vikram's left hand lay open on the floor. But the right was clenched into a fist, so tightly that the knuckles had gone white. And between two fingers something white was showing: the corner of a small piece of paper.

"Kabir."

"Sir?"

"Tell the forensic team that nobody opens this hand. Not here and not in the ambulance. Whatever is in this fist comes out on the post-mortem table, and gets photographed."

Kabir already had his phone out.

Dev stood and looked at the door again. A broken frame. A bolt fastened from the inside. A shut window. And a man who had died in his own room, beside his own desk, holding on to something he wouldn't let go of even in death.

He didn't pick up anything from the desk himself.

The case had begun.
