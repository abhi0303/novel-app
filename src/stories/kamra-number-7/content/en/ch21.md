On the morning of the ninth day, the rain came back.

Dev called everyone to the hotel's private dining room: the same room where dinner had been served at eight on the night of the fourteenth. Where Vikram had announced the sale of the hotel, and Arjun had said "You can't do this", and Vikram had said "Watch me".

Rain had started falling again on the tall arched windows. Outside, the hills had vanished into the fog. Inside, the old chandelier hanging from the ceiling was lit, and on the long mahogany table Thapa, without being asked, had lit candles. "You can't trust the power, sahab," he had said.

@scene dining-room | The dining room, the ninth day. A table, four suspects, and a man standing at the window.

On one side of the table sat Meera and Dr. Kapoor, with an empty chair between them, as if even sitting together had become a sin. On the other side sat Arjun, whom Rawat had brought up from the lock-up at the police station. His handcuffs had been removed, but Rawat stood behind his chair. And next to Arjun sat Rohan, in his maroon blazer, hands on the table, fingers laced together.

The staff stood along the wall: Neelam, Mohan, Bahadur, Pooja, Thapa. Bahadur's back was as straight as ever. Mohan's hands were shaking.

Kabir laid files on the table, one by one. Then he looked at Dev. Dev gave a small nod, and Kabir slipped quietly out of the dining room with a constable. Nobody paid it much attention.

Dev stood in front of the window. In the dim light from behind him, half his face was in shadow.

"After Vikram Sethi died," he said, "each of you told us your own story. Every story had a gap in it. We looked at those gaps one at a time. But to understand the murder, we had to put them all together. Today I'm going to tell you the whole story. From the beginning. And where I'm wrong, stop me."

Nobody said a word.

---

"First, the clock," said Dev. "The brass clock lying face down on the desk, stopped at 10:15. For three days we built our whole investigation on that 10:15. But Pooja told us the clock had been stopped since the morning of the fourteenth. The battery was dead. When Payal put in a new battery, the clock started again from exactly where it had stopped." He looked at Pooja, and Pooja lowered her eyes. "That clock didn't stop that night. It only fell over. And all of us took its hands for the time of death."

He looked at Arjun.

"Arjun Malhotra. You hid the fact that you came back to the hotel. You had a business motive too, worth ₹4.2 crore. Vikram had found out from the audit. At 9:54 he called you back. At 10:12 he sent you a message himself: 'If you don't come and see me by 10:30, I'll tell everyone the truth.' That message wasn't faked. Vikram wrote it himself. You went to his room at 10:28. You stayed eight minutes. You saw him ill, sweating, vomiting, saying 'everything looks yellow'. And you left."

Arjun lowered his eyes.

"You went down the service staircase, out of the side gate, and drove away at 10:41. And as you stepped out of the door, you heard the bolt slide shut on the inside. At 10:36. Vikram bolted the door himself. Alive." Dev paused. "And the phone call at 11:07, from the pump at Kolhukhet, was yours. 'Room number seven, go quickly.' Without a name."

Neelam put a hand over her mouth.

"You're a thief, Arjun. And you ran away from a man who was ill. But it wasn't enough to prove that you were angry with Vikram. You didn't poison him. By the time you reached his room, the poison was already doing its work."

Arjun said nothing. Only his eyes closed.

---

Dev turned to Meera.

"Mrs. Sethi. You didn't tell us the whole truth about where you went. At 10:20 you were in the corridor, not going to the pantry but to Room Nine. Your earring was found there. You phoned the lawyer to ask about the will, because you were afraid. You had a motive." Dev looked into her eyes. "But your being in the corridor was not, in itself, proof of murder. The poison was in the tea before 10:05. At that time you were in your room. You had neither that poison nor that opportunity."

Meera took a long breath and slowly nodded. For a moment Dr. Kapoor's hand moved towards her, then stopped, and came back to rest on the table.

"Dr. Kapoor." Dev's voice was gentle, but there was no mercy in it. "Your timeline contradicted itself. You said 9:50, but you went at 10:22. It was you who went out at 10:14 with the red umbrella, to fetch Vikram's medicine from your car. Your medical kit became part of the investigation. So did your medicine. But your bottle was sealed. Thirty out of thirty. And the poison in Vikram's stomach came from pink-coated tablets made by a company that closed down two years ago. Not yours."

The doctor bowed his head.

"Your crime is that you were in that room at 10:22. You heard Vikram say he was seeing yellow, heard him complain the tea was bitter, and you left. You saw it and you didn't recognise it. That will eat at you for the rest of your life." Dev paused. "But a doctor visiting his patient does not, without any other evidence, prove murder."

Then Dev turned to Rohan.

"Rohan Malhotra. You had the master key. You hid Arjun's visit. You discovered the body. Your alibi had to be checked." He spoke without any change of tone. "But it would have been wrong to treat access and opportunity as guilt."

Rohan inclined his head slightly. "Thank you, sir."

---

Standing by the wall, Rawat asked quietly, as if voicing what everyone was thinking, "Sir, then who is the real killer?"

Dev closed a file.

"Motive alone won't give us the answer. We have to match the method of the murder, the actual time of death, the authorship of the message and the physical evidence against each other. So listen."

He came to the table and laid a blank sheet among the candles. On it, a line. A timeline.

"Method: poison. A heart medicine, in a lethal dose. Dissolved in the teapot. Pink-coated tablets."

"The locked room: no trick. Vikram slid the bolt himself, at 10:36, when Arjun left. After that nobody went in until the door was broken at 10:58. The killer never needed to enter the room. The poison went in with the tea."

"Time: at 10:22 Dr. Kapoor found Vikram sweating and seeing yellow. Anita Joshi says that at this dose those signs come ten or fifteen minutes after drinking it. So Vikram drank the poison before 10:10. The tea arrived at 10:05. So the poison was already in the tea when it reached the door."

He slid his finger backwards along the line.

"So the question isn't who went into Room Seven. The question is how the tea got to Room Seven."

Every eye went to Mohan. Mohan pressed himself back against the wall.

"Not Mohan," Dev said at once. "Mohan picked up the tea from the kitchen. He stopped in the pantry on the first floor, off the service staircase. There he was told the sugar-free had run out and to fetch some from the store. He went down. We timed it: one minute, five seconds. And for that one minute, the tray sat alone in the pantry." Dev looked at Meera. "Sugar-free, for a man who, in his wife's words, never took sugar-free. The sachet was found on the tray unopened. It was no use to anyone. Except for that one minute."

A silence fell over the room so deep that you could hear the faint hiss of the candle flames.

"Before that one minute," said Dev, "there were two people in the pantry."

He looked at the wall.

"Bahadur-ji. And Rohan."

---

Bahadur's back grew straighter still, if that were possible.

Rohan slowly unlaced his fingers and laid them flat on the table.

"Bahadur-ji," said Dev. "That night, how long were you in the pantry?"

"Saab." Bahadur's voice was heavy but clear. "Every night at ten I go to fill my thermos. That day too. I filled the thermos and left. When Mohan came with the tray, I was at the door. On my way out."

Mohan suddenly looked up.

Something had woken in his eyes: a picture that had been blurred for days and had suddenly come clear.

"Yes." His voice shook. "Yes, sir! Bahadur dada was at the door. He said to me, 'That's sahab's tea? Take it quickly, it'll get cold.' And he went. Down the stairs. After he'd gone..."

He stopped.

His eyes moved slowly, as if against his will, towards the table. Towards the chair next to Arjun.

"After he'd gone," Mohan said, almost in a whisper, "Rohan sir said, 'Put some sugar-free on for sir as well. It's run out here. Get some from the store downstairs.'"

Dev took a photograph from his file and laid it on the table in the candlelight: a page from the register that hung on the door of the ground-floor store.

"Mohan's habit," said Dev. "Whenever he takes anything from the store at night, he writes in the last column the name of whoever asked for it. It's the rule. He'd forgotten he wrote this himself. The register didn't forget."

Mohan looked at the photograph, and tears came to his eyes. "I always write it, sir. Always. I just didn't remember."

Rohan looked at the photograph. For a long time. Then he raised his head, and his voice still had the same politeness, the same composed, tidy calm it had had since the first day.

"Yes, sir. I mentioned the sugar-free. I was looking at the stock register; I thought the box was empty. Mohan went down, and I went off on my rounds. What crime is there in that, sir? Asking for one sachet?"

Dev looked into Rohan's eyes.

"So when the tray sat alone in that pantry for one minute," he said, "there was only one person in that room."

Lightning flashed outside. For an instant the arched windows went white, and Dev's shadow stretched long across the whole table.

"Rohan Malhotra." Dev's voice was very quiet. "It was you who put the poison in Vikram Sethi's tea."
