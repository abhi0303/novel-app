Arjun Malhotra came to the card room at nine that night, with his lawyer. Mr. Tandon had taken the afternoon flight from Delhi and then a taxi up the hill: an expensive suit, shining shoes, and the kind of smile that is billed by the hour.

"Inspector," said Tandon as soon as he sat down, "my client has already been extremely cooperative. If you have any new questions, I'd ask that first you..."

Dev said nothing. He simply began laying sheets of paper on the table, one by one, in the light of the lamp. Very slowly. A breath's pause between each.

The parking record. *22:03 ENTRY. 22:41 EXIT.*

A frame from Norbu's camera. The black Fortuner, dark, empty. *22:20.*

A second frame. A tall man with a black umbrella coming out of a narrow lane. *22:39.*

The BSNL record. *23:07:12 — Kandiyal Filling Station — 00:09.*

The card slip from the pump. *23:04:38 — ****7712.*

And last, the email from the bank. *Card ****7712: Arjun Malhotra.*

Arjun looked at each sheet. No expression crossed his face. Then, at the last one, his shoulders dropped an inch, as if someone inside him had cut a string.

"Mr. Malhotra, you don't need to say anything," Tandon said quickly. "I'd suggest that..."

"Enough, Tandon." Arjun raised a hand without looking at him. "Enough. That's enough."

Silence filled the room.

"I'll tell you everything," said Arjun. "Everything. But Inspector, hear one thing first. I didn't kill Vikram." He looked Dev in the eye. "I'm a thief. I'm not a murderer."

"Himgiri Constructions," said Arjun, "is my brother-in-law's company. On paper. It has no office, no workers, no work. Over two years I had it raise bills worth ₹4.2 crore. For renovations. At Silver Pine, at the Dehradun hotel. The work was never done. The money came to me."

"Why?"

"There was a real-estate project in Delhi. My own. I'd put everything into it, and it went under. The banks were after me. I thought I'd pay it back in a year or two and nobody would know." He gave a bitter laugh. "Every thief thinks that."

"And Vikram found out."

"To sell the hotel he had an audit done. I couldn't stop it. On the evening of the fourteenth, at 8:50, the accountant's email arrived. One look at his face at dinner and I knew he knew. But he didn't say anything in front of me. He only talked about selling the hotel. To make me burn."

"Then?"

"I left at 9:45. Anger, fear, all of it. At 9:54 he called. 'Come back. It's important.' I knew what it was. I came back, at 10:03. Rohan was in the lobby. I told him, 'Don't log me in. Don't tell anyone I'm here.' He said, 'Yes, sir.' That's all."

"And then you didn't go to your car."

"No." Arjun lowered his eyes. "Next to the lobby there's the old bar, which is closed in the off-season. It was dark. I sat in there. Lit a cigarette, put it out, lit another. Worked out what I'd say. Worked out how to save myself. Then at 10:12 his message came. By 10:30. I knew Vikram didn't bluff. He did exactly what he said he'd do."

"So you went upstairs."

"At 10:26. I went up the stairs. The corridor was empty. At 10:28 I knocked on the door." Arjun's voice dropped. "He opened it."

"What was it like inside?" asked Dev.

For a while Arjun didn't answer. When he did, his voice seemed to come from somewhere else.

"He sat down at his desk. I stayed standing. He didn't ask me to sit. He said, 'The police are coming in the morning. And Sharma-ji. I'm giving you one night. Call your lawyer.' I begged, Inspector. I reminded him of twelve years of friendship. Talked about paying the money back. He looked at me as if I were an insect."

"How did he look? Vikram?"

Arjun paused. "Ill." He said the word as if he had only now understood what it meant. "He was sweating. His face was white. Once, in the middle of it, he held up a hand to stop me, turned, and was sick into the wastebasket. I asked, 'Are you all right?' He said, 'Don't think about me, think about yourself.' Then he looked at the lamp and said, 'What's wrong with the light in this room? Everything looks yellow.' I thought it was the storm, the voltage was low."

Kabir looked at Dev. The doctor's words. Yellow.

"What was on the desk?"

"The audit papers. A cup of tea, half drunk. And..." Arjun narrowed his eyes. "...a handwritten page. In fountain pen. Almost full, peeking out from under the audit papers. I glanced at it once and he covered it with his hand. He said, 'This isn't for you.'"

"Did you read anything?"

"No. Not one word. It wasn't for me. He'd made that clear."

"Then?"

"Then he said, 'Go.' Just one word. I came out. It was 10:36. I looked at my watch, because I don't know why, I felt I should remember the time." Arjun put his hands on the table. They were shaking. "And as soon as the door shut behind me..."

He stopped.

"I heard the bolt."

"Then?" Dev asked quietly.

"I didn't want to go through the lobby. Rohan might be there, and Neelam. So I went down the service staircase. The glass door, next to Number Seven. Below there was a pantry, empty. Then a door behind the kitchen, the garden, and the side gate. The lane. The car. At 10:41 I drove away."

"Did you see anyone on the way?"

"No. There was nobody."

"And 11:07?"

Arjun passed a hand over his face.

"As far as the Kempty turn I was only thinking about myself. The police tomorrow, everything finished tomorrow. Then, by the time I got to Kolhukhet, something else came into my head. His sweat. The vomiting. 'Everything looks yellow.' I'd left a sick man alone. A man who might have been dying. And I ran because all I cared about was myself." His voice cracked. "I stopped at the pump. There was no network on my mobile. I called the hotel from the landline. I said, 'Room number seven, go quickly.' The girl asked who it was. I put the phone down."

"Why didn't you give your name?"

"Because if I gave my name, they'd ask why I was there. And then everything would come out." Arjun looked at Dev. "It was the smallest thing I could have done, Inspector. And I only did half of it."

Tandon had gone completely silent.

"At eight in the morning your Rawat phoned. Said Vikram was dead. I came back up, and I lied. Because if I admitted I was in that room at 10:28, you'd take me for the killer. And the truth is, maybe I was the last person who..." Arjun stopped. He glanced at Kabir's notebook, as if he knew what was written in it. "Who saw him alive for the last time."

At eleven that night SI Rawat arrested Arjun Malhotra under the sections for cheating and criminal breach of trust. Not murder. Tandon talked about bail. Arjun said nothing. On his way out he paused for a moment at the card-room door and said, without turning round:

"Inspector. Whoever did this, catch them. He was my friend. I was robbing him, but he was my friend."

---

Now only Dev and Kabir were left in the card room. Papers lay scattered under the lamp.

For a long time Kabir said nothing. Then he said slowly, like someone reciting the answer to a very old riddle to himself:

"Vikram shut the door himself. At 10:36. Alive. Ill. Alone."

"Yes."

"And the poison went in with the tea at around 10:05. He drank it. It worked slowly. The doctor came and went. Arjun came and went. Vikram bolted the door, sat down at his desk and went on with his letter. And a while later..."

"His heart stopped."

"In a room bolted from the inside." Kabir looked up. "Sir, the locked room was never a trick."

"No." Dev looked at his own hands in the lamplight. "It was a man's last habit, Kabir. Every night he slid that bolt. That night too. From the very start we've been assuming the killer had to get into the room and out again. The door, the window, the footprints, the trick with the bolt. All of it was chasing that one question. But the killer never needed to go in."

"The poison went in with the tea."

"With the tea," said Dev. "And by the time Vikram bolted his door, the killer's work was already done. He was probably sitting somewhere else at the time. With his alibi."

Kabir wrote it down, and then stopped in the middle of writing.

"But sir. One thing did leave the room. After he died. The letter. Mr. Bhatt says it was pulled out of a dead man's fist. And the room was bolted from 10:36 until the door was broken."

"10:58."

"10:58. So the letter left after 10:58. After the door was already broken." Kabir counted on his fingers. "And who went into that room after the door was broken? Rohan and Bahadur, who broke it. The doctor, who came to do CPR. Meera, who screamed at 11:08. And Rawat sahab at 11:40."

"Leave Rawat out."

"So, four people." Kabir looked at Dev. "One of those four pulled the last letter of a dead man out of his fist. Put it in a pocket and took it away. And that letter had 'your mother' in it, and '1995', and 'forgiveness'."

"And that person's hands were wet," said Dev. "With rain. And diesel."

Kabir fell silent.

Dev got up and pulled the lamp's chain. The card room sank into darkness. Only a faint light from a distant Mussoorie streetlamp came in through the window.

"That letter is still somewhere, Kabir," he said in the dark.

"How do you know? It could have been burned."

"Because whoever pulls a letter from a dead man's fist and takes it away will certainly read it." Dev's voice was very low. "And some letters, once you've read them, can't be burned."
