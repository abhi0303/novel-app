In the first week of December, for the first time, the sky over Mussoorie cleared completely.

From Lal Tibba the snow peaks came into view: Bandarpoonch, Swargarohini, and more behind them, like a white wall that someone had put up overnight along the edge of the sky. The tourists were back on Mall Road. So were the monkeys. And at the Kulri crossing, in front of a little tea stall, two men stood leaning their elbows on a railing.

Kabir held a clay cup of tea as if it were the last warm thing in the world.

"Sir," he said. "You said you'd tell me later."

Dev looked out at the distant peaks.

A great deal had happened in the past six weeks.

Arjun Malhotra was in Sudhowala jail, awaiting trial for fraud. His brother-in-law had been arrested too. Of the ₹4.2 crore, two and a half crore had been recovered from the Himgiri Constructions account. The rest was lost somewhere in that sunken Delhi project. From jail, Arjun had written Dev a short letter, just three lines: *I couldn't go to his prayer meeting. If you ever visit his memorial, don't say anything on my behalf. I'm not worthy of it.*

Dr. Sameer Kapoor had written to the Medical Council himself to tell them what he had seen at 10:22 on the night of the fourteenth of October, and what he had failed to do. The Council's inquiry was still going on. But even before it reached a decision, the doctor had closed his clinic in Dehradun. Now he sat six days a week in a charitable dispensary in a village near Raipur. For free. He didn't tell anyone why. He didn't need to.

Meera and the doctor no longer saw each other. Some relationships can only live in the dark. The moment they come into the light of the truth, they stop breathing.

Vikram's new will was never written. Under the old will, everything went to Meera. She called Sharma-ji, and her first decision was that Silver Pine would not be sold. Her second was that the hotel would close for the winter and the staff would be paid in full: Neelam, Mohan, Pooja, Thapa, Bahadur.

Her third decision was not given to the newspapers. At the government school in Dehradun where Sunita Malhotra had taught thirty years earlier, a scholarship was established: the *Smt. Sunita Malhotra Memorial Scholarship*. For ten girls every year.

Meera also went to Sudhowala once. She met Rohan. Nobody knows what passed between them. The jailer told Dev afterwards only that she sat with him for half an hour, mostly in silence. And as she left, she put an old photograph, worn at the edges, into Rohan's hand: a young man of twenty-five or twenty-six sitting on an old scooter, his hair blown by the wind, big dreams in his eyes.

Dev went to Sudhowala once too, with the court's permission, carrying a photocopy of the letter.

Rohan took the photocopy and read it. In silence. Twice. Then he folded it and laid it in his lap very carefully, as if it were a bird that might fly away.

"Sir," he said. "Do you go to Dehradun?"

"Yes. Every week."

"Behind Rajpur Road, on the bank of the Rispana, there's an old ghat. A small one. There's a peepal tree. My mother used to sit there in the evenings, after school." Rohan looked at his hands. "Next year, on the fourteenth of October, I'll probably still be in here. Would you... would you light a diya? There?"

"I will."

For a while Rohan said nothing. Then, very softly, he said:

"Two diyas, sir."

"Two?"

"One for my mother." He stopped. For a long time. His fingers were touching the edge of the photocopy, exactly where, on the real page, a strip had torn away. "And one for him. He wrote that he wanted to light one. For two years he couldn't." He looked at Dev. "Somebody ought to light it, sir."

Dev nodded.

"Two diyas," he said.

"So, sir?" Kabir drank the last of his tea. "When did you know?"

Dev took out a cardamom pod. This time he ate it.

"In the basement. On the second day. When he went to the door, stopped, came back, and without anyone asking told me he'd seen the doctor with the bottle." Dev looked out at the mountains. "Nobody had asked him, Kabir. A man who helps without being asked is sometimes only helping his own story."

"The second day?" Kabir put his cup down. "You kept quiet for eight days?"

"At that point it was only a suspicion. An odd feeling. Why would a man who was so calm, so correct, so helpful, say something nobody had asked him?" Dev shrugged. "It took a week for that suspicion to become proof. When Norbu's camera showed that the bottle was in the car until 10:15, the suspicion took a name for the first time. And when the doctor mentioned the dark blazer, the name was certain. Everything else, the generator, the clock, the diesel, the tablets, was only to prove to myself that I wasn't wrong."

"And why didn't you tell me?"

Dev looked at Kabir. On his face was the faint smile Kabir had seen perhaps three times in the last six weeks.

"If I'd told you, Kabir, you'd have gone looking for him. And whoever goes looking for someone finds exactly what he's looking for. I wanted you to look for proof. The name would come by itself." He paused. "And it did. In that shop on Rajpur Road. In your hands. Not mine."

Kabir was quiet for a while. Then he called over to the boy at the tea stall.

"Bhaiya, another tea. Plain. No sugar."

Dev raised his eyebrows.

"What?" Kabir shrugged. "Bitter tea, bitter man. I'm giving it a try. Maybe it makes your brain work faster."

"Mine with sugar, bhaiya," said Dev. "Two spoons." And for the first time in six weeks, he laughed out loud.

That same morning, at Silver Pine, Pooja unlocked the door of Room Number 7.

The hotel was closed for the winter. New wood had replaced the broken wood of the frame, and the brass keeper for the bolt was back in its place with new screws. A white sheet covered the bed. The fireplace was cold. The desk was empty.

Pooja held a small box. Payal Thapliyal had sent it the week before, with a note: *This clock was never evidence. Please put it back where it belongs.*

Pooja opened the box. The brass clock. New glass. And inside, a new battery.

She set the clock on the desk, exactly where it had stood on the morning of the fourteenth, when she had seen it stopped at 10:15 and written it in the register. Then she checked the time on her phone and turned the hands.

The clock started.

*Tick. Tick. Tick.*

Pooja closed the door, locked it, and went down the stairs.

In the empty room, by the closed window, a line of sunlight came to rest on the desk. And the clock kept going. Not for anyone. Just for itself. Counting time in a room where there was no longer anyone to keep count.

The clock was running again.

But in some rooms, once time has stopped, it never quite starts again.
