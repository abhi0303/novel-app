Vikram Sethi was not just a businessman.

He owned three hotels, in Dehradun, Delhi and Mussoorie. He held land in two cities and a stake in a construction company, and he ran a charitable trust whose board had half the town on it. The newspapers called him "Doon's self-made man". Thirty years ago he had been a small-time contractor. Today bank managers stood up when they heard his name.

There was plenty of money in his life. Trust, perhaps, much less.

The hotel library was turned into an interview room: shelves of books, a long table covered in green baize, and in the corner an old globe on which Burma was still part of the British Empire.

Before the four suspects, Dev called in Neelam.

Neelam paused in the doorway, then came and sat down with her hands folded in her lap. She hadn't slept, and it showed in her eyes, but her voice was clear.

"Start with yesterday evening," said Dev. "Whatever you remember."

"Sir, the doctor came at seven, from Dehradun. It had already started raining. At eight there was dinner in the private dining room: Vikram sir, Meera ma'am, Arjun sir and the doctor. Mohan was serving."

"How was the dinner?"

Neelam thought for a moment. "Voices were raised, sir. Mohan told me afterwards that Vikram sir said the hotel was being sold, to some company in Delhi. Arjun sir was very angry. He said, 'You can't do this, Vikram.' And Vikram sir said, 'Watch me.'"

"Then?"

"At nine Vikram sir went upstairs. On his way he told me an attachment had come on his email and he needed a printout. I printed it. Rohan sir said he would take it up himself because he had to talk to sir about tomorrow's meeting. He went up at about 9:10."

"Fifteen, twenty minutes, sir. He was down before 9:30." Neelam paused, then added, "He was very quiet. I thought sir must have scolded him. Vikram sir... he wasn't easy to work for."

For a moment Neelam's eyes dropped.

"Sir, at around ten the paper jammed in the printer in the back office. I was fixing it, and then I heated up my dinner. I was inside for maybe fifteen or twenty minutes. I... I know I shouldn't leave the desk empty."

"No, sir. He took the morning off. It was someone's barsi, a death anniversary. He went to the temple and came back in the afternoon." Neelam gave a small smile. "He's like that, sir. He never says anything about himself. Seventeen months he's been here, and to this day nobody knows who's in his family."

Four people had come to see Vikram that night. Or rather, four people had been closest to his life that night. And one by one, all four were about to sit down at the green table.

**Arjun Malhotra** arrived at eleven. He was close to fifty, in an expensive leather jacket whose hem was caked with Kolhukhet mud. His hair was combed straight back, as if even at a roadside dhaba he had found himself a mirror. Before sitting down he shook Dev's hand, but not Kabir's.

"Terrible thing," he said. "Twelve years we were together. We had our fights. Where in business don't you? But this..." He shook his head.

"Tell us about last night."

"There was a dinner. Vikram brought up the sale. I wasn't happy and I told him so. Then I left the hotel at 9:45 and headed straight for Dehradun. Past Kolhukhet the road was closed, so I spent the night in a dhaba." He glanced at his watch, as if he had more important things to do. "I didn't meet Vikram again that night."

"No phone call either?"

Arjun's answer came a beat late. "No. I mean, I don't remember. The network was bad."

Kabir looked up from his notebook. "You and the manager, Rohan, are both Malhotras. Any relation?"

Arjun gave a short, dry laugh. "Inspector, from Punjab to Doon half the people are Malhotras. No. No relation."

**Meera Sethi** came at twelve. She was forty, tall, wrapped in a white shawl. Her eyes were dry, but they were the eyes of someone who had been holding back tears for a long time. She sat very straight with her hands in her lap, as if this were a job interview and not the aftermath of a death.

"Vikram and I had been sleeping in separate rooms for a few weeks," she said at once, as though it were better to say it herself than have them hear it from someone else. "He worked late. I couldn't sleep with his light on."

"Last night?"

"After dinner I went to my room. After ten I was in my room."

"You were the one who screamed," Dev said. It wasn't a question.

"Yes." For the first time her voice shook. "I heard a noise outside, something like breaking. I came into the corridor and... the door was open. He was on the floor."

Kabir checked his notes. "The door was broken at about 10:58, ma'am. Your scream was at 11:08. Ten minutes."

Meera looked at him. "I was in the bath. Maybe that's why I heard it late."

"At eleven at night?"

"I couldn't sleep, Inspector."

**Rohan Malhotra** had given his statement in the morning, but Dev called him back. He came in the same way, upright and polite, hands clasped behind his back.

"At 9:10 yesterday evening you went to Vikram's room."

"Yes, sir. To give him the printout. Sir asked about the seating for tomorrow morning's board meeting. Who would sit where, when breakfast would be laid. It was five or seven minutes' talk."

"Neelam says fifteen or twenty."

Rohan inclined his head a little. "That may be, sir. When sir talked, he didn't look at the clock."

"And after ten?"

"At about 10:10 I went to the basement to check the generator. The power kept tripping. At 10:52 I called sir on the intercom from down there, then I went up. You know the rest."

**Dr. Sameer Kapoor** came last. Fifty-four, greying hair, gold-rimmed glasses, a grey cardigan. He held a handkerchief that he kept folding and refolding. Today he had put his kurta on the right way round. Kabir noticed.

"I've been Vikram's doctor for fifteen years," he said. "His heart was weak. He had a small attack two years ago. A bypass was being discussed and he kept putting it off. Yesterday I brought his new medicine. The dose of the old one needed changing."

"When did you last see him last night?"

"I saw Vikram drinking tea at 9:50," said Dr. Kapoor. "He was alive then, and perfectly normal."

He leaned, very slightly, on the word "perfectly".

"After that?"

"I was in my room. My clinic phoned, then I read for a while and fell asleep. I woke up when Bahadur knocked on the door."

"What do you think, Doctor? A heart attack?"

The doctor folded his handkerchief. "It's entirely possible. With his history..."

"Is it possible, or is that what you think?"

Dr. Kapoor took off his glasses and began to clean them. "I won't say anything before the post-mortem, Inspector. It wouldn't be proper for a doctor."

When all four had gone, Kabir laid their statements side by side on the table.

"Sir, every one of them could have a motive. The wife: money. The partner: the business, and stopping the sale of the hotel. The manager: access. And the doctor: medical knowledge."

Dev closed the file.

"A motive isn't proof of murder. Everyone in the world has a reason to kill somebody, Kabir. Most people still get up in the morning and go to the office." He took out a cardamom pod. "First find out who's lying."

Kabir looked over the four statements again. "Where do we start?"

"Did you notice something?"

"What?"

"All four of them gave us their times. Exactly. A quarter to ten. After ten. Ten past ten. Ten to ten." Dev touched each sheet with his finger. "Nobody asked them what time it was. And yet every one of them remembered their clock."

"So?"

"So people who really have nothing to do with it don't remember the time, Kabir. They remember what they were doing. The only people who remember the time are the ones who know someone is going to ask them later."

Kabir stopped writing.

Out in the corridor Mohan the waiter walked past with a tray of teacups. Dev watched him, thought for a moment, and said to Kabir:

"Call that boy in. The one who took the tea up last night."
