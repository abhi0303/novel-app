At eight in the morning, four people and a laptop went down into the basement.

From Dehradun came Sandeep, the generator company's service engineer: a young man with the company logo on his jacket, whose fingers moved over a laptop like a pianist's. With him was Harish Rana, an old electrician who had known the wiring of half the hotels in Mussoorie for twenty years. Payal Thapliyal and a young man from her team. And Rohan, whom Dev had summoned himself to open the panel.

Rohan took out his bunch of keys, opened the panel, and stood to one side. Hands behind his back. Calm, as always.

"There's a chip in the generator's controller, sir," said Sandeep, plugging in a cable. "Every start, every stop, every alarm is saved with the time. The customer doesn't even know. We look at it when we do a service."

A list came up on the laptop screen. Black background, green letters.

"The bin," Dev said to Payal.

Next to the generator stood a heavy black iron drum. Someone had chalked *Waste oil* on it. Inside were rags soaked in diesel and engine oil, empty oil tins, a broken filter, and years of rubbish.

Payal's team spread out a large plastic sheet and began emptying the drum onto it, slowly. Every item separated. Every item photographed. Black, slippery, stinking work. Kabir held a handkerchief over his nose.

After fifteen minutes, one of Payal's fingers stopped.

An oil-soaked rag. In its folds, crumpled up, something that glinted. Payal lifted it out with tongs and brought it into the light.

A medicine strip. Aluminium foil, plastic blisters. It was in a bad way, crushed into a ball, soaked in oil. But when Payal gently opened it out, a fragment of the printing on the back of the foil was clearly legible.

*...DOON PHARMA LTD · DEHRADUN...*
*B.No. DP-2311 · EXP 05/2025*

Bahadur was called. Without being asked, he opened the door of his cubicle. "Look, saab. Whatever you want to see." Inside: a string cot, a trunk, an old photograph of the Gorkha Rifles on the wall, a radio. Payal's team went through all of it. They found nothing.

"Saab, I don't have anything," said Bahadur. "I don't even take my blood-pressure tablets. Doctor saab tells me to, and I still don't."

Rohan was still standing by the panel. He looked at the strip once. Only once. Then his eyes came back to Dev.

Doon Pharma Ltd. had shut down in the summer of 2024. Its factory was now a warehouse. But in Paltan Bazaar there was an old distributor called Garhwal Medical Agencies, run by an old man with heavy-lidded eyes. He set a mountain of dusty files in front of Kabir.

"Batch DP-2311. November 2023." He opened a file. "It went to nine shops. In Dehradun. Here's the list."

Nine shops. Kabir spent the whole day in those nine shops. Everywhere the same question: who was this brand of this medicine sold to? Most shops gave ten or twenty patients' names. Some said they didn't remember. One said the register itself had been lost.

At seven in the evening, the last shop. An old chemist's on Rajpur Road, with *Doon Medicos* on its board and the neon 'M' long since dead.

Behind the counter was Mr. Bisht: sixty, glasses on the tip of his nose, and a long ledger in which he wrote every customer's name by hand. "I don't trust computers, saab. A ledger never crashes."

Kabir showed him a photograph of the strip. Bisht adjusted his glasses and looked.

"Arre. This is..." His voice changed. "This medicine, this company, the pink one? Yes, I used to sell it. But only to one patient. Every month. For three years."

"Only one?"

"Only one. An old patient. Her doctor said to give her this brand, it was cheap, and she didn't have much money." Bisht turned back through the old pages of his ledger. One, two, ten. "Look here. Every month, on the first. Two strips."

He put his finger on a line. Kabir leaned over and read the name.

And then read it again.

"Who used to collect the medicine?" There was a strange roughness in Kabir's voice. "Did she come herself?"

"No, saab. Her son. Every month. A quiet boy. He never said anything, just put the money down and left. His name... I don't remember his name, saab. We knew them by the mother's name."

"When is the last entry?"

"September 2024." Bisht closed the ledger. "In October she passed away. It was very sad. She needed a heart operation, and they didn't have the money. The boy ran around everywhere, here, there. It couldn't be done."

"On what date?" asked Kabir.

"The fourteenth of October," said Bisht at once. "I remember, saab. It was the anniversary of my shop that day. Thirty years. We didn't hand out sweets."

"And the tablets she had left? The ones at home?"

Bisht shrugged. "They never came back here. They must still be at home. Who throws away his mother's medicine, saab?"
