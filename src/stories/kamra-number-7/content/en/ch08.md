The road from Mussoorie to Dehradun coiled down the back of the mountain like a snake. A JCB still stood where yesterday's landslide had come down, and someone had daubed white lime on the fallen rocks at the roadside so they could be seen at night.

Kabir talked the whole way: about Arjun, about Rohan, about the doctor's bottle. Dev watched out of the window and now and then said "hmm". Kabir knew Dev's "hmm" came in three kinds: one meant "I'm listening", one meant "you're wrong", and one meant "be quiet". All three turned up on this drive.

---

The mortuary at Doon Hospital was in the basement. Another basement, Kabir thought. In this case everything is hidden downstairs.

Dr. Anita Joshi was waiting for them in a white coat, her glasses pushed up on her forehead. She was around fifty, with short hair and the calm of someone who has seen so much death that nothing about it feels urgent any more.

"The body first," she said, picking up a clipboard. "The heart is enlarged. The scar from the old attack is clear. The coronary arteries are sixty to seventy per cent narrowed. A heart like that could have failed any day."

"So it was a heart attack?" asked Kabir.

"That's the problem." Anita set the clipboard down. "If this were a classic heart attack, I'd find a fresh clot in one of the arteries. A blockage that formed that night. I opened every artery. There's no fresh clot."

Dev looked up. "So?"

"So the heart stopped, but not because the blood supply was cut off. The heartbeat went wrong. An arrhythmia. The rhythm broke and the heart stopped. In a weak heart that can happen on its own." She paused. "It can also happen because of something."

"Something like what?"

"Toxicology will tell you that, not me. There's tea in the stomach, a little food, no intact tablets that I could see. He vomited, which fits with what he ate that night. The samples have gone to the FSL with your SP's letter. Three or four days."

"Time?"

"Same as I told you yesterday. A quarter past ten to midnight. Most likely half past ten to a quarter past eleven." Anita looked at Dev. "And yes, Rathore, your 10:15 clock theory? I'm saying neither yes nor no. From the body, it could be 10:15. It could also be 10:50."

"And the hand?"

Anita pointed to a trolley with a small glass tray on it, a lamp above, and a thin plastic cover over the tray.

"The hand."

---

The paper was a thin strip: about nine centimetres long and three wide. Both long edges were torn, ragged, as if it had been ripped away from a larger sheet. Cream-coloured, with faint blue ruling. The paper was still wet and had darkened at the edges. One corner had a large water stain, as if something had soaked in there.

And across the middle, in blue fountain-pen ink, ran a single line. The water had blurred a few of the letters, but it was perfectly readable:

> Asli chehra usi ka hai, jisne mujhe aakhri baar zinda dekha.

*The real face belongs to the one who saw me alive for the last time.*

@scene wet-paper | The paper from Vikram Sethi's fist. Wet, torn, and one line.

Above and below the line were fragments of other lines: the tail of a letter, the tip of a stroke. Too broken to make a single word.

Kabir read the line aloud. Then again. Then a third time, slowly, as if weighing every word.

"*The one who saw me alive for the last time.*" He looked up. "Sir, this is... this is a clue to identify someone. Whoever saw me last. Meaning whoever met him last is the real face. Is the killer."

Dev bent over the glass tray. He didn't touch the paper. He only looked, for a long time.

"Or," he said softly, "it's a line that was left so that we would suspect one particular person."

"Left? You mean planted?"

"I mean we don't know yet who wrote this line, when they wrote it, who they wrote it for, or how it got into Vikram's fist." Dev straightened up. "If it's a dying message, it's a very strange one. A dying man writes a name, Kabir. 'Arjun.' 'Meera.' He doesn't write riddles."

"Maybe he didn't know the name?"

"Maybe."

Anita cut in. "One more thing. The paper was wet when I opened the fist. Wet from inside. But his palm was dry. And it doesn't look like tea: no brown colour, nothing sticky. What it's wet with, the FSL will tell you."

Dev took an evidence pouch from his bag: the notepad from Room Seven. He laid it beside the tray, under the light.

The same cream colour. The same faint blue ruling, at the same spacing. And when Anita swung the lamp round behind the paper, a fragment of watermark glowed in one corner of the strip: parts of three or four letters. *...VER PI...*

On the notepad's sheet, in the same position, was the whole watermark. *SILVER PINE.*

"It's from this notepad," said Kabir. His voice had dropped. "The top sheet that was missing. This is a piece of it."

"A piece of what Vikram was writing," said Dev, "stayed in his fist. The rest of the page is gone."

"So someone pulled the page away and this strip stayed in his hand?"

"Or Vikram tore it himself. Or someone else did. Or someone put this strip in his fist." Dev returned the notepad to his bag. "For now, all three are possible."

They agreed that the handwriting and ink would be examined by Mr. Bhatt, the questioned-document examiner at the State FSL, against samples of Vikram's own writing: the "Since when?" in the margin of the audit printout, his diary, his chequebook. Anita sealed the strip in a special pouch and wrote on it: *Wet — examine before drying.*

---

On the way back, near Kolhukhet, an email arrived on Kabir's phone from the telecom company's nodal officer: the call detail record for Vikram's number.

Kabir zoomed in and read.

"Sir. 21:54. Outgoing call from Vikram's number to Arjun's number. One minute fifty-two seconds. Arjun told the truth, the call happened." He scrolled. "And... 22:12. An SMS from Vikram's number to Arjun's number."

"Content?"

"A CDR doesn't show content, sir. Only the time. But it'll be on Arjun's phone."

---

As soon as they got back to the hotel, Dev called Arjun into the card room and asked him to put his phone on the table.

At first Arjun refused. He talked about his lawyer. Then Dev laid the CDR printout in front of him with a finger on the 22:12 line. Arjun stared at that line for a while. Then, without a word, he unlocked the phone, opened Vikram's conversation and put it down on the table.

The chat was open on the screen, and in it was one message:

:::message Vikram → Arjun · 10:12 PM
Mujhe pata chal gaya hai tumne kya kiya. Agar aaj raat 10:30 tak mujhse nahi mile, toh main sabko sach bata dunga.

(I know what you did. If you don't come and see me by 10:30 tonight, I'll tell everyone the truth.)
:::

Below it, two blue ticks. *Read 10:13 PM.*

"You saw this message," said Dev.

"Yes." Arjun's voice was very tired. "I saw it. But I didn't understand what it meant."

Kabir raised his eyebrows. "Even after ₹4.2 crore, you didn't understand?"

Arjun looked at Kabir, but said nothing.

"What were you doing after this message arrived?" asked Dev. "From 10:13 to 10:41?"

"I was in the car. I told you."

"And the 10:30 deadline?"

"I thought he was bluffing. Vikram was always threatening something." Arjun picked up his phone. "I didn't go, Inspector. I didn't go."

---

After Arjun left, Kabir leaned his back against the card-room wall.

"Sir, something's going round in my head. If Vikram sent this message himself, he was calling Arjun to come and see him. By half past ten. But if someone else used his phone..."

"Then?"

"Then the message could be a trap. Someone wanted Arjun to go to Room Seven before half past ten. Or someone wanted the police to read this message later and think Arjun had gone."

Dev wrote the message's timestamp in his notebook: 22:12. Right above the CCTV's 22:14:07.

"Two minutes," said Kabir, peering at the notebook. "Message at 10:12. Red umbrella out of the gate at 10:14. Sir, it's all connected. I know it is."

"Maybe," said Dev. "Or maybe these are two separate stories that happened in the same minute. It happens, Kabir. On a stormy night there were ten people in one hotel. Each of them had their own story. When every story runs on the same clock, it looks as if everything is connected."

He opened the photograph of the paper on his phone. The wet strip. The blue ink.

*The real face belongs to the one who saw me alive for the last time.*

"Who saw him alive for the last time?" asked Kabir, as if asking the air. "If Mohan's telling the truth, Mohan, at 10:05. If the doctor's telling the truth, the doctor, at 9:50. If Arjun's lying, then maybe Arjun..."

"And if this line was written for someone else?"

Kabir stopped. "For whom?"

"I don't know." Dev switched off the phone. "But Vikram sat at his desk writing something, Kabir. A whole page. With a fountain pen, which people only pick up these days when they have something important to write." He looked out of the window, where darkness was settling on the hills again. "And something like that is always written for someone."
