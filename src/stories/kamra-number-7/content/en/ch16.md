At ten the next morning an email arrived from Dr. Mehra. Three attachments, and only three words in the subject line: *Bottle, strip, residue.*

Dev opened the laptop in the library. Kabir stood behind him, Rawat at the door. The three of them read it together.

**One. The doctor's bottle.** The seal had been examined under a microscope. No hole, no needle mark, no sign of the glue being reapplied. The seal had never been broken. The bottle was opened at the FSL. Tablets inside: thirty. According to the label: thirty. White, round, scored across the middle.

**Two. The strip from Vikram's kit.** White tablets, Vikram's old brand. A strip of fourteen, five taken. According to the doctor's clinic records, Vikram took one tablet a day, and this strip had been started on the tenth. The count matched. No tablet was missing.

**Three. The residue in the teapot and cup.** Here Dr. Mehra had written the most. The drug was the same family. But along with the drug there were the other parts of a tablet: filler, binder. And a coating dye.

Nobody in the library said a word.

Then Rawat said, very quietly, "The doctor's kit could have pink tablets in it."

"Check it," said Dev. "The kit, and the clinic."

Within the hour they had been through the doctor's kit: a small bottle of antacid, some painkillers, a blood-pressure cuff, an injection set, and two or three other medicines. No pink tablets. In Dehradun one of Rawat's men went through the clinic's stock register. The clinic carried only one brand of that medicine: the white one.

Dr. Kapoor was called to the library. Dev had him read that part of the residue report. *Pink coating dye.*

The doctor read it. Once. Then again. Then he put the paper down, and a strange expression came over his face, relief and pain at the same time.

"Pink," he said softly. "The pink-coated tablet... an old local company in Dehradun used to make it. I used to prescribe it myself, once. It was cheap; it suited poor patients. The company shut down two years ago. In 2024. You can't get it in the market any more."

"Two years ago?" Kabir looked up.

"Yes. Whoever has these tablets has old stock. From some old prescription. Sitting in someone's cupboard."

Dev watched the doctor for a while. Then he pulled his chair forward.

"Doctor. Now you know the poison didn't come from your bottle. It didn't come from your kit. So tell me the truth. When you went into Number Seven at 10:22, how was Vikram? The truth, this time."

---

Dr. Kapoor took off his glasses. This time he neither cleaned them nor held them. He just set them down on the table, as if he no longer needed them.

"He was ill," he said.

His voice was so low that Kabir had to lean forward.

"He was sweating. On his forehead. In this cold. His hands were shaking. He put the cup down and said, 'Kapoor, even the tea tastes bitter today.'" The doctor's voice broke. "Vikram. Who had drunk his tea bitter for twenty-five years. He said the tea tasted bitter."

Nobody spoke.

"Then he looked at the desk lamp and asked, 'Why is this bulb so yellow? Everything looks yellow.'" The doctor looked at his own hands, as if there might be an answer in them. "Seeing yellow, Inspector. The oldest sign of an overdose of this medicine. A hundred years old. They teach it in the first year of medical college, with the story of a famous painter who saw the world in yellow. I must have written it in an exam ten times."

"And what did you do?"

"I thought it was acidity. Tiredness. Anger. He'd fought so much at dinner, and then the will, the hotel. I thought he was worked up." The doctor closed his eyes. "I gave him a spoonful of antacid. Told him to sleep, we'd see in the morning. And I left."

"Why?"

The doctor opened his eyes. There were tears in them, and he made no attempt to hold them back.

"Because Meera was waiting for me in Number Nine." He looked straight at Dev. "I could have saved him, Inspector. If I'd stayed five minutes more. Taken his pulse. Looked at his eyes. Called an ambulance. If at that moment I'd been a doctor, and not a lover." His voice cracked. "That's why I said 9:50. 'He was perfectly normal then.' So that nobody would find out that when I saw him for the last time, he wasn't normal. And I did nothing."

Kabir wrote nothing in his notebook. For the first time.

The doctor was silent for a while. Then he wiped his eyes, picked up his glasses and put them on. As if he were becoming a doctor again.

"There's one more thing," he said. "It may mean nothing. At 10:22, as I was going from Number Nine towards Number Seven, someone was standing behind the glass door of the service staircase."

Dev sat bolt upright. "Who?"

"I don't know. Someone from the staff. The glass is frosted, and it was dark on that side. Just a shape. Something like a dark blazer. Or a jacket. Dark-coloured." The doctor tried to remember. "It wasn't a waiter's white jacket, that I'm sure of. White would have shown up even in the dark. I thought it was someone from the staff going downstairs. Or Bahadur. I didn't pay attention. I... I was in a hurry."

After the doctor left, Dev took a blank sheet and drew a line across it. A timeline.

"Listen carefully, Kabir. Dr. Mehra says at this dose the vomiting and dizziness start some time after it's drunk. Not straight away. The doctor arrived at 10:22, and by then Vikram was sweating, the tea tasted bitter, everything looked yellow. That means the poison was in his stomach well before 10:22."

"How long before?"

"At least fifteen or twenty minutes. Maybe more." Dev made a mark on the line. "10:05. The tea arrives. Vikram opens the door, takes the tray, shuts the door. When did he drink his first cup? Five minutes later? Ten?"

"Shortly after 10:05," Kabir said slowly. "So the poison was already in the tea. When it arrived. Or right after it arrived, when Vikram was alone."

"And the doctor came at 10:22. After the poison had already started working." Dev put the pen down. "The doctor didn't poison him, Kabir. He couldn't have. By the time he got there, the poison had already begun its work. The doctor's crime is that he saw it and didn't recognise it. That's a crime, but it isn't murder."

Rawat looked at his corkboard, at the red thread running from Meera to the doctor, and said nothing. After a while he quietly took the thread down.

Kabir stared at the line for a long time. Then he said, with a new, slow hesitation in his voice:

"Sir. There's one more possibility. If the poison went into the tea after 10:05 and before 10:22, there was only one person in the room at that time."

"Vikram."

"Vikram." Kabir took a breath. "Could he... could he have taken it himself? Suicide? The fraud, the betrayal, his wife's affair, the will. Maybe he thought... and the letter he was writing could have been a suicide note. And someone made it disappear. Meera, maybe, because insurance doesn't pay out on suicide, and..."

"It's a good thought," said Dev, and Kabir was surprised, because Dev's tone was completely serious. "But three questions. The pink tablets weren't Vikram's. Where would he get them, from a company that closed down, two years old? Second: why would a man about to kill himself ask his doctor for new medicine, saying 'I need it tonight'? And third: the torn paper in his fist. If he had torn up his own letter, the whole page would be in the room. That page left the room, Kabir. With someone."
