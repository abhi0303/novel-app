The forensic team arrived at half past eight: three people, two big aluminium cases, and their head, Payal Thapliyal. Her voice was so soft that people fell silent without being asked, just to hear her.

"Have you touched anything?" she asked Dev at the door.

"No."

"Good." Payal pulled up her mask. "People always say 'no', and then it turns out they only touched 'a little'."

Kabir opened his notebook. This was his job: every object, every position, every fragment, written down. Dev would talk and Kabir would write. Sometimes Kabir added something of his own, and Dev never let him cross it out. "Write down the wrong questions too," he would say. "Sometimes the right answer is hiding under a wrong question."

They started with the door.

---

**The door.**

Room Seven had two locks. One was an old mortise lock that worked with a key; that was the one the master key had opened. The other, on the inside, was a brass sliding bolt: long, heavy, from the British days. The bolt was fixed to the door, and its rod slid into a brass keeper screwed to the frame.

The keeper was no longer on the frame. It lay on the floor four inches away. Both screws were still in it, with splinters of wood torn out along with them.

Payal shone a torch on the wound in the frame. "This is fresh," she said. "Look, the wood inside is pale. It hasn't darkened yet. And the screw holes aren't clean, they're split. If someone had loosened these screws beforehand, the holes would be round and smooth. This was broken by force. In one go."

"So the bolt really was across," said Kabir.

"When the door was broken, the bolt was across. Yes."

Dev nodded and said nothing.

**The window.**

A sash window, latched from inside. Payal examined the edges of the frame. Along the bottom, old paint had sealed the sash to the frame.

"Nobody has opened this window in at least a year or two," she said. "If the paint seal had cracked, it would show."

Kabir wrote: *Window: shut, for years. Not an exit.*

**The fireplace.**

Cold. Old ash inside, under a layer of dust. Nothing burned recently.

**The bed.**

Made, and smooth. No dent in the pillow. Vikram hadn't gone to bed.

**The bathroom.**

Vikram's toiletry kit: shaving things, a comb, and a strip of medicine. White tablets, his heart prescription. A few had been taken. Kabir photographed the strip.

**The wastebasket.**

Under the desk stood a small brass wastebasket. Payal bent over it and wrinkled her nose.

"Vomit," she said. "A little. Fresh."

Kabir looked at Dev. "Do people vomit with a heart attack, sir?"

"They can," Payal answered before Dev could. "With a lot of things, they can." She sealed the wastebasket.

To the right of the tray lay a black fountain pen with a gold nib. Its cap was off, lying three inches away, as if the pen had been put down in a hurry. The ink on the nib had dried.

Dev looked at the body. On the middle finger of Vikram's right hand was a fresh stain of blue ink, exactly where a pen rests.

"He was writing," Dev said quietly. "Shortly before he died."

Beside the pen lay a notepad: Silver Pine Hotel stationery. Thick, cream-coloured paper, the hotel's name printed faintly at the bottom, and a watermark in the paper that showed against the light. At the top of the pad ran a strip of glue where the sheets are torn off.

The top sheet had been torn away.

Little ragged scraps of paper still clung to the glue strip, as if the sheet had been pulled off in a hurry, or with force. The sheet underneath was clean. Blank.

Dev signalled to Kabir. "Search the whole room. Everywhere. One written page."

For the next twenty minutes Kabir and a young man from the forensic team went through the room: the desk drawers, under the bed, the suitcase, the pockets of the kurta, the fireplace, the wastebasket, the bathroom.

There was no written page anywhere.

In one of the desk drawers they found an amber medicine bottle, sealed, with a pharmacy label in Vikram's name. Kabir added it to the list and moved on. At that moment it was just a bottle.

**The clock.**

At the edge of the desk lay a brass table clock, face down. Payal turned it over with both hands, very gently. Its glass was cracked across the middle, as if it had struck something heavy. The brass inkstand on the desk, perhaps.

The hands said 10:15.

"It must have stopped when it fell," said Kabir.

"Must have," said Dev. Kabir didn't write down whatever was in that "must have". But he remembered it.

**The phone.**

Vikram's mobile lay face down on the desk. It was locked. Kabir slipped it into an evidence bag. "We'll have to write to the company for the call records and WhatsApp, sir."

Last of all, the body.

Payal examined Vikram's eyes, his neck, his fingernails, and spoke each finding into her recorder. No injuries. No marks on the throat. Nails clean. A dried line of vomit at the corner of the mouth. Rigor fully established.

"Time of death?" Kabir asked.

"Roughly between ten and midnight," said Payal. "I won't say more than that standing here. We'll talk after the post-mortem."

She took several photographs of Vikram's right fist. The white corner of paper between two fingers was clearly visible now. It was slightly damp and darkened at the edges.

"As you said, Inspector," Payal said. "This hand opens on the table."

Bahadur was waiting in the corridor. He was fifty-five, with a straight back and a soldier's moustache. He had retired from the Gorkha Rifles and had kept the gate at Silver Pine for the last fifteen years. He held his cap in his hands, like a man standing outside a temple.

"Bahadur-ji," said Dev. "Tell me what happened last night."

"Saab, Rohan saab came down and said come upstairs, Vikram saab isn't answering his phone in Number Seven. We went. Rohan saab used the key, the lock opened, but the door didn't move an inch. I pushed it myself, saab. Not one inch. The bolt was on inside."

"How do you know it was the bolt?"

"Saab, I've been in this hotel fifteen years. I know the sound of the bolt in Number Seven. The door gives a little at the bottom, but at the top it's jammed solid. That's where the bolt is."

"And then?"

"Then we both used our shoulders. It broke the third time. Rohan saab went in first. He looked and said, 'Bahadur, get the doctor, Number Nine. Quickly.' I ran."

"How long before you came back?"

Bahadur thought. "A minute, saab. A minute and a half. Doctor saab opened the door, I told him, and he came right behind me."

"When you came back, what was Rohan doing?"

"Bending over by the desk, saab. Next to Vikram saab. Checking his pulse." Bahadur shook his head. "Then doctor saab did the CPR. Poor doctor saab was very upset. He'd even put his kurta on inside out in the hurry."

Kabir's pen paused for a moment, then kept moving.

"And when you went to Number Nine," Dev asked, "did you see anything else?"

Bahadur looked at Dev. Then at the floor. His moustache twitched slightly, as if he were about to say something.

"No, saab," he said. "Nothing."

Dev looked at him a moment longer. Then he said, "All right. Thank you."

Bahadur saluted and left. Kabir said quietly, "Sir, he's hiding something."

"Yes."

"Won't you ask him?"

"He won't tell us yet. Not until he thinks keeping quiet will do more harm than talking." Dev took out a cardamom pod. "He's been in that job fifteen years, Kabir. Whatever he's hiding might be someone else's honour."

By afternoon the body had left for the mortuary at Doon Hospital. The room was sealed again.

Dev and Kabir stood by the long corridor window. Below, the fog was thinning, and far off on the hills the first thin line of sunlight had appeared.

"So, sir," Kabir said, opening his notebook. "What have we got so far?"

Dev spoke slowly, as if setting things down on a table one at a time.

"A man was locked in his own room. Bolted from the inside, and it really was bolted. The window hasn't been opened in years. He was writing something at his desk: there's ink on his finger and the pen is uncapped. That written page is nowhere in the room. There's a scrap of paper in his fist. There's vomit in the wastebasket. There's an unopened sugar-free sachet on the tray. His papers ask a question worth ₹4.2 crore. And there's a clock that says 10:15."

"And a phone call," Kabir added, "that came after the body was found."

"And a phone call."

Kabir wrote it all down, then chewed the end of his pen. "That page, sir. If Vikram tore it off himself, it should be in the room. If it isn't in the room..."

"Then someone took it out."

"When?"

Dev drew a straight line with his finger in the mist on the window.

"That's the question. When. Did someone take it before the door was bolted? Or after the door was broken?"

Kabir watched the line slowly fade in the condensation.

"And if it was before," he said, "then who was the last person to see Vikram?"

Dev didn't answer. But Kabir noticed that his gaze had travelled down the corridor, past the glass door of the service staircase, and come to rest on the closed door of Room Nine.
