Pooja was given a chair in the library. She had left the broom leaning against the wall outside, but her hands were still curled as if they needed something to hold.

"Start from the beginning, Pooja," said Dev. "Take your time."

"Sir, I'm on the morning shift, eight to four. On the fourteenth I cleaned Number Seven at 11:20. Vikram sir was downstairs in the lounge, going over some papers with Arjun sir. He'd told me to be quick."

"Then?"

"I cleaned the desk. I picked up the clock, wiped the dust from underneath and put it back. That's when I saw it said 10:15. My phone said 11:20. I thought the battery must have run out." She took a breath. "Sir, that clock is always in that room. It's old, from British times, but a few years ago they had a battery movement put inside. The battery's run out twice before."

"What did you do?"

"I wrote it in the register, sir. It's at the housekeeping desk. For maintenance requests."

Kabir saw that Pooja had brought a thin exercise book with her, with a blue cover and dog-eared corners. She laid it on the table and opened it to a page.

"That's manager sahab's, sir. Every evening he goes through the register, and for each one he's read he puts a tick and his initials. Then the work gets done the next day."

"Rohan was on leave that morning."

"Yes, sir. He came in at three in the afternoon. He must have looked at it in the evening." Pooja glanced at the register. "The tap got fixed. The bulb got changed. The clock... I don't know."

Kabir looked at that tick for a moment. Small, neat, one quick stroke. *R.M.* He wrote in his notebook: *Clock request — seen, R.M.*

"Did you tell anyone else the clock had stopped?"

"No, sir. Nobody asked. Then everyone started saying the clock stopped at 10:15, so sir died at 10:15... so I thought..." Her voice wavered. "I thought maybe I was wrong. Maybe the battery started again at night and then..."

"Batteries don't do that, Pooja," Dev said gently. "You did right to tell us. You did very right."

In the afternoon Payal Thapliyal came herself, carrying a small box. She had driven straight up from Dehradun, and her face had the particular happiness scientists have when something in their test comes out exactly as they expected.

She spread a cloth on the library table and opened the box. Inside was the brass clock, standing upright, its back open.

"Three things," she said. "One." She picked up a small battery and touched the two leads of a multimeter to it. A number came up on the display. "0.31 volts. A new battery is one and a half. This battery is dead. At this voltage the movement won't run for a single second."

"Two?"

"Two. The movement isn't damaged." She pointed at the hands. "If the clock had stopped because of the fall, the hands would be bent, or a gear inside would have shifted, or the battery contact would have come loose. None of that happened. The glass is broken, that's all. The glass broke when it fell or struck something. But the movement itself is fine."

"Three?"

Payal took a new battery out of her pocket, fitted it into the clock and closed the back. Then she stood the clock upright.

For a few seconds nothing happened. Then, in the silence of the library, came a faint, clear sound.

*Tick. Tick. Tick.*

The long hand moved past 10:15. Towards 10:16.

"Three," said Payal. "As soon as the battery went in, it started. Right where it stopped. This clock didn't stop because it fell, Inspector. It stopped because the battery died. And according to your housekeeping girl, it had been stopped since that morning."

Kabir stared at the clock as though someone had pulled a brick out of the foundations of his house.

"The clock... never stopped that night," he said slowly.

"It had already stopped," said Dev. "At 10:15 on the morning of the fourteenth. Or maybe at 10:15 the night before. It doesn't matter. It has nothing to do with the death that night."

"Then the glass?"

"The glass broke that night. When Vikram fell, or slumped over the desk, his hand caught the clock, it went over on its face and hit the inkstand." Payal shrugged. "A stopped clock fell over. That's all. And everyone looked at its hands and assumed that time had stopped there."

After Payal left, Kabir was quiet for a long time. Then he opened his notebook, turned back through three days of pages, and made a light pencil mark against one line after another.

"Sir," he said at last. "I didn't even realise how much I'd built on 10:15."

"Tell me."

"The CCTV man at 10:14. I thought he was running away after the killing, because the clock stopped a minute later. Rohan's generator entry at 10:12. I thought he was in the basement at 10:15, so he was in the clear. The message at 10:12. I thought it came three minutes before the death. The doctor's 9:50. I thought that was twenty-five minutes before the death, so maybe he gave him something and the medicine took twenty-five minutes to work..." He put the pencil down. "Everything was standing on a clock that had stopped that morning."

"That's what clocks are for, Kabir." Dev was standing by the window. "A clock doesn't lie. People make clocks lie. Or we put our own lies into a clock's mouth."

"What now?"

"Now we can't treat 10:15 as the exact time of death. Anita says a quarter past ten to midnight. So we can't compare each suspect's alibi against one fixed minute any more. We have to look at the whole night. A broad timeline. Physical evidence. And the contradictions in what people have told us. All of it together."

Kabir nodded. Then he looked at the line from Pooja's register in his notebook. *Clock request — seen, R.M.*

"Sir. One question."

"Hmm."

"Did anyone know the clock had stopped?"

"Pooja."

"And anyone who reads that register." Kabir put his finger on the line. "R.M. Rohan Malhotra. On the evening of the fourteenth he saw this and ticked it. So he knew the clock had been stopped since the morning. And for three days we've been going on and on about 10:15, and not once did he say the clock had already stopped."

Dev didn't answer straight away.

"Maybe," he said. "Or maybe he ticked it without reading it. Managers often do. A three-line register, the evening of the fourteenth, after half a day off. Would you read every line?"

"I would."

"You're Kabir Ansari. You read the menu card twice." Dev turned and came back towards him. "Write it down. But don't build a story out of it yet. A tick is a tick."

Kabir wrote it down. And under it, out of habit, a small question mark.

In the evening Dev spread three days' worth of paper across the library table. The CCTV still. The parking printout. The CDR. The screenshot of the message. The photograph of the wet strip. The doctor's bottle. Pooja's register. The report on the clock.

There was a lot. And none of it joined up.

"Let's do one thing, sir," said Kabir. "Put it all in one place. But separately this time. What's confirmed, by a machine or a document. And what someone has only told us."

Dev nodded. Kabir wrote, and on every line Dev asked the same question: "Who said this? Or who recorded it?"

Kabir looked at the list and whistled softly. "Sir, not one line marked 'confirmed' tells us who killed Vikram. And everything that would tell us is only 'claimed'."

"That's exactly why people lie, Kabir," said Dev. "A machine only tells you what happened. A person tells you why. And 'why' is where a lie can hide."

"This leaves us a new question," said Dev. "If the clock can't tell us the time of death, who can?"

"The body."

"The body gives a window of an hour. That's not good enough."

"Then?"

"People." Dev picked up the photograph of the wet strip. "Who saw Vikram alive for the last time? That person will tell us how long he was alive. And maybe," he put the photograph down, "that's exactly what this paper is trying to say."

At that moment the library door opened. SI Rawat came in with a file in his hand and a glow on his face that even three sleepless nights hadn't put out.

"Sir," he said. "I think I've worked it all out."
