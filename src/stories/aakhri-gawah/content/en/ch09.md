He had kept his promise to Nandita on Wednesday night. It was the one thing that week he was sure he had done right.

At half past ten, an hour and a half after the arrest, he had driven to Aliganj and walked the last fifty metres up the dark lane to the green gate. A single bulb burned over Sharda Devi's door. She opened it before he knocked, already in her shawl, as if she had been sitting up waiting for bad news and was only surprised that it had taken this long.

He told her in the courtyard, under the neem tree, in as few words as he could. Nandita had been arrested. There were things she had concealed about the night of the fifth, and she would not explain them. She would be produced before a magistrate in the morning.

Sharda Devi closed her eyes. "Nandita," she said, as if trying the name on something it would not fit.

Then Dev looked up and saw the boy.

Rahul was standing at the top of the narrow stairs that led to the barsati, in a grey sweatshirt, one hand on the iron railing. He must have heard the gate. He had heard everything.

"For what?" he said.

"She concealed some things from us, Rahul. About where she was that evening. We need to know why."

The boy's hand tightened on the railing until Dev could see the knuckles even in that poor light.

"She didn't do anything," Rahul said.

"How do you know?"

For a moment Rahul seemed about to answer. Something moved in his face that Dev could not name. Then it closed, the way his grandmother's face closed when Pari's name was spoken.

"She never did anything," Rahul said. "Not once."

He turned and went back up into the dark. Dev heard the barsati door shut, quietly, not slammed. It was the sound of someone who had learned long ago that slammed doors bring consequences.

Only later, driving home along the empty Ring Road, did Dev notice that the boy's sentence could be read two ways. He did not know which one Rahul had meant. He was not sure Rahul knew either.

He turned the cap over in his hands.

Mohan's hand rose to his own collarbone, where there was nothing, and mimed it: lifting something heavy over his head, slowly. "Slowly. And he would put it down on the glass of his desk. Like this." He laid his hand flat on Dev's desk, gently, and Dev heard in his mind the sound it would make: gold on glass, a small, clear *chhan*.

"That sound, sahab." Mohan's voice dropped. "That sound meant it was going to start. Whatever it was going to be. The children knew. I knew. "It was a Wednesday," said Mohan. "Late. Ten o'clock, maybe more. Rahul baba had a test that day, I think. I was in the kitchen. The study door shut. And then the sound." He closed his eyes. "*Chhan.* I put the radio on. Like always. But this time…"

The women's police station at Hazratganj was a two-storey building with a mural of Rani Laxmibai on horseback painted across its front wall, and a visitors' room that smelled of floor cleaner and old tea. SI Mamta Chauhan brought Nandita in at a quarter past nine and stood by the door.

Nandita had been in custody for twenty-four hours, and it showed in small ways: the hair tied back carelessly, the eyes ringed with sleeplessness, the same cotton suit. She sat down across the table from Dev and waited, her hands folded.

The malkhana at Gomti Nagar police station was a long room at the back of the building behind a steel door, lined with numbered shelves of sealed bags and boxes and bundles: a motorcycle helmet, a sack of seized liquor bottles, a framed wedding photograph in a plastic sleeve, a child's bicycle with a tag on its handlebar. The head moharrir, an old man with ink on his fingers and a register the size of a tombstone, found the red cloth bundle on shelf fourteen, checked its seal against the seizure memo, and laid it on the counter.

Dev signed the release. Kabir signed below him as witness. *Released to the legal heir, Rahul Sethi (minor), through his maternal grandmother, Smt. Sharda Devi, with the no-objection of the widow, Smt. Nandita Sethi.* There was a space for Sharda Devi's signature, which would be filled in when the chain was handed over.

Dev picked up the bundle. Through the red cloth he could feel the shape of the medallion, round and hard, and the cold weight of the links folded against it.

"Tomorrow," he said. "Aliganj."

"And Aditya, sir?" Kabir did not look at him. "You gave your word."

"Morning, Aditya. Evening, Aliganj."

"Nandita's custody runs out tomorrow morning. You have to produce her before the magistrate at eleven."
