*Thak. Thak. Thak.*

Dev stopped.

It came again, after a while, slower. Not to him. To a wall, to a girl who had been gone three years, to nobody. The boy had said he did it every night, and that nobody answered.

Dev stood on the dark stairs with his hand raised for a long time.

Then he knocked. Three times, with the knuckle that had the scar across it, on the rough whitewashed plaster of the stairwell.

On the other side of the wall there was a silence so complete that he could hear the kites' strings singing in the wind on the next roof. Then nothing more.

He did not know, then or afterwards, whether what he had done was an answer or a promise. He had told the boy he would not make him any promises. He went down the rest of the stairs with that knowledge sitting in his chest like a stone, and it did not go away.

Dev's flat was two rooms and a kitchen on the first floor of a house that belonged to a retired railway engineer. He had lived in it for six months and it still looked like a place someone was waiting to leave. He turned on the kitchen light, took off his coat, and laid the envelope on the table.

*Mohan — Dec.*

He sat and looked at it for a long time. He did not open it. There was no need. He knew what was inside to the rupee, and he knew it had been counted by a man who liked to count things in front of people.

At some point, he got up and went to the small desk in the other room and opened its one drawer. In their full sleeves.

Dev put the envelope in the drawer under the blue box and closed it.

He did not sleep. He lay on his back on the bed with the light from the street on the ceiling and went over it the way he went over everything, in order, fact by fact, as if it were a file.

He knew what the law said. He had known it on the roof. A seventeen-year-old who confessed to killing his father, twice, with the second blow a choice, would go before the Juvenile Justice Board. The Board would order a preliminary assessment, because the offence was the most serious kind, and the boy was more than sixteen. The assessment would look at whether he had understood what he was doing. He had told Dev, very clearly, not to let anyone pretend he hadn't. Dev did not think there was a psychologist in Uttar Pradesh who would find otherwise. The boy would very probably be tried as an adult in a children's court. Rajiv Sethi's friends would sit in the front row in their good coats. The newspapers would tell the city that a disturbed boy had killed a great man, the founder of a foundation that taught children to speak up. Somewhere in paragraph eleven, a girl who had wanted to be a teacher would appear as a *mitigating circumstance*.

And that would be the truth. The record would be complete. Every fact would be in its column.

Dev lay in the dark and knew, too, the other thing. He was not the first person to hold the truth about that house and decide what to do with it. Mohan had held it, and turned on his radio. Nandita had held it, and turned up her television. Mrs. Khanna had passed kulfi over the wall. A school had held a report in its hands and let it go. All of them had decided, in their own way, that it was not theirs to say.

Now it was his. He could tell himself that his silence was a different kind. That theirs had protected a man and his would protect a child. Perhaps it was even true.
