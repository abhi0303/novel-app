He knew something was different before he reached the green gate.

The lane was full of small lights. Someone had set clay diyas along the top of the boundary walls of the last three houses, one every arm's length, and their flames bent and steadied in the cold air coming up from the river. The smell of incense reached him at the corner, sandalwood and something sweeter, and under it, faint, the sound of girls' voices singing.

*Itni shakti humein dena Daata,*
*mann ka vishwas kamzor ho na…*

He stopped at the gate.

The courtyard had been swept and washed, and the old red bricks were still dark with the water. Under the neem tree, on a low wooden table covered with a white cloth, stood a photograph in a plain wooden frame. It was the same photograph that stood face down in Rajiv Sethi's study, the courtyard, the sunlight, the pink flower clip, but larger, and here someone had hung a garland of orange marigolds over its corner. In front of it burned a brass diya and three sticks of incense in a holder made from a half potato. A steel plate held squares of orange mango burfi, arranged in a careful star.

The five girls from Sunday's grammar lesson sat on the dari in a row, in their best clothes, singing with the stiff, earnest care of children who have been told this matters. Around them sat eight or nine women from the lane, shawls over their heads. Sharda Devi sat on her low stool beside the table, very straight, her hands folded in her lap and her eyes on the photograph.

And on the single step in front of the small room off the courtyard, the room with the yellow curtains and the pencil marks on the door frame, sat Rahul, with something white in his lap.

It was a soft toy. A rabbit, perhaps thirty centimetres long, of a white plush that had gone grey and flat with years of being held. One of its long ears had been sewn back on at the base with clumsy black stitches, much too big. Rahul was not holding it the way a seventeen-year-old holds a child's toy he has been handed to look after. He was holding it the way a child does, against his chest, both arms round it, his chin resting on the top of its head.

Dev understood before anyone told him. He understood from the diyas and the marigolds and the burfi and the girls' voices and the boy on the step with his sister's rabbit, and the understanding went through him like cold water.

One of the women at the back of the group turned her head at the sound of the gate, and he saw that it was his mother.

She did not seem surprised to see him. She looked at him, and at his hat, and very slightly she shook her head. *Not now. Not here.* Then she shifted along the dari to make room.

He took off his hat and went in and sat down beside her on the cold bricks.

"*Aaj Pari ki teesri barsi hai*," his mother whispered, without looking at him. Today is Pari's third death anniversary.

Across the courtyard, Sharda Devi had seen him. Their eyes met over the heads of the singing girls. She did not stand, or frown, or look away. She only held his gaze for a long moment, as if checking that he understood where he was. Then she inclined her head, a fraction, and turned back to the photograph.

When the bhajan ended, the smallest of the girls carried the plate of mango burfi round the circle with great seriousness, holding it in both hands. Dev took a piece. It was too sweet, and he ate all of it.

The women began to talk in low voices, the way people do after prayers, and the talk was all of Pari. Dev sat and listened, because there was nothing else he could do and because he found he wanted to.

"She used to sell tickets," said a stout woman in a green shawl, smiling. "You remember, Behenji? For her Shah Rukh Khan show. Five rupees. She came to every house in this lane with tickets she'd made out of an old notebook. *Pari Sethi presents: Shah Rukh Khan Live!* Then on Sunday she stood under this tree with a dupatta for a cape and did all the dialogues. *Rahul, naam toh suna hoga.*" The woman laughed, and wiped her eyes at the same time. "And of course her brother's name is Rahul, so every time she said it, she'd point at him, and he'd go red as a tomato, and the whole lane would fall about."

Several of the women laughed. On his step, Rahul did not look up, but Dev saw the corner of his mouth move.

"She did the teachers too," said another woman. "My Neha came home from school once crying with laughter. Pari had done the maths teacher, the one with the moustache, in the lunch break. She'd drawn a moustache on her lip with a sketch pen. It wouldn't come off for two days."

"She wanted to be a teacher," said Dev's mother quietly. "She told me once. She came to my house to return a dish and stood in the door and told me, very seriously, that she was going to be a teacher like her Nani, in a government school. And that in her class, no child would ever be made to stand in the corner." She paused. "I remember thinking, what a strange thing for a ten-year-old to say. *No child will ever be made to stand.*"

The courtyard was quiet for a moment.

"Mango," said the woman in the green shawl, softly. "Everything had to be mango. Mango ice cream, mango juice, mango burfi. In June she would sit on that wall and eat four mangoes one after another, with the juice running down to her elbows, and Behenji would shout at her from the kitchen…"

The women left one by one, touching Sharda Devi's feet or her shoulder as they went, saying the things people say. Dev's mother was the last. At the gate she put her hand on her son's arm for a moment and said nothing, and then went off down the lane between the diyas with her shawl pulled close.

The girls blew out the incense and went home.

He found Rahul still on the step outside the little room, the rabbit still in his arms. The courtyard was empty now except for the two of them and the photograph under the tree and the last of the diyas on the wall. Sharda Devi had gone inside. He could hear her moving about in the kitchen, the clink of steel plates.

He sat down on the bricks a few feet from the boy, not too close.

"I didn't know it was today," he said. "I'm sorry. I wouldn't have come."

"She cut his ear off once. With Nani's scissors. She was angry with me and she couldn't cut me, so she cut him." He touched the clumsy black stitches at the base of the ear. "Then she cried for three days and sewed it back herself. Badly."

"She sounds like she had a temper."

"She had everything," Rahul said. It came out very quietly, almost to the rabbit. "She was loud and she was funny and she was rude to everyone and she was scared of the dark and she wasn't scared of anything else. She used to fight me for the TV remote. She used to steal my Maggi." He stopped. His arms tightened round the rabbit. "She was supposed to be fifteen now."

After a while Rahul said, without looking at him: "Every year, on the fifth. A week before today. He made me go up on that stage." His voice was perfectly level. "Every year. The same speech. *My sister Pari was my best friend.* Written by the PR girl." His fingers moved in the rabbit's grey fur. "This year I couldn't say it. It was the first time I couldn't say it."

Sharda Devi gave Dev tea in the front room among the photographs. She had changed back into her ordinary grey cotton sari, and the heater glowed in its corner, and for a while neither of them said anything.

"You didn't know," she said at last.

"No, Mataji."

"Nobody tells the police anything." She almost smiled. "Forty years I taught in a government school. I know how it works."

"Will you tell me now?"

She looked at her tea.

"She was twelve," she said. "Three years ago today, she… took her own life. In that house. In the morning." Her voice did not break. It only became very careful, the way a person walks on ice. "Rajiv said it was exam pressure. The newspapers said it was exam pressure. The doctors wrote what they were told. Three months later there was a foundation with her name on it, and her photograph on a banner nine feet high, and everyone said, *what a father, what courage.*" Her hands had found the gold bangle again and were turning it. "Every year since, a week before her barsi, he has stood on a stage and talked about listening to children."

"What did you think it was, Mataji? If not exam pressure."

The bangle stopped.

"I have thought a great many things, Inspector," she said. "What good does thinking do, after?"

She got up, slowly, with a hand on the arm of her chair, and went to the almirah, and came back with a thick photograph album bound in faded red cloth. She sat down again and opened it on her knees and turned the stiff black pages, past weddings and school functions and a young Anjali on a scooter, until she came to the photograph she wanted, and turned the album so Dev could see.

Two children under the neem tree in summer. A boy of about nine, gap-toothed and sunburnt, his hair standing up in a crest. A girl of about seven, with a pink flower clip, squinting into the sun. Both of them were laughing, and both of them held up one hand to the camera with three fingers raised.

"Three," said Sharda Devi. "Always three. Look." She turned the pages. The same two children at a birthday party, three fingers each. On a train, three fingers. In front of the Bara Imambara, solemn in school uniforms, three fingers held up by their sides where the photographer might not notice. "It was their code. Pari taught it to me one day, laughing, as if it were a great secret. In the Vipul Khand house their rooms were side by side, upstairs, with one wall between. At night, after lights out, one of them would knock on that wall. Three times. *Thak, thak, thak.* And the other one would knock back." She put her finger on the girl's raised hand. "It meant, *I'm all right.* Three knocks. *Main theek hoon.*"

Dev looked at the photograph for a long time.

He was not seeing it. He was seeing a small window onto a lane, with yellow curtains half drawn, and a thin boy standing very close to a wall with his forehead almost touching it, raising his knuckles and knocking: once, twice, three times. And then tilting his head to listen, and waiting, and getting nothing back from the other side of the wall, because there was no one on the other side of the wall any more, and had not been for three years.

He had thought, on Sunday, that he had seen grief. He had not understood what he was seeing.

The boy had not been silent. He had been talking all this time. He had simply been talking to someone who could not answer.
