By Wednesday morning the whiteboard in the corner of the Crime Branch office had become a map of the evening of the fifth of December, and Kabir had stopped erasing anything.

He had drawn six columns in blue marker: RAJIV, NANDITA, VIVEK, MOHAN, RAHUL, SHARDA. Down the left side ran the hours, from three in the afternoon to ten at night, in fifteen-minute steps. Every entry had a small letter after it in red. **V** for verified by something that wasn't a person's word: a camera, a receipt, a call record, a bank log. **C** for claimed, meaning someone had said it and nobody had yet proved it. Over four days the V's had been multiplying like weeds in the Rajiv and Mohan and Vivek columns. In some places the C's had been crossed out and replaced, and in others they still stood alone.

Dev stood in front of it with a glass of tea going cold in his hand.

"Talk me through it," he said. "As if I'd never seen it."

Kabir uncapped a red marker.

"Rajiv. Home at five thirty-one, doorbell, V. Tea at five forty-five, Mohan's word, C. Calls Mohan into the study at six twenty-five, shows him the envelope, C. Calls Nandita at seven-twelve, four minutes, V. After that, nothing, until Nandita finds him at eight-forty. Siddiqui says dead between seven-thirty and eight-thirty. If I had to bet, I'd say just before eight. Vivek heard a phone ringing inside at seven fifty-four, and nobody answered it."

He moved the marker.

"Mohan. Leaves by the back gate at six-thirty, his word, C. Jeweller at seven forty-one to seven forty-three, camera and register, V. Dhaba at eight twenty-one, UPI, V. Back at nine-fifteen, C, but the SHO saw him come in. He can't have been in that study between seven-thirty and eight-thirty. Mohan is out." He drew a line through the column. "For the murder, anyway. Not for lying."

"Vivek."

"Leaves the café seven thirty-five, barista's word, C. Loan-shark call at seven thirty-three, V. Lane camera in at seven fifty-one, V. Doorbell seven fifty-three to seven fifty-six, rings, calls, begs, never goes in, V. Lane camera out at seven fifty-eight, V. Theka at Patrakarpuram at eight-ten, UPI, V." Kabir tapped the column. "Seven minutes in the lane, three of them on camera at the front door. The other four, he's driving down a lane with a speed-breaker. There's no time to get round the back, through the green belt, in by a gate he doesn't have a key to, kill a man, wash up at a tap and come back out. I tried walking it on Monday night. It's nine minutes just to get from his car to that tap the long way round." He drew a second line. "Vivek's a liar and a gambler and he's terrified, but he didn't go into that house."

"Sharda."

"At home in Aliganj with her tuition girls until seven, C. The girls' parents will confirm it if we ask, but sir, she's sixty-nine, she doesn't have a key to that house, Nandita says Rajiv never gave her one, and she'd have needed to cross half the city in a taxi that nobody saw." He did not draw a line through her name. He simply left it, as one leaves a thing that has not yet become a question.

"Nandita."

Kabir's marker hovered.

"Leaves the bank at seven thirty-one, access log, V. Told us eight-fifteen, so she lied. Phone silent from seven-sixteen to eight forty-four. Lane camera eight thirty-eight, V. Doorbell eight thirty-nine, V. In between…" He drew a long red bracket down the column, from 7:31 to 8:38, and wrote inside it in capitals: **67 MIN — ??**. "She has a key to the back gate on her house bunch. She knew about the envelope. She hid a four-minute phone call with him at seven-twelve. She lied to a school counsellor about bruises on a child. And she's the nominee on a two-crore term insurance policy, which Agnihotri was kind enough to mention to me yesterday."

"And Rahul."

Kabir capped the red marker and uncapped the blue one. He did not write anything new in Rahul's column. He only pointed at what was already there.

*18:14 — leaves by front door — doorbell — V.*
*18:30 → 21:10 — at Aditya Verma's, Indira Nagar — C.*
*20:55 — Nandita calls him; he says he is at Aditya's — V (call) / C (location).*
*21:25 — lane camera, walks in — V.*

"Two hours and fifty-five minutes," Kabir said. "And the only person who has told us where he was during them is him."

"Nandita confirmed it," Dev said. "She called him at eight fifty-five and he said he was at Aditya's."

"With respect, sir, she confirmed that he *said* he was at Aditya's. She didn't see him there. She heard his voice on a phone." Kabir turned from the board. "Every other person on this board, we've checked. Mohan's dhaba, Vivek's theka, the jeweller, the bank. We've checked the gambler and the servant and the wife. We haven't checked the boy."

Dev drank some of his cold tea and put the glass down on the windowsill.

"All right," he said. "Call the friend."

Kabir already had his phone out. He dialled the number Rahul had recited on Sunday from memory without checking. It went straight to a recorded voice. *The number you are calling is switched off.* He tried again. Switched off.

He found the number for the Shubh Residency society office on a property website and rang it. A guard answered, with a television playing behind him. Yes, the Vermas, Flat 304. No, nobody home. The whole family had gone on Saturday morning, early, in their car, for a cousin's wedding in Bareilly. The mother had left the keys with the society office for the maid. Back on Wednesday night, the mother said. Tonight, late.

Kabir put the phone down slowly.

"They left on Saturday morning," he said. "The morning after."

"People go to weddings, Kabir. It's December. Half of Lucknow is at a wedding."

"I can ask the Bareilly police to send a constable to the wedding venue. Take the boy's statement. One question. *Was Rahul Sethi at your flat on Friday evening between six-thirty and nine?* It'll take them an afternoon."

"A constable from a Bareilly thana walking into a wedding mandap to ask a seventeen-year-old about another seventeen-year-old in a murder case." Dev shook his head. "By tonight it'll be on every family WhatsApp group in two cities. And he's back tonight anyway. We'll see him tomorrow."

"Then the boy's phone." Kabir's voice had gone very level, the voice he used when he was trying not to raise it. "I can have a CDR on Rahul Sethi's number by this evening. Tower locations. If his phone was in Indira Nagar, it'll show Indira Nagar. If it wasn't…"

"No."

The word sat in the room between them.

"Sir?"

"No," Dev said again. He was looking out of the window at the courtyard of the Crime Branch building, where a constable was feeding a stray dog the crusts of his paratha. "Not yet."

"Why not?"

Dev did not answer straight away. When he did, his voice was quieter than usual.

"Because his father is four days dead, Kabir. His sister has been dead three years. He's living in his grandmother's barsati with his books stacked in piles and his sleeves pulled over his hands. And we have a woman who lied to us about sixty-seven minutes in the hour of the murder, who stands to receive two crore rupees, who has a key to that back gate. And you want to go after the boy's phone."

"I want to go after the truth, sir. Wherever it is."

"So do I."

"Then what's the difference?" Kabir came round the desk. "You saw his wrist. I saw you see it. You saw the way he looked when you mentioned the photograph. He calls his stepmother *aunty*. He doesn't say *Papa*. His alibi is a friend who has been unreachable since the morning after the murder. And the killer went out through the back gate with a key, and that boy has a key to the back gate." He stopped. "Sir, I'm not saying he did it. I'm saying we've checked everyone except him, and I don't understand why."

Dev turned from the window. For a moment Kabir thought he was going to be angry. Instead Dev looked at his own right hand, at the small white scar across the second knuckle that Kabir had noticed on their first day together and never asked about, and rubbed it absently with his thumb.

"There are some children, Kabir," he said, "for whom the police are just one more man who opens the door and walks in without knocking."

Kabir held his gaze.

"And there are some children, sir," he said, "who lie."

The stray dog in the courtyard barked once at a crow. Somewhere down the corridor a phone rang and rang and was picked up.

"Tomorrow," said Dev. "Aditya Verma, tomorrow morning, first thing. I'll go myself. Today, the sixty-seven minutes."

Kabir looked at him for a long moment. Then he turned back to the whiteboard, and under RAHUL, beneath the line that said *at Aditya Verma's — C*, he wrote in small blue capitals: **UNVERIFIED — Aditya away (Bareilly) till Wed night. CDR not requested.** He wrote the date and his initials after it, the way you write something into a case diary that you want to be able to point to later.

At eleven the DCP sent for Dev.

Anupama Singh had been a district SP in three districts before she came to Lucknow, and she ran her office in the East Zone like a well-made watch: everything on time, nothing on the desk that didn't need to be there. She had a framed photograph of herself with the Chief Minister on one wall and a child's crayon drawing of a police jeep on the other, and she did not invite Dev to sit.

"Inspector Rathore." She had the morning's papers fanned on her desk. *SAMAJ RATNA'S KILLER STILL AT LARGE.* *POLICE CLUELESS IN SETHI MURDER?* *Wife, Brother-in-law, Servant: Who Had the Key?* "Five days. Do you know how many times my phone has rung since Friday?"

"I can guess, madam."

"Don't guess. Count." She tapped the papers. "The Rotary Club. Two MLAs. The Chamber of Commerce. The principal of St. Aloysius. The Commissioner, twice." She looked up. "ACP Tripathi tells me the wife lied about where she was for over an hour during the time of death. She has a back-gate key. She's the insurance nominee. She knew where the cash was. She concealed a phone call with the victim. And she's refusing to explain the hour."

"All true, madam."

"Then what are you waiting for?"

"For her to tell us where she was."

"Then make her tell you." The DCP closed the newspapers. "Today, Inspector. Before the press finds out about the bank log. Because when they find out, and they will, they will want to know why a woman who lied about the hour of her husband's death is still sitting in her drawing room accepting condolences."

Nandita came to the Crime Branch at three, with a lawyer.

Mr. Harish Bhalla was a thin, elegant man in a black coat with a briefcase that clicked expensively, and he did most of the talking for the first twenty minutes, mainly about his client's grief, her cooperation and her rights. When Dev finally laid the bank's access log on the table between them, Nandita looked at it for a long time without touching it.

"Mrs. Sethi," Dev said. "You left the bank at seven thirty-one. You turned into your lane at eight thirty-eight. The drive takes twenty-five minutes, thirty in Friday traffic. That leaves about forty minutes. Where were you?"

"My client was not at her house," Bhalla said. "The doorbell camera shows that clearly. She arrived at eight thirty-nine."

"The front door camera shows she didn't use the front door. The back gate opens onto the green belt, which anyone can walk into from the riverfront road, and Mrs. Sethi has a key to it." Dev kept his eyes on Nandita. "I'm not accusing you, Mrs. Sethi. I'm asking you. Forty minutes. Tell me where you were, and if it's true, I'll prove it for you myself."

Nandita looked at the bank log. Then at Dev. Something moved behind her eyes, a struggle, a door being tried and found locked. She opened her mouth.

"I wasn't at home," she said. "I didn't go anywhere near that house. That's all I'm going to say."

"That isn't enough."

"It's all I'm going to say." Her voice cracked very slightly on the last word and then held. "Think whatever you want to think, Inspector. Everyone else always has."

Dev looked at her for a long time. He had sat across tables from a great many people who were lying to him, and he had learned that guilt and shame wear almost the same clothes. Both look at the table. Both clench their hands. Both say *that's all I'm going to say*. The difference was somewhere underneath, and he could not see it.

He could not tell which one this was.

Kabir rang Sana that evening from the steps of the Crime Branch building, where the signal was better and nobody could hear him. Sana taught Class 3 at a school in Mahanagar and was the only person he knew who could make a day sound good by describing it.

Tonight she told him about the Christmas play, and the boy who was supposed to be a sheep and refused to say *baa*, and the girl who had cried because her crown was the wrong colour. Then she was quiet for a moment.

"There's a boy in my class," she said. "Ayaan. Every time I raise my hand to write on the top of the board, he flinches. Every time. Like this." He heard her breath catch, imitating it. "I asked him about it today. He said it's nothing, Ma'am, it's a habit. He's eight, Kabir. What kind of eight-year-old has a habit like that?"

Kabir watched a rickshaw go past on the road below, its bell ringing.

"If you found out," he said slowly, "that a child was being hurt at home. And everyone knew. The neighbours, the school, the family. And nobody did anything. For years."

"Then I'd do something."

"And if that child grew up," Kabir said, "and then one day…"

He stopped.

"One day what?" said Sana.

"Nothing." He rubbed his eyes. "Long day. Tell me about the sheep again."

Dev ate dinner with his mother in Aliganj, six lanes from Sharda Devi's green gate, because it was Wednesday and he had promised.

She had made arhar dal and baingan bharta and kept the rotis hot in a cloth, and she talked about the neighbours' dog and the price of tomatoes and the cousin's daughter in Pune who still wasn't married, and then, with the casualness of a mother who has been waiting all evening to say something, she said:

"This Sethi case. The poor boy. His Nani is Sharda Behenji, you know. From Govt. Girls Inter College. She taught half this mohalla."

"I know, Ma."

"The granddaughter used to come every Sunday. Pari. You could hear her laughing three lanes away." His mother broke a roti in half and didn't eat it. "After she died, Behenji stopped going to the school. Took early retirement. Started teaching the girls at home for free. People said it was grief." She looked at her son. "People say a lot of things."

Dev ate his dal.

"Your father also used to say *discipline*," his mother said, very quietly, looking at her plate. "You remember? You used to wear full sleeves in May. Forty-four degrees, and you in full sleeves, so the teachers wouldn't see."

"Ma."

"I'm only saying." She put the roti down. "I'm only saying I know what a house sounds like when it's too quiet. I lived in one."

He did not answer her. After a while she got up and brought him more rotis, and they did not speak of it again.

---

It was past eleven when he got back to the Crime Branch. The building was dark except for the duty room and, at the end of the corridor, the light in their office, where Kabir was still at the whiteboard with a marker in his hand.

Kabir did not turn round when Dev came in. He had added something under RAHUL. Next to *at Aditya Verma's, Indira Nagar — C*, he had drawn a circle in red marker. Then he had gone over the circle again, so that it stood out from everything else on the board, a red ring round a single unproved sentence.

Dev stood beside him and looked at the circle.

Then he looked at the long red bracket in Nandita's column, **67 MIN — ??**, and at the newspapers on the desk, and at the DCP's *today* that had come and gone.

"First the sixty-seven minutes," he said.

Kabir capped the marker. He did not move away from the board.

"Sir," he said, without looking at Dev. "Do you know why you're leaving him for last?"

Dev did not answer. He switched off the light. In the sudden dark the whiteboard went grey, and the columns and the names and the times faded into it, all except the red circle in Rahul's column. For a moment it seemed to hold the last of the light from the corridor.
