He opened it on the morning of the twenty-sixth, at his kitchen table in Mahanagar, with the window grey with fog and a glass of tea going cold at his elbow. He had told himself he would wait until he knew what he was going to do with it. Then he understood that he would not know until he had read it, and that this was only another way of not looking.

The first page was the one he had already seen. *PROPERTY OF PARI SETHI. CLASS 6-B.* And under it, squashed in: *Agar mile toh lauta dena, warna Rahu-Ketu ko bata dungi.* If found, return it. Otherwise I'll tell Rahu-Ketu.

He turned the page.

---

It was not a diary. He had been afraid it would be, and it was not. It was the kind of notebook a twelve-year-old keeps for herself at her grandmother's house, half rough book and half secret, with no order to it at all. There were fractions in it, a page of them, worked in pencil and rubbed out and worked again, with a large red tick at the bottom in a different hand, an adult's hand, with *Shabash!* written beside it. Well done. Sharda Devi's, he thought. There was a list of SRK dialogues, numbered, with the best ones starred. *Rahul, naam toh suna hoga* had three stars and the word *ALWAYS* beside it. There was a page on which someone had practised a signature, over and over, getting more and more elaborate: *Pari Sethi. P. Sethi. Ms. Pari Sethi. Ms. P. Sethi, Principal.*

Then, on a page by itself, a drawing.

It was done in crayon, pressed hard into the paper so that the wax shone. A girl in a pink frock, with long black hair and a pink flower clip, stood on a strip of green grass beside a row of tall blue and yellow flowers, holding one of them as if she were about to give it to someone. Her smile went almost from ear to ear. In the top corner of the page was a sun, round and yellow, with rays like the spokes of a wheel. The sun had a face. It was smiling too.

Along the side of the page, in careful pencil joined-up writing, slanting uphill:

*I want to be a teacher when I grow up* ♥

Dev looked at the sun for a long time. He thought of another round sun, engraved in gold, that had been laid on a glass desk before every punishment in that house. He did not know whether the girl had ever thought of it when she drew hers. He thought probably not. He thought she had simply drawn a sun, because that was what you drew in the corner of a picture, and given it a face because she was the kind of child who gave things faces.

---

On the next page was a list.

*THINGS I WILL DO WHEN I AM BIG*

*1. Keep 3 dogs. (Names: Shahrukh, Kajol, Ladoo)*
*2. Mango ice cream for DINNER.*
*3. Teach in Nani's school. Class 3 or Class 4. NOT Maths.*
*4. Never make any child stand in the corner. EVER.*
*5. Buy Rahu-Ketu a new cricket bat because Papa broke his.*

He read the fifth line twice. He remembered a cricket bat pushed to the very back under a seventeen-year-old's bed in Vipul Khand, its handle snapped clean across and bound together again with far too much black electrical tape, by someone who could not mend it and could not throw it away.

The page after that was headed in red felt pen, underlined twice:

*RAHU-KETU'S ANNOYING HABITS*

*— Snores like a pressure cooker.*
*— Steals my Maggi. EVERY TIME.*
*— Says "Pari tu pagal hai" 100 times a day.*
*— Thinks he is Virat Kohli. He is NOT.*
*— Hides the remote under the cushion and then says "I don't know where it is."*

And under it, in much smaller writing, squeezed in at the very bottom of the page:

*But best bhaiya. Don't tell him.*

On the roof, Rahul had said that she stole *his* Maggi. Dev found that he believed them both.

---

There was a page with nothing on it except three dots, drawn in a row in black pen, each one gone over many times until it was nearly a hole in the paper. Underneath:

*Our code. Thak thak thak = main theek hoon.*

I'm all right.

There was a page, near the middle, headed *OFFICIAL BIODATA*, with a drawing of a white rabbit with one ear sticking up and one flopped over.

*Name: Gajar*
*Age: 8 years*
*Father's name: Gajar ka Halwa*
*Favourite food: Halwa (obviously)*
*Hobbies: Sleeping, listening*
*Best quality: He is a very good listener. He never tells anyone ANYTHING.*

Dev sat with that page open for a long time. The fog outside the window had begun to thin, and somewhere below a scooter would not start, and then did.

After the biodata there were some pages of homework, and a half-finished drawing of the neem tree in Sharda Devi's courtyard, and a page of hangman games with words he could not guess, and then a great many blank pages. He turned them one by one, the way you go on listening after a piece of music has stopped.

The last page with writing on it was about two-thirds of the way through.

There was no heading, and no drawing. Only two lines, in pencil, in the same careful joined-up writing as the line beside the girl in the pink frock. But the letters were smaller, and closer together, as if the hand had not wanted to take up any more room than it had to.

*Nani ne kaha Papa promise karenge.*
*Main wait karungi. — 19/11*

Nani says Papa will promise. I'll wait.

After that, the pages were empty all the way to the back cover.

---

He sat at the table until the tea was stone cold and the fog had gone and the sun was on the rooftops opposite, and he went over it the way he went over everything, in order, as if it were a file.

On the roof, Rahul had asked him: *He was lying, wasn't he? To hurt me?* And Dev had not answered, because he had not known. Now he knew. Rajiv Sethi had not been lying. In the last minutes of his life, he had told his son the one true thing that would hurt him most, and his son had picked up the trophy.

If Rahul read this page, he would know it too. He would know that his sister had gone to the one person in the world they both trusted, and asked, and been told to wait. That his Nani had sent her home at six o'clock, three Sundays running. That the woman who sat on the edge of his bed in the barsati with her hand an inch from his had known. He would be seventeen and a half, and he would have killed his father, and he would have no one.

Dev knew exactly what he was going to do, and he knew exactly what it was.

It was what Sharda Devi had done for three years. It was what Rajiv had done for ten, in the study with the door closed. A grown man sitting at a table deciding how much of a child's own life that child would be allowed to know. Dev did not tell himself it was different. He had stopped telling himself that on the morning he had not called the SJPU.

He got up and fetched the blade from his shaving kit. He opened the notebook flat at the last written page, pressed it down against the spiral with the side of his hand, and cut the page out close to the wire, slowly, in one long stroke. Then, with the point of the blade, he picked the little torn teeth of paper out of the spiral, one by one, until there was nothing left in the wire to show that a page had ever been there.

It took him nearly half an hour. His hands were steady the whole time.

He folded the page in half, and in half again, and went to the desk in the other room and put it in the drawer, under the blue box with his father's medal in it, beside the white envelope with *Mohan — Dec* written on the front in someone else's hand. Then he closed the drawer, and stood with his hand on it for a while, and went to wash his face.

On the twenty-seventh he drove to Barabanki.

Mohan Lal's house was at the end of a brick lane on the edge of a village off the Faizabad road, a two-room pucca house with a buffalo tied in front of it and a tulsi plant in a painted tin. Kamla, his wife, a thin, shy woman who covered her head when she saw the car, would not let Dev stand in the yard; she brought out the one plastic chair, and a glass of tea, and a plate of biscuits nobody touched. Guddi was home from her nursing college for the winter holidays. She stood in the doorway in a cardigan and watched the policeman with frank, unafraid suspicion, the way her father never had.

Mohan had not found another job. Nobody in Gomti Nagar would take on a servant whose name had been in the papers in a murder case, however it had ended.

"I'm going to a cold storage in Lucknow after Holi," he said. "Sukhiya's cousin. Watchman. It's all right." He sat on the edge of the string cot with his hands folded between his knees. "What has sahab come for?"

Dev took the envelope out of his inside pocket and held it out.

It was a plain brown envelope from the stationery shop by the Commissionerate. Inside were twenty five-hundred-rupee notes that Dev had drawn from an ATM in Hazratganj the evening before.

"Your December salary," he said. "It was owed."

Mohan looked at the envelope. He did not take it at once. Then he did, with both hands, the way he had once received a red cloth bundle across a jeweller's counter, and he held it on his knees and looked at it for a long time. The notes inside were new; Dev had not thought of that. They were stiff, and the envelope was brown, and the name on it was not written in anyone's hand at all.

"Sahab's envelope was always white," Mohan said quietly.

Dev said nothing.

Mohan raised his eyes to Dev's face. Whatever he saw there, he looked at it for a moment, the old servant who for nine years had turned on his radio in a kitchen, and then he lowered his eyes again. He did not ask. Dev watched him decide not to. It was the same look Dev had seen on Sharda Devi's face under the neem tree, and on Nandita's at the jail gate, and he had come to know it very well.

"Guddi's fees," Mohan said, and put the envelope inside his sweater. "The second year. Thank you, sahab."

On the way back to the car he asked, in the lane, with his hands still folded in front of him, "Rahul baba. Did he get the chain?"

"Yes. His Nani keeps it for him."

Mohan nodded slowly.

"That's good," he said. "Let it be with them."

On Sunday, the twenty-eighth, he took the notebook to Aliganj.

It was another kite afternoon, cold and blue, the sky over the rooftops thick with paper diamonds. Sharda Devi opened the green gate and looked at the plastic bag under his arm and then at his face, and stood aside to let him pass without a word. At the foot of the stairs she put her hand on his sleeve for a moment, very lightly, and took it away.

Rahul was on the roof, sitting on the parapet with the cheap wooden charkhi in his lap, winding pink manjha onto it from a new spool. He looked up when Dev came out onto the terrace, and something in his face moved that was not quite a smile and was no longer quite the opposite of one.

"She cuts my string every time," he said, holding up the reel. "Even now. Every time I fly it, it gets cut in about a minute. I think it's her."

Dev held out the bag.

"Your Nani had this," he said. "It was in Pari's room. She's kept it for three years. She asked me to give it to you."

Rahul put down the charkhi. He took the bag and opened it, and when he saw what was inside, he went very still. Then he took the notebook out and turned it over and read the cover, and Dev watched him read the second line, the squashed-in one. *Otherwise I'll tell Rahu-Ketu.*

He laughed.

It burst out of him before he could stop it, short and startled, the first time Dev had ever heard him laugh, and then he pressed his wrist against his mouth.

"She would have, too," he said through it. "She told me everything. She told on everyone, to everyone."

He sat on the parapet and read it, page by page, with the kites going over. Dev stood a little way off at the edge of the roof and looked at the sky, and listened. He heard the boy snort at the SRK dialogues. *Naam toh suna hoga.* He heard him say, to nobody, "I do *not* snore," and then, after a moment, more quietly, "I do." At the Maggi, he said, "*Liar*," with deep and outraged love. At the list of things she would do when she was big, he stopped making any sound at all, and Dev did not look round. He knew which line the boy had reached. He waited until he heard a page turn.

Then Rahul reached the page at the bottom of the annoying habits, the small writing squeezed in at the end, and he did not turn the page after it for a very long time.

When Dev looked round at last, the boy was sitting with the notebook open on his knees and his head bowed over it, and he was crying, without any sound, the way a person cries who learned a long time ago to do it quietly. Dev looked back at the kites. A red one was climbing very high over the next lane, higher than all the others, its string singing.

After a while the pages began to turn again.

He heard the boy reach the three dots, and stop, and Dev knew without looking that he had put his finger on them. He heard the rabbit's biodata, and a small sound that might have been a laugh or might not. He heard the blank pages begin, one after another, more and more slowly, all the way to the back cover. Then he heard Rahul turn back to the middle and run his thumb down the spiral, along the wire, the way a person checks a thing to be sure there is nothing more.

There was nothing there. Dev had been careful.

"Is this all of it?" Rahul said.

"That's what your Nani had," said Dev.

It was true. It was the kind of true thing a policeman learns to say, and he had said a great many of them in twenty years, to a great many people. He had never minded before.

Rahul closed the notebook and held it on his knees with both hands, the way he had held the rabbit at the barsi and the red cloth on this same roof.

"Someone knocks back now," he said. "At night. On her wall. From Nani's side." He did not look at Dev. "Since last Sunday. She thinks I don't know it's her."

Dev did not trust himself to say anything.

"I used to think, if anyone ever knocked back, it would stop. The noise. The second time, and the doorbell." The boy's voice was quiet and level. "It doesn't stop. It's there every night."

"No," said Dev. "I don't think it will stop."

Rahul thought about that, seriously, the way he thought about everything.

"Good," he said at last. "It shouldn't."

He looked out over the rooftops for a while. Then, without turning his head, he asked the question Dev had known for four days he would one day ask, and had hoped every one of those days that he would not.

"On the roof. That day. I asked you if he was lying. About Nani." His hands tightened on the notebook. "You didn't say anything."

The kites went over. Down in the courtyard, a steel glass was set on a counter, and then there was no sound at all from below, as if someone was standing very still at the foot of the stairs.

"Your Nani loves you," Dev said, "more than anyone alive."

It was true too.

Rahul looked at him for a long moment. Then he nodded slowly, as if that were the answer, and perhaps for him, now, it was. He picked up the charkhi and wound a few more turns of pink thread onto it, and held the notebook against his chest with his other arm, and did not ask again.

In January, a message came from Kabir. It was the first in three weeks, and it was very short.

*Ayaan's father called before the CWC today. Counselling ordered, family under supervision. Mother made a statement. Sana went with her. Thought you'd want to know.*

Dev read it several times. He typed *Thank you*, and then deleted it, and then typed it again and sent it. Kabir did not reply. On the twelfth of February a wedding card came to the Crime Branch office, addressed in Kabir's small blue capitals: *Kabir & Sana*, with a pair of painted shehnai on the front. Dev went, and stood at the back of the shamiana with his hat in his hands, and when the groom looked up from the stage across three hundred guests and saw him, Kabir held his eyes for a moment, and nodded once, and looked away.

---

The final report in the murder of Rajiv Sethi went before the Chief Judicial Magistrate in the second week of March. Inspector Rawat had written it with great care. It set out four volumes of investigation in eleven pages, recorded that despite every effort the person or persons responsible could not be identified on the evidence available, and asked that the case be closed as untraced, subject to being reopened if fresh evidence came to light. The magistrate accepted it. In a different courtroom, the same week, in a hearing that lasted four minutes, Nandita Sethi was discharged.

The morning papers gave it two paragraphs on page seven. The arrest had been on page one.

In the same week, the Pari Sethi Foundation announced, in a notice of three lines, that its annual *Suno* ceremony would not be held this year. It gave no reason. Nobody asked for one.

Dev's censure entry went onto his annual record. He was still at the Crime Branch, on files that other people had finished with, and he did not mind them as much as he had expected. On Wednesdays he ate dinner with his mother in Aliganj. Sometimes, walking back to the main road afterwards, he went six lanes out of his way and passed a green iron gate with the paint peeling off it in long curls, and did not stop.

---

On the last night of March, he opened the drawer again.

The blue box. The white envelope, its corners soft, the seal still closed, *Mohan — Dec* in a dead man's careful blue hand. And the page, folded in four.

He unfolded it under the lamp and read it again. *Nani ne kaha Papa promise karenge. Main wait karungi.* Two lines, in a twelve-year-old's pencil, smaller than the rest, as if she had not wanted to take up any room.

He did not burn it. He had thought, more than once, that he would. But it was the last thing she had written in the world, and it was the only true account of what had happened in that house that anyone had ever set down, and he found he could not be the one to destroy it, any more than her grandmother could. So he would keep it. That was all. He would keep it as Sharda Devi had kept the notebook, and Rahul had kept the envelope, and Mohan had kept the chain in a trunk under his folded dhotis on the night of the murder: a thing he could neither give away nor put down.

He thought of all of them, the whole long line of people who had known some part of the truth about the Sethi house and decided what to do with it. Mohan with his radio. Nandita with her television. A neighbour passing kulfi over a wall. A school with a report in a drawer. A grandmother who sent a child home at six o'clock. A boy who did not knock back. A detective on a terrace who chose. Every one of them had been a witness. Every one of them had decided how much of what they knew the world would be allowed to hear.

Pari had been the first. She had told the truth to the only two people she trusted, in a kitchen doorway and on a phone with her father breathing beside her, and they had told her to wait. And she had written it down, in pencil, in a notebook with a cat on the cover, and waited.

He folded the page in four and put it back beside the envelope, under the blue box with his father's medal, and closed the drawer. It stuck an inch from the end, the way it always did, and he had to push it shut with the heel of his hand.

Out over the city the last lights were going off, one by one, in the windows of people who would never know any of it.

It is never the first witness, Dev thought, who decides what the world will hear.

It is always the last.
