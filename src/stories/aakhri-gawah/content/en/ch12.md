"He's a child in conflict with the law, sir," Kabir said. "Or he's about to be."

They were in the car outside Dev's building in Mahanagar on Sunday afternoon, with the engine running and neither of them reaching for the gear lever.

"If you go there and he says anything, anything at all, it has to be done properly. His guardian present. The Special Juvenile Police Unit informed. A Child Welfare Police Officer in the room. No pressure, no threats, no promises, nothing in a police station lock-up. You know the rules better than I do." Kabir's hands were tight on the steering wheel. "If he talks to you alone on a terrace, it won't stand up anywhere. And it'll be you who did it."

"I'm going to give him a chain, Kabir. His grandmother has to sign the release. That's all."

"And if he talks?"

Dev looked out of the window at the Sunday street: a man washing a scooter, a girl on a bicycle with a kite under one arm, the sky above the rooftops already speckled with paper diamonds.

"Then tomorrow we'll do everything properly," he said. "I give you my word."

Kabir laughed. It was not a happy sound.

"Your word, sir," he said. "I've been collecting those all week."

But he put the car into gear.

In Lucknow, the winter Sundays belong to kites. By four o'clock the sky over Aliganj was full of them, red and green and purple and paper-white, dipping and climbing over the rooftops, their strings invisible against the blue. From every terrace came the shouts of boys and grown men as a line was cut: *wo kaata!* There it goes. A loose kite went drifting over the lane, trailing its string, and three small boys ran after it with their heads tipped back.

Kabir stopped the car at the end of the lane and turned off the engine.

"I'll be here," he said. "Right here, sir."

Sharda Devi opened the green gate before Dev reached it. She had changed back into her grey cotton sari, and the marigold garland had been taken off Pari's photograph and the photograph taken indoors. Only the dark patches on the bricks where the diyas had stood showed that anything had happened in the courtyard two nights before.

"Inspector." She looked at his face, and whatever she saw there made her stand very still. "You said a small matter of property."

"Yes, Mataji." He took the red cloth bundle from his inside pocket. "Rajiv's chain. The jeweller had it for repair; Mohan collected it on the night he died. It's been verified, and it isn't evidence. It belongs to the family. Mohan has asked that it go to Rahul, and Nandita-ji has signed a no-objection. Because Rahul is a minor, I need you to sign for him."

He held out the bundle and the folded release memo. Sharda Devi looked at the red cloth and did not take it. The colour went out of her face so completely that for a moment Dev thought she would fall, and he moved, but she put a hand out to the neem tree and steadied herself.

"That chain," she said.

"Yes."

She sat down on her low stool under the tree, slowly, as if her knees had decided for her. Dev unfolded the memo on his notebook and gave her his pen. Her hand shook so badly that her signature came out in pieces, *Sharda Devi* in three separate attempts, with *guardian (maternal grandmother)* written beside it in the small, upright Hindi of a woman who had marked forty years of homework.

"He's on the roof," she said, without looking up. "In the barsati. He's been there since this morning." She handed back the pen. "You go. I'll make tea."

She did not get up to make it. She sat on the stool under the neem tree with her hands in her lap and watched him go up the stairs, and Dev felt her eyes on his back all the way.

---

Rahul was sitting on the low parapet at the edge of the roof with his back to the barsati, looking out over the rooftops at the kites. He was not flying one. There was a reel of string on the parapet beside him, a cheap wooden charkhi with a few loops of pink manjha still on it, the glass-coated thread glittering in the low sun. It looked old. Dev thought it might once have been someone else's.

The boy turned at the sound of footsteps on the stairs, and saw who it was, and turned back to the sky.

Dev walked across the roof and stood a few feet away from him. Below them, three terraces over, a man in a vest let out a whoop as his green kite cut a red one, and the red one fell away, spinning, over the lane.

"Rahul."

Nothing.

"I've brought something for you. It's your father's. Mohan asked that you have it, and your Nani has signed for it."

He held out the red cloth bundle.

For a long moment Rahul did not move. Then he took it, because it was being held out to him and he had been taught all his life to take what he was given. He held it in both hands on his knees, the way he had held the rabbit two nights before, and looked at it.

"What is it?"

"Open it."

The boy pulled the string. The knot came loose. He folded back one corner of the red cloth, then another, and the late sun found the gold inside and lit it up like something on fire.

Rahul stopped breathing.

Dev saw it happen. The boy's chest stopped moving. His fingers, which had been holding the cloth, went rigid, and then they began to shake: slightly at first, then more, so that the cloth trembled and the chain shifted in it and the links made a small dry sound against each other.

He picked it up. Dev did not think he meant to. His hand simply went into the cloth and closed round the chain and lifted it, and the medallion came up out of the red folds and swung there in the air between them, turning slowly in the light, its engraved sun flashing and dimming and flashing as it turned.

Rahul stared at it. His lips moved. Dev could not hear what they said. Later, he would think it had been the first half of a sentence that the boy had been made to say a great many times as a child, with his hand on that medallion, and had never been able to forget.

Then Rahul opened his hand and let it fall.

The chain hit the terrace tiles. It made a sound, a small, clear, metallic *chhan*, gold on stone, and the medallion bounced once and settled face up between his feet.

The boy flinched from the sound as if it had been a slap.

He came off the parapet in one movement, backwards, away from the thing on the floor, the way a person moves away from a snake, and stood with his back against the barsati wall and his fists clenched at his sides. He was breathing again now, hard and fast and shallow. His face had gone white and then red, and he was staring at the chain on the tiles as if it might move.

"Why did you bring this?" His voice was raw. "Why did you bring this *here*?"

"Because Mohan said it should be yours."

"I don't *want* it." He took a step forward, and Dev thought he was going to kick it off the roof, and then he did something else. He dropped to his knees beside it and slammed his open palm down on the medallion, flat and hard, as if to stop it ringing. He held his hand there, pressing down, his whole arm shaking, his head bowed.

"Stop," he said to it. "Stop. Stop."

The sound had stopped long ago. He went on pressing.

Dev waited.

It was the hardest thing he did that day. He stood on the roof in the cold sun with the kites going overhead and the shouts of *wo kaata* coming from the other terraces, and watched a seventeen-year-old boy kneel on the tiles with his hand pressed over a gold medallion. He did not move towards him and he did not speak. He had learned a long time ago that some silences have to be allowed to finish on their own.

After a while, Rahul's arm stopped shaking. He did not lift his hand.

"Rahul," Dev said quietly. "I went to Indira Nagar yesterday."

The boy's back went very still.

"Aditya told me the truth. You didn't go to his flat on Friday. You messaged him at six-twenty to say you were coming, and at six fifty-two to say you weren't. And on Saturday morning, at fourteen minutes past nine, you asked him to say you had been there." Dev kept his voice level. "He's a good friend. He didn't want to lie. His mother didn't let him."

Nothing. A kite string brushed the edge of the parapet and was jerked away.

"I was by the river," Rahul said to the floor. "I walked. By the Gomti. I didn't want to go to Aditya's. I didn't want to go anywhere. That's all."

"For some of the time, yes. Your phone was on the riverfront tower from six forty-one until seven thirty-five." Dev paused. "And then from seven thirty-eight until four minutes past eight it was on the tower in your own lane. Three hundred metres from your house."

The hand on the medallion pressed down harder.

"You left the house at six-fourteen in a grey hoodie. The doorbell camera recorded you. You came back at nine twenty-five in a navy sweater. The camera recorded that too." Dev let a moment pass. "Where's the hoodie, Rahul?"

Rahul's other hand went to his cuff and pulled it down over his fingers, hard, until the knuckles showed white through the cloth.

"The killer came in through the back gate with a key," Dev said, "and went out the same way, and washed their hands at the garden tap and left it running. Mohan told us the first night who has keys to that gate. One of them is yours. The one for your cycle."

Silence.

"Whoever it was took one thing. An envelope from the top right drawer of the desk, with *Mohan — Dec* written on it. It was the fifth of the month. You'd been made to stand in that study on the fifth of every month for years and watch him count that money out."

The boy's shoulders rose and fell.

Rahul made a sound. It was not a word. It was the sound of something being pushed back down inside a chest.

The boy's eyes closed.

For a long time nobody said anything. On the next roof but one, two little girls were trying to get a kite up and failing, and their father was laughing at them. Somewhere a pressure cooker whistled. The sun was going down behind the water tanks, and the light on the terrace had turned from gold to orange.

Then Rahul lifted his head, and his face was wet, and furious.

"Have you ever been in that house?" he said. "Have you ever been in that house *at night*?"

"No."

"Then you don't know." His voice cracked and kept going. "You don't know anything. Everybody knew. Do you understand? *Everybody.* Mohan bhaiya knew. He used to turn the radio on. Nandita aunty knew, she used to turn the TV up. Khanna aunty next door knew, she used to give Pari kulfi over the wall and tell her not to tell. The school knew, they had a report, and he went to the principal and the report went away. Nani—" He stopped, and something flickered in his face and was gone. "Everybody knew. For years. And nobody did *anything*. Nobody ever did *anything*. And now *you* come here, with *that*," he jerked his chin at the chain under his hand, "and ask me where I was?"

"Yes," said Dev. "I'm sorry. I do."

"Why?"

"Because a woman who didn't kill your father is in Lucknow Jail."

The boy's face changed.

It was not a large change. It was something like what had happened on the stairs on Wednesday night, when Dev had told him Nandita had been arrested: a flicker, then a closing. But this time the closing did not quite work. Dev watched it fail.

"She's still there?" Rahul said. "Since Wednesday?"

"Since Wednesday. She's in judicial custody for two weeks. Her bail comes up tomorrow."

"Because of me." It came out almost as a whisper. "Because of… of what I said? That I was at Aditya's?"

"Because she lied to us about sixty-seven minutes on Friday night and she won't tell us where she was. And because we were looking in the wrong place." Dev paused. "Because I was looking in the wrong place. For a week. I didn't want to look at you."

Rahul stared at him.

"Why not?"

Dev was quiet for a moment. Then he did something he had not done in twenty years of police work. He took off his glasses, folded them and put them in his pocket, and held up his right hand so the boy could see the small white scar across the second knuckle.

"My father had a belt," he said. "He was a head constable, in Saharanpur. He said *discipline* too. I wore full sleeves in May." He lowered his hand. "I looked at you on the stairs that night and I saw a boy who'd been hurt. I didn't want to see anything else."

Rahul looked at the scar. Then at Dev's face, without the glasses. Then down at his own hand, still flat over the gold medallion on the tiles.

Slowly, he lifted the hand away. The chain lay there in the orange light, the engraved sun face up, the links pooled round it.

"She didn't do anything," Rahul said. His voice had gone very quiet and very steady. "Nandita aunty. She never did anything. That was what she did wrong. She never did anything, not once, not for Pari, not for me." He swallowed. "But she didn't do this."

He looked up at the sky, at the last kites going down over Aliganj, at the dark shapes of the water tanks against the orange. A loose kite was drifting very high up, cut free, going nowhere, trailing its string.

When he spoke again, it was to the sky, not to Dev.

"*Maine hi unhe maara.*"
