The meeting in the DCP's office began at six on Wednesday evening, and by seven the case against Nandita Sethi was standing up on its own.

Nobody built it dishonestly. That was what Dev would remember afterwards, when he went back over it in the long nights that followed. Nobody planted anything, or invented a witness, or bent a fact until it broke. Every piece they laid on the DCP's polished desk was true. It was only the order they laid them in, and the shape the order made, that was wrong.

ACP Rakesh Tripathi set it out, because he was senior and because he was good at it. He was a precise, grey-haired officer who had spent twelve years in the economic offences wing before coming to Lucknow, and he spoke about murder the way he had once spoken about forged balance sheets: one column at a time.

"Seven-twelve. The victim phones his wife. Four minutes. She conceals it. We don't know what was said, but we know she didn't want us to know it was said." A tick on his notepad. "Seven thirty-one. She leaves the bank. She tells us eight-fifteen. A lie. Sixty-seven minutes during which she refuses, even with counsel present, to say where she was." Another tick. "The doorbell camera rules out the front door. The killer came in by the back gate, using a key, and left the same way, locking it behind them. Mrs. Sethi has a back-gate key on her house bunch. She lives here; she knows the green belt; she knows the service lane on the riverfront road where you can leave a car without passing the colony camera."

"So does the servant," said Dev.

"The servant was in Aminabad at seven forty-two and eight twenty-one. You proved that yourself, Inspector." Tripathi did not look up. "The killer struck the victim from behind or the side, twice, with an object from his own desk. No defence wounds. He did not raise a hand. Dr. Siddiqui says he either didn't see it coming or didn't believe it was coming. Who does a man sit with at his desk, his back half turned, without believing anything could happen?"

Nobody answered. Tripathi made another tick.

"The killer took one thing. The servant's salary envelope. Not the watch, not the wallet. One envelope with *Mohan* written on it. And within minutes of the police arriving, who told the SHO that Mohan had stolen from that drawer before?" He looked up at last. "She did. Before we'd asked. She pointed us at the servant on the first night."

"Her prints are on the drawer pull," SHO Tiwari added from his chair by the wall. "Right-hand top drawer. Fresh."

"She told us she pulled it open further when she saw the money was missing," said Kabir.

"So she says." Tripathi turned a page. "Motive. Two crore in term insurance, she's the sole nominee. The house. A share of a business worth a great deal more. A husband who was publicly threatening her brother with ruin over eighteen lakh rupees." He slid a printout across the desk. "And this. Cyber cell recovered it from the victim's laptop yesterday. An unsent draft in his email, addressed to his family lawyer, dated the first of December."

The DCP read it aloud. "*Need to discuss terms for N. Discreetly. Nothing before the 15th, the foundation anniversary. Please prepare options.*" She put it down. "Terms for N."

"A man planning to divorce his wife quietly after a public event," said Tripathi. "And a wife who stands to lose everything in a divorce, and gain everything as a widow."

"Did she know about the draft?" asked Dev.

"We'll ask her."

"And her brother?" said the DCP. "At the door at seven fifty-three?"

"Either coincidence," said Tripathi, "or a lookout who lost his nerve. I don't need him to have gone in. I note that the first person Mrs. Sethi telephoned after the police was her brother, not the victim's son." He closed his notepad. "Add to all of this a documented pattern of concealment. She lied to a school counsellor about injuries to a child. She is still lying, today, about what went on in that house. *Ghar ki baat ghar mein.*" He gave the phrase a dry, distasteful weight. "A woman who has practised silence for eight years is a woman who knows how to keep one more."

The DCP looked at Dev.

"Inspector? You've been very quiet."

Dev had been looking at the printout of the unsent email. *Terms for N.* He thought of Nandita's hand stopping an inch from the silver frame. He thought of her sitting in her car in the driveway every evening for two minutes, sometimes five, before she could make herself go in.

"It fits," he said.

"That's not what I asked."

"It fits everything we have, madam." He took off his glasses and folded them. "I'd like to know where she was for those sixty-seven minutes before we put a name on it."

"So would I," said the DCP. "And she won't tell us. She's had five days and a lawyer." She looked at each of them in turn. "We have reasonable grounds. We have lies on record, opportunity, access, motive, and conduct after the fact. ACP sahab?"

"Arrest," said Tripathi. "Produce her before the magistrate tomorrow, ask for police custody. Two days. Put the evidence to her properly. Search for the money and the clothes she wore. If she has an explanation for the sixty-seven minutes, custody is where people usually remember it."

The DCP looked at Dev one more time.

He could have said no. He could have said *give me twenty-four hours, give me the boy's friend, give me the boy's phone*. He thought about it afterwards, many times. He did not say it, partly because the case in front of him was strong, and partly because a part of him, the part that rubbed the scar on his knuckle when he was tired, did not want to look anywhere else.

"Yes, madam," he said.

They arrested Nandita Sethi at a quarter to nine that night in her drawing room in Vipul Khand.

There were two women constables and SI Mamta Chauhan from the women's police station, and Dev, and Kabir. Somebody had talked, because by the time the police vehicles turned into the lane there were already three motorcycles at its mouth and a man with a camera standing on Ramvilas's plastic chair. Ramvilas stood beside him, wringing his cap in his hands.

Nandita was sitting where she had sat every evening that week, under the brass Ganesh, in her plain cotton suit. Vivek was with her. When Dev read out the grounds of arrest she did not interrupt and she did not cry. She listened to the whole thing with her hands folded in her lap, like a woman hearing the terms of a loan.

Vivek was the one who shouted.

"You can't do this! She didn't do anything! She was— Didi, tell them where you were! Just tell them! Whatever it is, it can't be worse than this!"

"Vivek." Nandita did not raise her voice. "Call Bhalla. Then go home and sleep."

"Didi—"

"*Go home.*"

She stood up. She went to the console table and took off her silver earrings, one and then the other, and laid them beside the Ganesh, and looked at them there for a moment as if they belonged to someone else. Then she turned to Dev.

"Inspector. Will you tell Rahul? Before he sees it on his phone." She hesitated. "And his Nani. Tell her yourself. Please. Not on the phone."

"I'll tell them."

"Thank you."

She walked out between the women constables with her head up. At the top of the front steps the doorbell's blue ring woke and recorded her, as it had recorded her coming home five nights earlier. Flashes went off at the mouth of the lane like summer lightning. Upstairs in the dark house, in the window of the room above the study, a curtain moved. Then Dev remembered there was nobody left in the house to move it, and that it had been the draught from the open front door.

The court of the Chief Judicial Magistrate sat in the old Collectorate complex, a sprawl of yellow colonial buildings and newer concrete annexes, with lawyers in black coats moving through its corridors like crows, typists clacking at tables under the trees, and a tea stall at the gate that had been there longer than most of the judges.

Nandita was produced at eleven on Thursday morning, within the twenty-four hours the law required. She sat on a wooden bench with SI Chauhan beside her and looked at nothing. Bhalla argued for twenty minutes, standing very straight in his black coat. There was no forensic evidence connecting his client to the scene beyond the ordinary traces of a woman in her own house. There was no witness. There was no weapon in her possession, no blood on her clothes. The police had a gap in a timeline and a lie about it, and a gap and a lie were not a murder.

The assistant prosecutor answered with Tripathi's columns: the call, the lie, the bank log, the key, the envelope, the insurance, the draft email, the refusal.

The magistrate was a tired woman in her fifties with half-moon spectacles who had heard ten thousand remand applications and believed perhaps a third of them. She looked at Nandita over her spectacles for a long moment.

Dev sat in his car outside the court complex long after dark, with the engine off and the windows fogging, and the file open on his knees.

It was a good case. He knew good cases. He had sent men to prison on less. Every piece of it was true. Every fact Tripathi had laid on the DCP's desk would stand up under cross-examination, and Bhalla, for all his elegance, would struggle to knock any one of them down. The call. The lie. The bank log. The key. The envelope. The insurance. The draft. The silence.

It fitted. That was what troubled him. It fitted everything.

He turned the pages until he came to the photographs from the study, and stopped at the one he had been avoiding all day.

The silver frame, face down on the middle shelf, between a crystal award and a leather-bound annual report. Photographed from the front, from the side, from above. The field photographer had included a scale ruler in one shot, as if the size of the thing might matter.

Nandita Sethi had not touched that photograph in three years. Rajiv had not allowed it. When she found it face down on Friday night with her husband dead on the floor behind her, she had reached out to set it upright. Her instinct had been to turn Pari back towards the room. She had stopped only because a young man on the phone told her not to touch anything.

Whoever had laid it face down had wanted the opposite. They had done the one careful, gentle thing in that whole violent room. They had turned the girl's face away so that she would not see. You do that for someone whose eyes still matter to you. You do it because you cannot stand to be looked at by them.

Nandita could not bear to look at Pari. He had seen that in her, in the way she said *I loved Pari* and then could not go on. But this was not someone who could not bear to look at Pari.

This was someone who could not bear Pari looking at them.

And there was the trophy. Wiped clean, so carefully that Mishra had noticed the absence of dust. But Nandita's fingerprints on her husband's trophy would have meant nothing. She lived in that house. Anyone who lived in that house could have explained a print on that trophy. Mohan dusted it. Rajiv polished it. Rahul had probably carried it in from the car the day he got it. Wiping it clean was not the act of a calculating woman who managed other people's money for a living. It was the act of someone in shock, scrubbing at a thing because it was the thing their hands had held.

And the tap, left running by the back gate. And the Rolex, left on the dead man's wrist by a killer who had supposedly staged a robbery.

The case against Nandita Sethi was a careful case about a careful woman. Nothing about that room had been careful, except one thing.

Dev closed the file. Through the fogged windscreen the lights of the court complex blurred into soft gold smears, and somewhere a lawyer's scooter coughed and started and went away down the road.

The answer fitted everything, he thought, except a dead girl's photograph.

And that was the one thing in the room that had been done with love.
