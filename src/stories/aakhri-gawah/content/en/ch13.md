The sun went down behind the water tanks, and the cold came up off the bricks the way it does in Lucknow in December, all at once, as if someone had opened a door.

Dev did not move. Rahul sat on the terrace floor with his back against the barsati wall and his knees drawn up and the chain lying on the tiles in front of him, and the light went from orange to grey around them both.

"Do you want your Nani here?" Dev said at last. "You have the right to have her with you. You have the right not to say anything else to me at all."

"No." Very fast. "Not Nani. Don't tell Nani. Not yet." Rahul wiped his face with the heel of his hand. "You said you'd tell me the truth. On the first day. You said you were sorry about my father. Were you?"

"I was sorry for you."

The boy considered that. Then he nodded, as if it were the right answer, or at least an honest one.

"I'll tell you," he said. "All of it. Then you can do whatever you have to do." He looked at Dev. "Don't promise me anything. Please. Everybody always promised Pari things."

"I won't promise you anything," said Dev. "I'll listen."

He did not take out his notebook. He did not take out his phone. Afterwards, Kabir would ask him why, and he would not have an answer that he liked.

---

"She was five when Mummy died," Rahul said. "I was seven. I don't remember much from before. I remember Mummy's sari smelled of Pond's powder. I remember the kitchen being noisy. I remember we had a dog for a while, and then we didn't." He was looking at the chain. "After, everything got quiet. And correct. That's what Nani calls it. *Correct.*"

He told it in pieces, not in order, the way memory comes.

He told about the rules. Shoes in a line by the door, toes to the wall. Marks on the fridge. Phone in the study by eight. No closed doors upstairs, so he took the locks off. No crying at the table; if you cried at the table, you stood in the study until you stopped. No drawings, after Pari turned eleven, because drawing was a waste of time for a girl who couldn't do fractions.

He told about the study.

"He'd call you in. Not shout. He never shouted. He'd say your name from the study, quietly, and you'd hear it from anywhere in the house. *Rahul.* And you'd go in, and he'd say *close the door*, and you'd close it. And then he'd stand up, and take off the chain."

He did not look at the chain on the floor while he said it.

"Slowly. Over his head. And he'd put it down on the glass. And it made that sound." His mouth twisted. "You heard it just now. That sound. Every time I hear anything like that, a spoon on a plate, keys on a table, my whole body… And then he'd talk. Very calmly. About how much you'd disappointed him. And then…" He stopped. "And then whatever it was going to be."

"How old were you, the first time?"

"Eight. Pari was six." He was quiet for a moment. "That was the first time he made us swear. Both of us, together. He held out the medallion, and we had to put our hands on it, and say it. *Is chain ki kasam. Ghar ki baat ghar mein.*" He said the words flatly, the way a person recites a prayer he no longer believes in. "Pari couldn't say it. She was crying too much. He made her start again. Three times."

Dev said nothing. He thought of Mohan in the Crime Branch office on Thursday, telling the same story from the other side of the study door.

---

"She invented the knock," Rahul said.

For the first time something came into his voice that was not flat. It was very small, and it was almost warm.

"After that first time. She was six. That night she came into my room in her nightdress with Gajar, and said, *Rahu-Ketu.*" He nearly smiled. "That's what she called me. Rahu-Ketu. Because she said I was two bad planets in one boy. She said, *when we're in our rooms and we can't come out, if you're OK, you knock three times on the wall. Thak thak thak. And I'll knock back. And then we'll both know.*" He looked at his knuckles. "She was six. She thought of that."

"Your Nani showed me the photographs. The three fingers."

"Nani knew?" A flicker of surprise. "She told Nani. Of course she did. She told Nani everything." Then the flicker went out, and something else replaced it, something he pushed away. "She wasn't… don't think she was some sad little angel. She was horrible. She was the most annoying person in the world. She stole my Maggi. Every single time. She'd wait till I'd made it and then come and stand in the kitchen door and say *ek bite* and then eat half. She fought me for the remote every night. She told on me to Nani when I bunked tuition. She used to do impressions of me. Badly. Walking like this." He hunched his shoulders and dragged his feet, and for one second Dev saw the boy he must have been. "*I am Rahul Sethi, I am very serious, I am going to be an engineer.*" He let his shoulders drop. "She flew kites up here every Sunday. That charkhi was hers." He nodded at the cheap wooden reel on the parapet. "She was terrible at it. She'd shout at me, *dheel de, dheel de, Rahu-Ketu!* and then get her string cut in about thirty seconds and scream at the boy on the next roof like she was going to kill him."

He stopped smiling.

"She wanted to be a teacher. Like Nani. In a government school. She said in her class nobody would ever have to stand in the corner. Nobody would ever be scared of the teacher." He was quiet. "She was twelve."

---

"Tell me about that night," Dev said gently. "Three years ago."

Rahul was silent for so long that Dev thought he would not answer.

"I was angry with her," he said.

He said it to the chain on the floor.

"That's the thing nobody knows. Not Nani, not anybody. I was angry with her. That day, the eleventh, he found out I'd bunked maths tuition to play cricket in the park, and he found out because he asked Pari, and she told him. He made her. I know he made her. He used to do that, ask one of us about the other one, in the study, with the chain on the desk. But I didn't think about that then. I only thought, *she told.*" He swallowed. "So that night it was my turn. Two hours in the study, standing. And afterwards…" He gestured at his own body without finishing. "And then I went to bed and I was so angry with her. I thought, I'm not going to talk to her tomorrow. I'm not going to talk to her for a week."

His voice went very quiet.

"And then she knocked. On the wall. *Thak, thak, thak.*"

The terrace was almost dark now. Below, someone in the lane was calling a child in for dinner.

"I didn't knock back," Rahul said. "I lay there and I heard it and I didn't knock back. Because I was angry. Because I wanted her to feel bad. Because I thought there'd be tomorrow." His hands were clenched on his knees. "She knocked again. A bit later. Three times. Softer. And I put the pillow over my head."

He did not say anything for a while.

"In the morning she didn't wake up," he said finally. "That's all. I won't… that's all. The house was full of people. Ambulance. Him on the phone in the hall, his voice so calm. Nobody let me go into her room. By the evening he was telling everyone it was exam pressure. Three months later there was a foundation. And a banner. And every year, a week before, he made me stand up and say she was my best friend." He looked at Dev with wet, dry, terrible eyes. "She was. She was my best friend. And the last thing she ever asked me was whether I was all right, and I didn't answer her."

Dev could not speak. He found that he had taken off his hat at some point and was holding it in both hands.

---

"After that, it was just me," Rahul said. His voice had gone flat again. "Me and him. Nandita aunty upstairs with the TV. Mohan bhaiya with the radio. And every night I'd lie in my room, and there was no one on the other side of the wall."

He told about the three years. The speeches on the stage. The full sleeves in summer. The thoughts. He did not hide the thoughts.

"I used to think about it," he said. "Every night. How. What it would be like. I'm telling you because you asked for the truth. Then in the morning I'd get up and go to school and come home and stand in the study when I was called. I never… I never had a plan. I want you to know that. I didn't buy anything. I didn't write anything down. It was just… there. In my head. Like a sound you can't turn off."

"And the twenty-sixth of November?"

Rahul looked at his right wrist. He pulled the cuff back, slowly, for the first time not hiding it, and showed Dev the old yellow-green bruise on the inside of the wrist.

"Low marks in a unit test. Physics. He called me in. Took the chain off. Put it on the glass." He touched the bruise. "And he raised his hand, and I don't know what happened. I just… I grabbed. The chain. It was in his hand, he was going to put it back on, or… I don't know. I grabbed it and pulled and it broke. It just broke. The clasp. It fell on the floor."

"Mohan heard you shout."

"I said *bas*." Rahul almost laughed. "That's all. *Bas.* Enough. I'd never said anything to him before. Not in ten years. And he looked at me. He twisted my wrist, like this, really hard, and I thought he was going to break it. And then he stopped." He stared at nothing. "I saw his face. For a second. He was surprised. Maybe even… scared. Like he'd never thought I could. And then he let go and picked up the chain from the floor and told me to get out." He lowered his sleeve. "He didn't speak to me for three days. He didn't speak to anyone. It was the first time I'd ever seen him not know what to do."

"And you?"

"I found out I could do something," Rahul said simply. "That's what I found out on the twenty-sixth. That I could."

---

"Then the fifth," said Dev.

Rahul nodded slowly.

"The fifth. The event. That stage." He shut his eyes. "*My sister Pari was my best friend.* I got that far, and I looked up and there was her face on the banner, nine feet high, laughing. And there was his hand coming onto my shoulder. And I thought, if I say one more word of this, I'll be helping him. I'll be part of it. Every year I'd been part of it. And I couldn't. That's all. I couldn't say it."

"And in the corridor he said, *we'll talk at home.*"

"*Ghar chal ke baat karenge.*" Rahul opened his eyes. "In the car he didn't say anything. At home he called me into the study at six. He had my maths mock on the desk. Sixty-one. He'd written *Friday* on the chart on the fridge, two days before. He'd been saving it." He drew a breath. "He said I'd made a tamasha of the foundation in front of the whole city. He said, *don't turn into a tamasha like Pari.* Like her. He said tonight we'd talk properly. He said next year I was going to a hostel in Kota, and the Sundays at Nani's were finished. Then he told me to go and study."

"And you left. At six-fourteen."

"I was going to Aditya's. I really was. I messaged him." Rahul shook his head. "I took an auto from the main road. And then at the riverfront, by Samta Mulak, I just… told the driver to stop. I got out. I went down to the steps by the water and I sat there." He looked at the dark sky. "For an hour. I thought about everything. Her knock. The stage. Kota. No more Nani. The chain on the floor and his face on the twenty-sixth, that one second when he was afraid. *Don't turn into a tamasha like Pari.*" He was quiet. "And at six fifty-two I messaged Aditya that I wasn't coming."

"Why did you go back, Rahul?"

The boy did not answer at once. When he did, he spoke very carefully, as if he were afraid of getting a single word wrong.

"I told myself I was going back to tell him. That I was going to tell everyone. About Pari, about the study, about the chain, about the school report. That I'd go to the police, to the newspapers, to that foundation's stage, and say it all into the microphone." He paused. "And I knew he'd laugh. I knew exactly how he'd laugh. And I knew that if he laughed…"

He stopped.

"No," he said. "That's not true. I'm not going to say that. I knew. Sitting on those steps, I knew why I was going back. Not exactly. Not *how*. But I knew it wasn't to talk." He took a breath. "I put the cycle key in my pocket. I went the back way, through the green belt, so the guard wouldn't see me. I knew what I was doing. Don't let me pretend I didn't."

---

He told it all then, and Dev let him, and did not interrupt.

How he came along the green belt in the dark at about twenty to eight, past the bench where Pari used to sit, and let himself in at the back gate with his own key. How the kitchen was dark because Mohan was out, and the house was cold and very quiet. How the study door was half open and the green lamp was on.

How his father had looked up from the desk, over his reading glasses, papers in front of him.

"*Aa gaye?*" Rahul's voice changed as he said it, became lighter, drier, perfectly polite. "*So soon from Aditya's?*"

"I said I hadn't gone to Aditya's. I said I'd come back to tell him something. I said…" He swallowed. "I said I was going to tell everyone what he did to Pari. Everyone. The police, the papers, the foundation. That I'd stand on his stage next year and say it into his microphone."

"What did he say?"

"He laughed." Rahul's face did not change. "Not loudly. He never did anything loudly. He took off his glasses and he laughed, and he said, *Who's going to believe you? I'm Rajiv Sethi. You're a failure who cried on a stage in front of two hundred people today. They'll say grief has made you unstable. They'll say you need help. And they'll be right.*"

He paused.

"And then he said something else. He said, *Pari told someone too, you know. She told her Nani. Do you know what your Nani did? Nothing. She came to me. I cried a little, I made a promise. She went home.*" Rahul's hands were very still on his knees. "I said he was lying. He said, *Ask her.*" He looked at Dev in the dark. "He was lying. Wasn't he? He was lying to hurt me. He always knew where to put his hand."

Dev did not answer. Later, much later, he would understand that he had not answered because he did not know. At that moment, on that terrace, he only knew that he could not say *yes*.

Rahul looked away.

"Then he picked up his phone," he went on, more quickly now, as if he wanted to get through this part. "He said, *I'm calling the hostel principal in Kota now. Tomorrow your phone, your cycle, your Sundays, all of it, finished.* He turned back to the desk, half turned away from me. He was scrolling for the number. And without looking at me he said, *Pari was weak. She chose her way out. And you've turned out exactly the same.*"

The terrace was completely dark now. In the dark, Rahul's voice was very clear.

"The trophy was on the corner of the desk. The one they gave him for her foundation. For her. I picked it up." He was silent for a moment. "I hit him. Once. He fell off the chair. His phone went skidding under the desk. He was on the floor between the desk and the shelf, and he was trying to get up. He had his hand out, towards the leg of the desk. He was saying something. *Rahul*, I think. *Ambulance*."

He stopped for a long time.

"And then the doorbell rang."

---

Dev closed his eyes.

"*Ding-dong.* Twice. Very loud, in that quiet house. And I just… stopped. I stood there with that thing in my hand. And he heard it too. He turned his head towards the door and he opened his mouth and he tried to shout. *Vi—* Like that. Half a word. And then his phone started ringing under the desk. Buzzing on the floor. Ringing and ringing."

Rahul's voice did not shake. That was the worst of it, Dev would think afterwards. It did not shake at all.

"And I knew," he said. "I knew exactly what would happen if he got up. That door would open. Vivek mama would come in. There'd be an ambulance. He'd go to hospital, and come home in a week with a bandage, and he'd sit on that sofa and tell everyone, very sadly, that his son was disturbed, that his son needed help. And they'd send me somewhere. And everyone would say, *what a father, what courage, after everything.* And it would all go on. For ever. Exactly the same." He drew a long breath. "And Pari would still be… nothing. A banner. A speech."

He looked at Dev.

"So I hit him again," he said. "On purpose. I knew what I was doing. I want you to write that down, if you write anything. Don't let anyone say I didn't know. Don't let me say it. The second time, I knew."

Dev thought of Dr. Siddiqui in the mortuary, tapping her pen against the lightbox. *The second one is the one that mattered.*

"The doorbell rang again," Rahul said. "And then it stopped. And the phone stopped. And after a while I heard a car outside, going away." He let out a breath. "And then it was quiet. It was so quiet."

---

"What did you do?"

"I sat on the floor." He said it simply. "I don't know how long. A minute. Five. My hands were… I was shaking. And when I looked up, she was looking at me."

"The photograph."

"On the shelf. Right in front of me. Laughing." His voice broke for the first time, a small crack, quickly mended. "Laughing at me. Like she always did. And I couldn't… I couldn't have her see. She never hurt anyone in her life. She cut Gajar's ear off and cried for three days. She wanted to be a teacher. She didn't want anyone to ever stand in a corner. And I'd just—" He stopped. "So I turned her over. Gently. So she wouldn't have to look."

Dev remembered Nandita's hand stopping an inch from the silver frame.

"Then I started thinking. Properly. Like it was an exam." Rahul's voice had gone flat and quick again. "I wiped the trophy. With my handkerchief. I don't know why. My hands had been on it, I suppose. I scrubbed it and scrubbed it and put it back on the corner of the desk where it had been. Then I thought, *it has to look like a robbery*. A thief would take money. I opened the top drawer, the right one, because that's where he always kept cash. And there was the envelope." He looked at Dev. "*Mohan — Dec.* In his writing. I saw Mohan bhaiya's name and I felt sick. Actually sick. I nearly was. And I took it anyway. I didn't even think about his watch. I didn't think about anything else. I left the drawer open so it would look like someone had been searching."

"And then the back door."

"Through the kitchen. Out to the yard. There was blood on my hands, and on the cuff of my hoodie. I washed my hands at the garden tap. It was so cold. I couldn't make my fingers turn it off properly afterwards. I think I left it running." He looked at his hands. "I took the hoodie off and put on the navy sweater from my bag. Nani always makes me carry a sweater. *Sweater rakh lo, beta, shaam ko thand hoti hai.* I stuffed the hoodie and the handkerchief in the bag. I locked the back gate behind me with my key. Then I went back through the green belt to the riverfront, and there's a big green municipal bin by the riverfront parking, and I put the hoodie and the handkerchief in it, under all the rubbish. And then I went down to the steps and sat by the water."

"And Nandita called you."

"At five to nine. She said, *Rahul, where are you? Something's happened to your father. Come home.*" He shook his head slowly. "And I said, *I'm at Aditya's. I'm coming.* And my voice was fine. Completely fine. That frightened me more than anything." He was quiet. "I took an auto to the main road and walked into the lane. There were police cars. A constable put me on the stairs. And then Nani came."

"And on Saturday morning you messaged Aditya."

"I thought I just needed one day. One day to think. I didn't know what I was going to do with the day." He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "And then he went to Bareilly with his phone off, and nobody asked him, and the days kept going. And then you arrested Nandita aunty."

He took his hands away from his face.

"I didn't know what to do," he said. "I've been lying up here every night since Wednesday, knocking on that wall downstairs, and nobody knocks back."

---

He got up then, stiffly, and went into the barsati, and came back with his black school backpack. He knelt on the tiles beside the chain and unzipped the front pocket and took something out and held it out to Dev in the dark.

It was a white envelope, creased down the middle, its corners soft with being carried. Dev took it and turned it to the faint light coming up from the courtyard below. It was sealed. Across the front, in a square, careful, unmistakable hand, in blue ink, was written:

*Mohan — Dec*

"I never opened it," Rahul said. "It's all there. It's Mohan bhaiya's. Please give it to him. I couldn't… I couldn't even look at it."

Dev held the envelope. It weighed almost nothing, and it was the heaviest thing he had ever held.

"Rahul," he said. "When you were sitting on those steps by the river, after. What did you feel?"

The boy thought about it seriously, the way he must once have thought about exam questions.

"I thought I'd feel quiet," he said at last. "I thought, for ten years there's been this noise, and now it'll stop. That's what I thought it would be. That everything would go quiet." He looked at the chain on the tiles. "It didn't. Nothing went quiet. Now there's just a different noise. The second time. And the doorbell. Over and over." He swallowed. "I don't feel better. I don't feel like I did something for her. I keep thinking she'd hate it. She'd be so angry with me. She'd cut my ear off."

He tried to laugh and could not.

"I'm not going to say I was right," he said. "I'm not going to say he deserved it, even if he did. Pari wouldn't have done it. Pari never hurt anyone in her whole life. I'm the one who did it. Not for her. She didn't ask me to. I did it." He looked up at Dev, and his face in the dark was very young and very old at once. "And Nandita aunty is in jail. And Mohan bhaiya's salary is in my bag. And everybody thinks it was somebody else."

He was quiet for a long moment.

"Are you going to take me now?" he said.

---

Dev did not answer.

He stood on the dark terrace in the cold with a dead man's envelope in one hand and his hat in the other, and the chain lying at his feet, and a boy on his knees in front of him waiting to be told what would happen to the rest of his life.

He knew what the law said. A seventeen-year-old who had confessed to killing his father would be brought before the Juvenile Justice Board. For an offence this grave there would be a preliminary assessment, and the Board might decide that he should be tried as an adult. There would be a children's court, a long proceeding, lawyers, a sentence. There would be the newspapers. *SAMAJ RATNA KILLED BY OWN SON.* Rajiv Sethi's friends were the Rotary Club, the Chamber of Commerce, the principal of St. Aloysius, two MLAs and the Commissioner of Police, and they would sit in courtrooms in their good suits and remember aloud what a devoted father he had been. And somewhere in all of that, a girl who had wanted to be a teacher would become, at best, a mitigating circumstance.

He knew that a woman was lying tonight on a jail mat in Lucknow District Jail for something she had not done, because she would not tell anyone that she had spent sixty-seven minutes doing nothing. He knew that his partner was sitting in a car at the end of this lane with the engine off, waiting for him, having asked him three times since Wednesday to do this properly, and having been given his word three times.

And he knew, though he did not want to, that on some Sunday thirty-five years ago in Saharanpur, a boy in full sleeves had lain in bed listening to a belt being taken off in the next room, and had thought about it every night, *how*, and had got up in the morning and gone to school.

Below them, in the courtyard, a light came on. Sharda Devi's kitchen. He heard the clink of a steel tumbler set down on a counter.

At the end of the lane, a pair of headlights flicked on, and off again.

Dev looked down at the boy. He had arrested men for less. He had sent men to prison for far less, and slept afterwards. He had never in twenty years wanted so badly not to know what he knew.

"Rahul," he said, and then stopped, because he did not know what came next.

He did not know what his answer was. He only knew that whatever he said now, someone would have to live with it for the rest of their life. And for the first time in his career, he was not at all sure that it should not be him.
