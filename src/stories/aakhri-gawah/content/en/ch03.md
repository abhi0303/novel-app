The mortuary at King George's Medical University smelled of phenyl and cold tile, and Dr. Farhana Siddiqui had the gift of saying terrible things in the tone of someone reading out a train timetable.

"Two impacts," she said. She had pinned the photographs to a lightbox on the wall, and she pointed with the cap of her pen. "Here, and here. Left side of the back of the head. Same object, slightly different angles. The first is higher and glancing. It would have hurt him badly and probably knocked him down. It may not have killed him. The second is lower and much deeper, and the angle tells me he was already low when it landed. Falling, or on the floor."

"How long between them?" Dev asked.

"Seconds. A minute. I can't give you a stopwatch, Inspector. What I can tell you is that the second one is the one that mattered."

Kabir wrote that down exactly. Later, he underlined it twice.

"Time?"

"Stomach contents put tea at around a quarter to six, nothing after. With the temperature readings from the field unit and the rigor, I'd say death between seven-thirty and eight-thirty. If you want my private opinion, closer to eight than to half past. But my private opinion doesn't go into the report."

"Defence wounds?"

"None. Nothing under the nails, no bruising on the forearms. He didn't raise a hand." She capped her pen. "Either he didn't see it coming, or he didn't believe it was coming. People who believe it's coming put up their arms. It's the oldest reflex we have."

"And the weapon."

"Alok sent me photographs of the trophy." She pulled another sheet from the lightbox. "The square base has a bevelled edge, about this width. The wound margins fit it very well. I'll put *consistent with* in writing, which is as far as an honest pathologist goes. But Alok will tell you the rest."

Alok Mishra told them the rest over the phone twenty minutes later, as they drove back through the Saturday traffic on Shah Najaf Road. Someone had wiped the trophy, he said. Wiped it thoroughly, the base especially. But the name plate had engraved letters, and engraved letters have grooves, and in the grooves of *LUCKNOW SAMAJ RATNA* the reagent had found blood. Not much. Enough. Rajiv Sethi's, almost certainly; they would confirm.

"Prints?"

"Smears. A partial of the deceased, old. Nothing usable on the base. Whoever wiped it was careful, or lucky." Mishra paused. "And the tap. You were right to call us out to it. There are traces of diluted blood in the mud under the garden tap, and in the gravel by the drain. Someone washed there, Inspector. Someone with blood on their hands came out of the kitchen door and washed at that tap before leaving by the back gate."

Dev thanked him and put the phone away.

"So the killer went out the back," Kabir said. "Washed, locked the gate behind them with a key, and left the tap running because they were in a hurry or they were shaking. Back-gate key holders: Mohan, Nandita, the kitchen hook, the dead man's own ring, and the boy's cycle key." He drummed his fingers on the steering wheel. "Mohan says he was at a market he can't name. Nandita says she was at the bank until a quarter past eight. And the boy was at a friend's."

"And the dead man was dead."

"Which leaves the kitchen hook. Anybody in the house could have taken that key." Kabir glanced over. "Including someone who wasn't supposed to be in the house."

Sethi Packaging occupied a long grey shed in the Talkatora industrial area, smelling of glue and wet cardboard, with a glass-fronted office upstairs where the machines sounded like distant rain. Kulhad & Co. was on a lane behind Hazratganj, between a shop selling chikan kurtas and a dentist's clinic with a faded poster of a smiling molar. Exposed brick, fairy lights strung along the ceiling, a chalkboard menu in three colours of chalk, wooden stools painted turquoise. At two on a Saturday afternoon there were three customers. A handwritten sign taped to the counter said *Card machine not working, UPI only, sorry!* and had the look of a sign that had been there for some time.

Vivek Saxena was behind the counter, making a cold coffee himself. He looked as if he had slept in his clothes, which, Kabir would later learn, he had: on his sister's sofa in Vipul Khand, until five in the morning. When he saw the two policemen come in, he set down the steel jug very carefully.

"Inspector. Is Didi—"

"Your sister is fine, Mr. Saxena. We'd like to talk to you."

They sat at a corner table under a framed print of an old Bollywood poster. Vivek fiddled with a vape pen without using it. Up close he had his sister's eyes and none of her stillness; everything in him moved, his knee, his fingers, his gaze.

"Jiju and I had our differences," he said, before anyone had asked. "Everyone knows. I'm not going to pretend."

"Tell us about the differences."

He told them, more or less as Agnihotri had: the café, the loan, the slow months, the cheques. When Kabir said "cricket betting app", Vivek's face went the colour of the turquoise stools, and then a dull red.

"Some bad decisions," he said. "I'm dealing with it."

"Eighteen lakh rupees of bad decisions."

"I said I'm dealing with it." The knee stopped. "Look. He lent me money. He also made sure I never forgot he'd lent me money. Every family function, every Diwali, in front of my parents, in front of Didi. *Vivek, how's the business? Vivek, still in the red?* With a smile. Always with a smile." He laughed, without humour. "You don't know what he was like. Everybody thought he was a saint. Lucknow Samaj Ratna." He pronounced the title as if it tasted of something. "My sister has been living in a museum for eight years. Everything polished, everything in its place, and nobody allowed to touch anything."

"What do you mean by that?" asked Dev.

Vivek opened his mouth. Then he looked at the fairy lights for a while and closed it again.

"Nothing. It's a figure of speech."

"Yesterday at the Awadh Regency you told him someone would settle accounts with him."

"I was angry." Vivek met his eyes. "People say things. If everyone who said that about Rajiv Sethi killed him, Inspector, you'd need a bigger jail."

"Where were you yesterday evening between seven and nine?"

"Here." He gestured at the café. "Till about eight-thirty. Then Didi called me. Eight forty-five, something like that. And I drove straight to Vipul Khand."

"You were here the whole time."

"The whole time."

"Did you know about the envelope? Mohan's salary?"

Vivek blinked at the change of subject. "The… yes. I saw it once. A few months ago I went to the house on the fifth. Jiju made Mohan come into the study and stand there while he counted the money out. In front of me. In front of Rahul." His mouth twisted. "It was a show. Everything was a show with him. He wanted you to see who gave and who received."

The residents' welfare association of Vipul Khand had installed its camera eighteen months earlier, after the theft of the cylinder, on a pole at the mouth of the lane where Ramvilas kept his plastic chair. It was not a good camera. At night it reduced the world to grey shapes and white haloes. But it had a timestamp, and it pointed at the only road in and out of the lane, and the association's secretary, a retired bank officer called Mr. Khanna who lived two doors from the Sethis, had copied the whole of Friday evening onto a pen drive before anyone had asked him to.

"I watch crime shows," he told Kabir, handing it over. "The first forty-eight hours are crucial."

They went through it that evening at the Crime Branch office in Hazratganj, on Kabir's laptop, with two glasses of tea going cold beside it. Kabir worked the playback. Dev sat back in his chair with his glasses pushed up on his forehead and watched the grey shapes come and go.

*18:15:02.* A thin figure on foot, hood up, a backpack on one shoulder, walking out of the lane towards the main road.

"That's the boy," said Kabir. "Rahul. Leaving for his friend's, like he said."

*18:15* to *19:50*. Two cars belonging to residents. A delivery boy on a scooter. A woman walking a dog in a sweater.

*19:51:14.* Headlights. A white SUV turning into the lane at speed, braking hard for the speed-breaker. Kabir froze the frame and zoomed. The image broke into squares, then came back enough to read.

*UP32 — —  4417*.

He did not need to check. He had seen the car parked outside Kulhad & Co. that afternoon, a white Hyundai Creta with a dent in the rear bumper. He checked anyway.

"Vivek Saxena," he said.

He let it run. The SUV disappeared down the lane towards the Sethi house and out of the camera's view. The timestamp ticked on. Neither of them spoke.

*19:58:40.* The same headlights, coming back the other way. The Creta passed under the pole and turned onto the main road without slowing for the speed-breaker, and was gone.

Kabir sat back.

"Seven minutes," he said. "He came into the lane at seven fifty-one and left at seven fifty-eight. The man who swore he was at his café until eight-thirty." He looked at the frozen grey frame, the white smear of the taillights. "Seven minutes, sir. Right in the middle of the window Siddiqui gave us. Right when Rajiv Sethi was dying."

Dev pulled his glasses down from his forehead and leaned forward, and looked at the screen for a long time.

On the desk beside the laptop lay the field unit's photograph of the brass trophy, with *LUCKNOW SAMAJ RATNA* engraved on its base. He picked it up and turned it to the light, as if the grooves might show him something more.

"Run it forward," he said. "To the end of the night. I want to see everyone who came home."
