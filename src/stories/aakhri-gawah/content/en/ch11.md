Shubh Residency was one of the newer buildings in Sector 14 of Indira Nagar: six storeys of beige plaster and blue glass, a guard cabin at the gate with a boom barrier that went up and down all day for delivery boys, and a fat visitors' register on the guard's table, bound in green cloth, its pages ruled into columns by hand.

Kabir got to the register before Dev had finished parking.

"Every visitor signs, sir," he said, turning the pages. "Name, flat number, time in, time out. The guard's strict about it, the RWA fined him last year." He ran his finger down November. "Look. Twenty-second November, *Rahul S., 304, 4:10 PM — 7:45 PM.* Twenty-ninth November, *Rahul, 304, 4:05 — 7:30.* Every Saturday." He turned to the fifth of December. The page was full of names: a plumber, two Swiggy boys, a woman visiting 501, somebody's tuition teacher. He went down the column slowly, then went down it again. "Nothing for 304. Nothing at all between five and ten. No Rahul."

The guard, a young man from Gonda with a woollen monkey cap pulled down to his eyebrows, confirmed it with some pride. He had been on duty on Friday evening from six until two. Aditya's friend, the quiet one? He would know him. The boy always said *namaste bhaiya* and signed in neat handwriting. He had not come on Friday. The guard was sure.

Flat 304 smelled of agarbatti and freshly made tea. Mrs. Shalini Verma opened the door in a starched cotton sari, reading glasses pushed up on her head, a red pen still in her hand. She was the vice-principal of a girls' school in Mahanagar and had the brisk, unsurprisable manner of a woman who had been interrupting children's excuses for twenty years.

"Inspector Rathore. Come in. Aditya's back from his practical; he's changing." She lowered her voice. "I should tell you he hasn't slept properly since we came back from Bareilly. He saw the news there, at the wedding. My sister showed him on her phone." She looked at Dev over her glasses. "Whatever you're going to ask him, I want him to tell you the truth. I've already told him that."

Aditya came out of his room still half in his school uniform, a grey sweater pulled over his white shirt, his tie hanging loose. He was tall and thin in the way of boys who have grown four inches in one year and haven't caught up with themselves, with rectangular glasses and a nervous habit of pushing them up his nose. He sat on the edge of the sofa with his mother beside him and looked at the two policemen as if they were an exam he had not prepared for.

"Aditya," Dev said. "You know why we're here."

"Rahul."

"On Friday the fifth, between six-thirty and nine in the evening, where was Rahul?"

Aditya looked at his mother. She did not say anything. She simply looked back at him, steadily, the way she must have looked at a thousand girls across a thousand desks.

"He wasn't here," Aditya said.

It came out very fast, as if it had been waiting behind his teeth for a week. Then he put his face in his hands.

"Sorry," he said through his fingers. "Sorry. I'm sorry."

"Tell us what happened," Dev said. "Take your time."

Aditya took out his phone. His hands were shaking slightly. He unlocked it, opened his chat with Rahul, scrolled up, and held it out to Dev without looking at it himself.

*Fri 18:20 — Rahul: Aaj aa raha hoon padhne. Maths.* (Coming over today to study. Maths.)
*Fri 18:21 — Aditya: aaja*
*Fri 18:52 — Rahul: Nahi aa paunga. Sorry.* (Can't come. Sorry.)
*Fri 18:53 — Aditya: kya hua? sab theek?* (What happened? All OK?)

No reply. The next message was from the following morning.

*Sat 09:14 — Rahul: Agar koi poochhe toh kehna main kal tere ghar tha. Please. Baad mein bataunga.* (If anyone asks, say I was at your place yesterday. Please. I'll explain later.)
*Sat 09:16 — Aditya: ok bro. sab theek hai na?* (OK bro. Everything's fine, right?)

No reply to that either.

Dev read the messages twice and handed the phone to Kabir, who photographed the screen and the chat details, timestamps visible, and wrote the times in his notebook.

"I thought it was about his dad," Aditya said. He was speaking to the carpet. "Like, that he'd had a fight with his dad and gone somewhere and didn't want his dad to know he hadn't been studying. He's done that before, gone and sat somewhere by himself. His dad—" He stopped. "Everybody knew his dad was… a lot. Rahul never called him *Papa*. He said *woh*. *He.* Like, *he'll kill me if I get less than ninety*. So when he said *if anyone asks*, I thought he meant his dad. I said OK. And then we left for Bareilly at six in the morning, and Mummy's rule is no phones at family functions." A glance at his mother. "I kept it switched off. I didn't know. I didn't know anything until Masi showed me the news on the second day. And then I didn't know what to do."

"You could have called the police," his mother said quietly.

"I know." His voice cracked. "I didn't want to get him in trouble. And then I thought, if I lie to the police and they find out, that's worse for him. And for me." He looked up at Dev for the first time. "He's my best friend, sir. I'm not lying for him. I'm sorry, Rahul. I'm not."

Mrs. Verma put a hand on her son's back.

"For the record, Inspector," she said, "I was at home all of Friday evening. I was marking half-yearly papers at that dining table from five until eleven. Nobody came to this flat except the delivery boy with the milk. Rahul was not here." She hesitated. "He usually comes on Saturdays. He signs the register, he says namaste, he drinks his tea, he studies with Aditya for three hours and barely speaks. Once, last summer, he reached for a book and his sleeve went up, and there was…" She stopped and took her glasses off her head. "I'm a vice-principal. I have done the child-protection training four times. And I told myself it was none of my business, because he wasn't my student and his father was Rajiv Sethi." She folded the glasses. "So you'll forgive me if I'm making sure my son tells the truth now."

They sat in the car outside Shubh Residency with the engine off and the heater on, and neither of them said anything for a full minute.

Then Kabir took out his phone and opened two photographs side by side on the screen.

The first was the doorbell still from 18:14:22. A thin boy on the top step of the Sethi house in a **grey hoodie**, a black backpack on one shoulder, turning to look back at the closed front door.

The second was the doorbell still from 21:26:05. The same thin boy on the same step, hands in the pockets of a **navy sweater**. No hoodie. The same backpack.

"He left in a grey hoodie," Kabir said. "He came back in a navy sweater. In December. It was nine degrees on Friday night."

Dev looked at the screen for a long time.

"I saw that clip," he said. "On Tuesday. In the conference room. I watched him walk up those steps in the sweater and I didn't see it."

"Neither did I, sir. I saw it at two this morning, going through the stills again." Kabir put the phone on the dashboard between them. He did not sound triumphant. He sounded tired. "Let me do the whole thing, sir. Out loud. The way you made me do the board."

"Do it."

"Six-fourteen, he walks out of the front door in a grey hoodie. Six-fifteen, lane camera. Six-twenty, he tells Aditya he's coming. Six fifty-two, he tells Aditya he's not. They drove back along the Ring Road in the dark. Kabir did not put the radio on. At the Kapoorthala crossing, waiting at the red light behind a tempo stacked with sacks of potatoes, he spoke without turning his head.

"You'll have to put it in the case diary, sir. Aditya's statement. The register. The CDR. All of it. The ACP will see it on Monday."

"I know."

"And Nandita is in Lucknow Jail tonight."

"I know that too."

The light turned green. Kabir moved the car forward slowly, past the tempo, into the stream of tail-lights going north.

"I was right," he said. "On Wednesday. I was right, and I didn't want to be." He swallowed. "I kept hoping Aditya would open the door and say *yes, he was here, we did quadratic equations till nine*, and I'd have been an idiot, and you'd have said *hmm*, and that would have been the end of it."

Dev said nothing for a long time.

"I left him for last," he said at last. "You asked me why, and I didn't answer. I left him for last because I looked at him on that staircase on Friday night, with his sleeves pulled down over his hands, and I saw…" He stopped. "I saw what I wanted to see. A child who had been hurt. Not a person who could hurt anyone back."

"He can be both, sir."

"Yes." Dev took off his glasses and rubbed his eyes. "He can be both."

He put his hand inside his overcoat, to the inside pocket, and felt the shape of the red cloth bundle there: the medallion, round and hard, the weight of the links. He had been carrying it for two days now, through a magistrate's court and a Commissioner's office and a little girl's death anniversary, and he had not been able to give it away.

On Sunday, a week ago, he had looked at a quiet boy in a barsati and believed the boy had nothing to tell him. He had been wrong about that. The boy had said one thing, very carefully, on the morning after his father's death. He had said it in writing, to the only friend anyone might think to ask.

*If anyone asks, say I was at your place.*

A child who is only grieving does not build himself an alibi. Somebody who knows exactly what he needs an alibi for does.

"Tomorrow," Dev said. "I'm going to give him back his father's chain."
