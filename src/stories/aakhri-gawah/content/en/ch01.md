By the time Rajiv Sethi reached the part of his speech about listening, half the banquet hall was in tears.

Ritu Bajpai had organised a great many events at the Hotel Awadh Regency: weddings, product launches, a minister's sixtieth birthday that had overrun by three hours. She had learned to watch a room the way a nurse watches a monitor. She watched it now from the side of the stage, clipboard pressed to her chest, and saw it happen row by row. School principals in silk saris dabbed their eyes. Rotary members in blazers stared hard at the carpet. In the front row, twelve girls from government schools sat in their best salwar suits holding scholarship certificates they had not yet dared to read, and they looked at the man on the stage as if he were the father in a film.

Behind him, on a banner nine feet tall, a girl laughed at the camera. She was perhaps twelve, with a small pink flower clip in her hair, one front tooth slightly crooked, eyes squeezed half shut by her own laughter. Above her photograph, in letters Ritu's team had spent two days aligning, it said:

**SUNO.**
*The Pari Sethi Foundation — Third Annual Scholarships*

"Three years ago," Rajiv said, and the hall went still, "my daughter left us."

He did not say *died*. Ritu had noticed that at the rehearsal. He never said *died*, or *suicide*. He said *left us*, *went away*, *the day we lost her*. When he said it, he looked down at the lectern for exactly as long as it took the audience to look down with him, and then he raised his eyes.

"I stand here as a father who asks himself one question every night. Did I listen? Did I hear what she was trying to tell me?" He let it settle. "We give our children everything. Schools, tuitions, phones, holidays. What we forget to give them is our attention. So I ask you, as parents and teachers: listen to them. Suno. Before it is too late to hear anything at all."

He was forty-nine and looked younger: tall, broad through the shoulders, his hair still black and combed back from a face that photographed well from every angle. His suit was charcoal, his tie a muted maroon. Ritu had dealt with rich men who treated event staff like furniture. Rajiv Sethi had learned her name the first time they met. He had asked after her mother's knee and remembered the answer a week later. He had sent a box of sweets to her office when the Rotary dinner went well. The press called him *Lucknow's most respected businessman* without irony. Sethi Packaging supplied cartons to half the factories in Talkatora, and for three years the Pari Sethi Foundation had been putting counsellors into government schools that had never had one.

As he spoke, his right hand rose briefly to his collarbone, below the knot of his tie, as if to touch something that hung there. It found nothing, and he lowered the hand again. Ritu noticed only because she had seen him do it twice before, in the green room. She assumed it was a nervous habit. Even saints had those.

The applause, when it came, was long and warm and sincere.

"And now," Rajiv said, smiling for the first time, "someone who knew Pari better than any of us. Her brother. Rahul."

---

The boy came up the three steps to the stage as if each one were taller than the last.

He was seventeen, Ritu knew from the run-sheet, but he looked younger in the way thin boys do: a navy school blazer that fit him at the shoulders and nowhere else, black hair that had been combed and had quietly given up, a face that would one day have his father's bones but not yet his father's ease. He held an index card in both hands. He had pulled the cuffs of his shirt down over his wrists so far that only his fingers showed.

He stood at the lectern. Rajiv stepped back, to one side, and rested a hand on the edge of the lectern near his son's elbow. Close, supportive. The photographer from the *Times of India* raised his camera.

"My sister Pari," Rahul read, "was my best friend."

The card had been written by the foundation's communications girl. Ritu had seen the draft: three paragraphs about Pari's laughter and her kindness, ending with *I know she is listening now*. It was a good card. It would have taken ninety seconds.

Rahul read the first line and stopped.

The microphone picked up his breathing. Two hundred people waited. One of the scholarship girls in the front row leaned forward. Ritu checked her clipboard automatically, as if the next line might be written there.

Rahul looked at the card. His thumb moved across the words. His lips parted. Nothing came. A second passed, and another, and then the silence had a weight of its own and everyone in the hall could feel it pressing down.

Rajiv moved. He did not hurry. He stepped up beside his son, laid his left hand on Rahul's shoulder, and with his right drew the microphone gently towards himself.

"Forgive us," he said softly, into the mic. "It still hurts him. It hurts us all." He looked out at the audience with wet eyes. "That is why we do this work."

The hall rose to its feet. Ritu found that she was clapping too.

The photographer took his picture: the father's hand on the son's shoulder, the son's head bowed, the laughing girl on the banner behind them. Ritu would see it on the front page of the city supplement the next morning, under the headline *A FATHER WHO LISTENS*. At the time she was watching the hand.

Under it, Rahul's shoulder had gone rigid. It wasn't a flinch. It was the stillness of something holding its breath. The boy did not lean into his father, the way grieving sons in photographs usually did. He did not move at all until the hand lifted, and then he walked off the stage on the far side, away from the steps his father had used.

---

Ritu caught up with them in the service corridor behind the hall, where the waiters were stacking chafing dishes. She needed Rajiv's signature on the hotel's release form.

He signed it against the wall without reading it and handed back her pen with a smile. "Wonderful arrangements, Ritu. Thank you. Tell your mother I said namaste."

"I will, sir. And Rahul—" She glanced at the boy, who was standing very straight beside a trolley of dirty cups. "He did really well. It's not easy."

"He's a strong boy," Rajiv said. "He gets that from his mother."

He reached over and straightened Rahul's collar where it had folded under the blazer. It was an ordinary gesture, a father's gesture. While he did it, he leaned close to his son's ear and said something too low for Ritu to hear. Only his lips moved. The smile stayed exactly where it was.

Ritu was turning away when she caught the last words. She did not think about them until much later, when a policeman with tired eyes and black-framed glasses asked her to remember everything.

"…*ghar chal ke baat karenge.*"

We'll talk at home.

Rahul said nothing. He pulled his cuffs down over his hands again, though they were already as far down as they would go.

Rajiv's phone buzzed. He glanced at it, and for a fraction of a second something closed in his face, like a shutter coming down on a shop. Ritu saw the screen before he turned it away: a message from *Nandita*.

*Audit running late, can't make it. So sorry. Proud of you both.*

He typed one word with his thumb, *Fine*, and put the phone in his pocket. Then he was smiling again, shaking hands with the principal of Loreto, accepting the condolences and congratulations that came, at these events, in the same breath.

In the lobby, by the tall brass urns of white lilies, a man in a crumpled shirt was waiting for him. Ritu noticed the man because he was wrong for the room: unshaven, jiggling one knee, holding a car key so tightly his knuckles were pale. When Rajiv came through the doors, the man stood up.

Ritu went back to the hall to supervise the dismantling of the banner. Two of her boys climbed ladders and unhooked the laughing girl from the ceiling, and she came down slowly, face first, folding in on herself until there was nothing left of her but a roll of vinyl under someone's arm.

---

At 8:38 that evening, Nandita Sethi turned her car into Vipul Khand.

The fog had begun to come down off the Gomti, the way it did in December, not all at once but in a slow white leak along the ground. The streetlights wore haloes. At the mouth of the lane, Ramvilas, the colony guard, stood up from his plastic chair and raised a hand to his cap as her headlights swept across him. She lifted two fingers from the steering wheel in reply. Eight years she had lived in this lane, and every evening the same exchange: the guard's salute, her two fingers. Rajiv had told her once that it was important to acknowledge the staff. *People remember small courtesies.*

The house was the fourth on the left. White, two storeys, a curved balcony, a bougainvillea that the mali kept clipped into a shape like a fist. Rajiv had built it the year before Pari was born. Nandita pulled into the driveway, switched off the engine and sat.

She always sat. Two minutes, sometimes five. It was the only part of the day that belonged to no one. She looked at the house through the windscreen. The upstairs was dark. The living room was dark. A single window glowed at the back left corner of the ground floor: the study. Of course. He would be at his desk. He was always at his desk.

She thought about the phone call at seven. She put the thought away.

The blue ring of the video doorbell lit up as she came up the steps, sensing her. Rajiv had installed it last year. *For security*, he said, though the colony had a guard and a camera and nothing had ever happened in Vipul Khand that was worse than a stolen cylinder. The app was on his phone. He liked to know who came to his door.

She unlocked the front door and stepped in.

The cold was the first thing. The house was cold the way a house is when no one has walked through it for a while. The heater in the living room was off. The television was off. Usually, at this hour, Mohan would have the kitchen lights on and the pressure cooker going, and the smell of dal and hing would reach her before she had taken off her shoes. Tonight the kitchen was dark and there was no smell at all.

"Mohan?"

Nothing.

"Rajiv?"

Her voice went out into the house and did not come back.

She put her bag on the console table, where it always went, beside the brass Ganesh that his mother had given them. She took off her heels. She noticed she was doing these things slowly and carefully, the way you do when some part of you has already understood something and the rest of you is trying to buy time.

The corridor to the study ran along the back of the house. The study door was not quite shut. A line of yellow light lay across the corridor floor, the colour of the desk lamp.

"Rajiv, are you—"

She pushed the door.

---

Afterwards, when the policemen asked her to describe what she saw, she found that she could describe it in the order she had seen it, and that the order mattered to her, as if she could keep it all manageable by keeping it in sequence.

The lamp first. The green banker's lamp on the desk, its light pooled on the glass top. Papers on the desk, a printout with red ink on it. His reading glasses, folded.

Then the drawer. The top drawer on the right side of the desk stood open by a hand's width. Rajiv never left a drawer open. He had once made Rahul stand in this room for forty minutes because a drawer in the kitchen had been left open. She told the police that, later, and then wished she hadn't.

Then the floor.

He was lying on his side between the desk and the bookshelf, one arm under him, the other flung out towards the desk's leg as if he had been reaching for something. His face was turned away from the door. She could see the back of his head, and the back of his head was wrong. It was the wrong shape, and darker than it should have been, and the dark had gone into the pale carpet beneath him in a way that the carpet would never forget.

She did not scream. She would wonder about that for a long time. She had always assumed she would scream.

She knelt. She said his name, and when he did not answer she put two fingers against his neck, the way they showed on television. His skin was not cold. That was the worst part, the part she would come back to at night. He was not cold. He had been here, warm, a little while ago, and she had been somewhere else.

There was no pulse. She pressed harder, as if she had simply not found the right place. There was no pulse.

She stood up. She found her phone in her hand, though she did not remember taking it out, and she dialled one-one-two. The operator answered on the second ring, a young man with a Purvanchal accent, and Nandita heard her own voice giving the address, spelling *Sethi*, explaining that her husband was on the floor and not breathing and that there was blood, and the voice was so steady and so clear that it frightened her more than anything in the room.

"Ma'am, is anyone else in the house?"

She looked at the open door behind her, at the dark corridor.

"I don't know," she said.

"Please go outside, ma'am. Stand near the gate. Don't touch anything. The PCR is on its way."

"Yes."

But she did not go outside. While the young man repeated the instructions, her eyes moved over the room the way they would have moved over a balance sheet, looking for the entry that was wrong. The open drawer. She knew what should have been in it, because today was the fifth. Every month on the fifth, Rajiv paid Mohan his salary in cash, in this room, in a white envelope with *Mohan* and the month written on it in his square, careful hand. He made Mohan come in and stand by the desk to receive it, and sign the register, and say thank you. She had once asked him why he didn't simply transfer the money. *Because then he would forget whose money it is*, Rajiv had said.

From where she stood she could see into the drawer. Files, his chequebook, a box of fountain-pen cartridges. No white envelope.

And then she saw the bookshelf.

The study bookshelf ran floor to ceiling along the wall opposite the window: Rajiv's management books, his father's Urdu poetry, the leather-bound annual reports of Sethi Packaging, a row of crystal awards. At eye level, in the centre, where anyone sitting in the visitor's chair would see it, stood a silver frame. Rajiv had put it there himself three years ago. Every journalist who had interviewed him in this room had photographed it. Nandita had never touched it, because Rajiv dusted it himself, every Sunday, with a soft cloth he kept in the desk.

In the frame was Pari, aged eleven, at Nani's house in Aliganj, laughing at something just out of the picture, a pink flower clip in her hair.

The frame was lying face down.

Not knocked over. Nothing else on that shelf had moved; the crystal awards stood in their perfect row. The frame had been laid flat, carefully, its silver back to the ceiling, its glass to the wood, the way you might turn a photograph over when you did not want the person in it to see what was happening in the room.

Nandita reached out to set it upright. Her hand stopped an inch away.

*Don't touch anything*, the young man had said.

She stood there with her hand in the air, in her stockinged feet, in the cold study, with her husband on the floor behind her and the operator's voice still talking in her ear. Somewhere far off on the main road a siren began. And she found that the thing she could not stop looking at was not the body, and not the open drawer. It was the silver back of the frame.

For three years, nobody in this house had been allowed to move Pari.

Tonight, someone had.
