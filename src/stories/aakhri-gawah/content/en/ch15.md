His mother telephoned on the morning of the twenty-fourth, while he was shaving.

"Sharda Behenji came to see me yesterday," she said, without hello. "She asked for you. Not at her house. Here." A pause, filled with the sound of a pressure cooker somewhere behind her. "She'll come at eight tonight. I said you would be here."

"Ma, I'm not on the case any more."

"I didn't ask you about any case," said his mother. "I said eight."

---

He had nothing to do that day, and he found it harder than he had expected.

Inspector Rawat had taken the Sethi file on Monday, all four volumes of it, and the faint pink ring on the whiteboard, and had telephoned twice with polite questions about the index. The inquiry officer from the West Zone had taken Dev's statement on Tuesday in a borrowed office, a careful, courteous man who wrote down *I failed to verify the FASTag record and the alibi of a family member within a reasonable time* and read it back to him twice. Dev had signed it. It was true.

Kabir's transfer had come through faster than anyone expected. He was clearing his desk on Monday and gone by Tuesday afternoon, to the Economic Offences Wing three streets away, and he had shaken Dev's hand at the door of the office, formally, as if they had only just been introduced.

So on the twenty-fourth, Dev went to the office and read old files that did not need reading. He ate lunch at his desk. At five he walked down Hazratganj in the early dark, past the cathedral, where they were stringing coloured lights along the railings and a choir was practising something in English behind the closed doors. He stood outside for a while and listened, without knowing the words. Then he took an auto to Aliganj.

---

His mother had lit the heater in the front room and put out the good cups, the ones with the gold rims that were used for guests and funerals. When Sharda Devi came, at eight exactly, his mother brought the tea herself, set it on the table, touched the older woman's shoulder once, and went into the kitchen and closed the door. Dev heard the radio come on in there, low. He thought of Mohan, and then made himself stop thinking of him.

Sharda Devi sat very upright on the edge of the divan with a cloth bag on her knees. She was in a grey shawl over her grey sari, and she looked, in the orange light of the heater, older than she had at the barsi, and smaller, and much more tired. She did not touch the tea.

"How is he, Mataji?"

"He eats." She said it as if it were a great thing, which perhaps it was. "Since that Sunday he comes down from the roof for meals. Last night two rotis. On Monday he went back to school. His pre-boards are in January." She looked at her hands. "He gave me the chain to keep. The same night, when he came down. He put it in my hand, in that red cloth, and said, *Nani, rakh lo. Mujhe nahi chahiye.* Keep it. I don't want it." She was silent a moment. "I put it in my trunk."

Dev waited.

"I have not come to ask you anything, Inspector." She said it quickly, as though she had practised it on the way. "About that night. About anything. Whatever you know, you keep it. I didn't ask you on Sunday when you came down my stairs, and I won't ask you now. I know you won't tell me." Her hands moved on the bag. "I have come to give you something. And to tell you something first, so you understand why."

"Mataji, I'm not the investigating officer any more."

"I know. Your mother told me." For the first time, something like a smile touched her mouth. "That's why I came to you."

---

She began, as he would remember afterwards, with the carrots.

"It was the nineteenth of November. Three years ago. A Sunday. The children came at ten as they always did; those were his rules. Rahul went straight up to the roof with his kite. Pari didn't go. That was the first thing. She always went. She followed him everywhere, she shouted at him, she lost her kite in thirty seconds and screamed at the boy next door." Sharda Devi's voice was steady and very low. "That Sunday she stayed in her room. The small room. She lay on the bed with Gajar and read. I thought she had exams.

"In the afternoon I was making gajar ka halwa. It's the season, the red carrots come in November. She loved it. It's why the rabbit is called Gajar. She named him when she was four, eating halwa in my kitchen, with the rabbit sitting on the counter beside her bowl." A breath. "She came and stood at the kitchen door. I said, *come, taste it, tell me if there's enough sugar.* She didn't come. She said, *Nani, can I live here? Always?*"

Dev did not move.

"I laughed. God forgive me, I laughed, and I said *and what about school?* and she said, *I'll go to your school. The government one. You can teach me.* And I was still laughing, and stirring, and I said *your Papa would never allow it.* And she didn't say anything. She just turned round, in the kitchen doorway, and lifted up the back of her kurta."

The heater ticked. In the kitchen, on the other side of the closed door, the radio was playing an old song very softly.

"I will not tell you what I saw," Sharda Devi said. "I have never told anyone. I taught girls for forty years, Inspector. I have seen children who were beaten at home. In this mohalla, in my school, it was not so rare. I knew the look of it. I had never seen a child's back like that."

She was silent for a long moment.

"She pulled her kurta down again. She didn't cry. She said, very politely, the way you'd ask for a glass of water: *Nani, mujhe apne paas rakh lo. Please. Main sab kaam karungi. Main roungi bhi nahi.*" Sharda Devi's voice did not break. It went on, flat and exact, like a woman reading out a sentence passed on herself. "Keep me with you, Nani. Please. I'll do all the work. I won't even cry."

Dev found that he had taken off his glasses and was holding them in his hand.

"And what did you say, Mataji?"

"I said I would talk to her Papa." She looked at him. "I turned off the gas and I held her, there in the kitchen door, and I said, *I'll talk to your Papa. He'll promise, you'll see. It'll stop. Just wait a little, beta.* And she said, *Promise?* And I said, *Promise.*" She turned the thin gold bangle on her wrist, once. "And she went back to her room. And at six o'clock, because those were the rules, I sent her home."

"The next morning I went to Vipul Khand, while the children were at school. I didn't telephone. I didn't want him to have time. Mohan let me in. Nandita had gone to the bank. Rajiv was in the study, at the desk, in that chair." She spoke the words *that chair* as if they were a place she had been forbidden to go. "I closed the door. I told him what I had seen. I told him that if it happened again I would go to the police myself."

"What did he do?"

"He wept." Something moved in her face, very fast, and was put away. "He sat down in the chair and put his face in his hands and wept like a boy. He said that since Anjali died he had been alone with everything. The business, the children, the house. That he had no one. That he had lost his temper. Once or twice. That it was wrong and he knew it was wrong and he was ashamed. He said he would see a counsellor. A doctor. Anything I wanted." She paused. "He got up out of the chair and touched my feet, Inspector. He said, *Mummyji, I promise you. On Anjali's name. Never again.*"

"And you believed him."

"I wanted to." She said it simply. "That is not the same thing, but I didn't know that then. Or I did know, and I didn't let myself." She looked at the heater. "And then, when I was at the door, with my hand on it, he said the other thing."

She did not need to be asked.

"He said it very gently. He was standing by the desk, and he had his hand up at his neck, at that medallion, turning it the way he always did. I didn't know then what it meant. He said, *And Mummyji. If you speak of this to anyone, the school, the police, anyone at all, I'll have to protect my family. I'm their father. I have lawyers. You are a widow on a teacher's pension. The court will give them to me, and I'll make sure you never see them again. Not on Sundays. Not ever.* And then he smiled and said, *But that won't be necessary, will it? We both want what's best for the children.*" She stopped. "He opened the door for me. He walked me to the gate. He asked after my knees."

"I went home," Sharda Devi said. "That evening I telephoned the house and asked for Pari, and Rajiv himself called her to the phone, very pleasantly, and stood beside her while she talked. I could hear him breathing. And I said, *Papa ne promise kiya hai, beta. Sab theek ho jaayega.* Papa has promised. Everything will be all right." She closed her eyes. "She said, *Okay, Nani.* In a very small voice. *Okay.*"

"And after that?"

"After that she came three more Sundays. The twenty-sixth. The third. The tenth." Sharda Devi counted them on her fingers, three fingers, and then looked at the fingers and slowly folded them down. "She didn't say anything more. She didn't ask again. On the last Sunday she went up to the roof with Rahul and lost her kite and shouted at the boy next door, and I stood in the courtyard and listened to her shouting and I thought, *you see. It's better. He kept his word.*"

Her voice dropped.

"I didn't ask her. Do you understand? Not once in three weeks. I didn't lift her kurta. I didn't take her into the small room and close the door and say, *tell me the truth.* I was afraid of what the answer would be. And I was afraid that if I heard it, I would have to do something, and then I would lose them both." She opened her eyes. "I chose not to know. Every Sunday. And every Sunday at six o'clock I sent her home."

The radio in the kitchen had stopped. There was no sound in the house at all.

"On the twelfth of December," said Sharda Devi, "at ten past six in the morning, Rajiv telephoned me. He was very calm."

Dev said nothing. There was nothing to say that would not be smaller than what she had just said.

"At the cremation he stood next to me. In front of everyone. He put his hand on my shoulder, and he leaned down and he said very quietly, so no one else could hear: *Exam pressure, Mummyji. That's what it was. The doctors say it happens.* And then, *You still have Rahul. Sundays.*" She looked at Dev. "That was the price. He told me what it was, at my granddaughter's pyre, and I paid it. For three years. I paid it every Sunday at six o'clock when I sent Rahul home to that house."

"You took early retirement."

"I couldn't stand in front of a class any more. I kept looking at the girls' backs." She almost smiled again, terribly. "So I teach them at home now, for nothing. Five girls under a neem tree. As if that pays for anything. And every year I went to his foundation, and sat in the front row, and listened to my grandson say *my sister was my best friend*, and clapped." Her hands were very still on the cloth bag. "*Suno.* That's what he called it. Listen. Nobody in that hall had listened less than I had."

---

For a long time neither of them spoke. Dev put his glasses back on. Somewhere outside, in the lanes, someone's phone was playing a Christmas song, tinny and cheerful, and it passed and faded.

"Why are you telling me this, Mataji?"

Sharda Devi looked at him for a long moment, with the sharp, attentive eyes of a woman who had spent forty years listening for whispering at the back of a classroom.

"On Sunday," she said, "when you went up to my roof. Afterwards, when you were coming down, I was sitting under the tree. And I heard someone knock on the wall of my staircase. Three times." She paused. "Rahul has never knocked there. Not once. He goes down to her room at night, the small room, and knocks on the wall next to her bed. My bed is on the other side of that wall. Pari knew that. That's why she chose it, when they were small, before his rules, and slept here at Diwali. So she could knock and her Nani would hear."

Dev said nothing.

"I hear him every night, Inspector. Every night for three years. *Thak, thak, thak.* And I lie in my bed on the other side and I have never once knocked back." Her voice was perfectly level. "I didn't think I had the right. And then a policeman I have known for three weeks came down my stairs in the dark and did it."

She opened the cloth bag on her knees.

Inside was a plastic bag, the kind sweet shops use, folded over twice. And inside that, a school notebook: spiral-bound, with a laminated cover gone soft at the corners, and a sticker of a cartoon cat half peeled off the front. The spiral had been bent and straightened many times. Someone had written on the cover, in thick felt pen, in the large, careful capitals of a child who has just learnt that capitals look important.

"It was in her room at my house. On the shelf, between her storybooks. I found it the week after." Sharda Devi held it on her knees and did not open it. "I read it. All of it. I read the last page." Her thumb moved along the edge of the cover. "If Rahul reads that last page, he will know that his Nani was told. That his sister asked me, and I sent her home. And then he will have no one left at all." She looked up. "I couldn't burn it. It's the last of her handwriting in the world. I couldn't give it to him. So for three years it has been at the bottom of my trunk, under my wedding sari. And on Sunday night I put his father's chain in there on top of it."

She held the notebook out to him across the low table, the way Rahul had held out the envelope on the roof.

"You decide," she said. "Give it to him or don't. Give him all of it or some of it. I can't any more. I have decided enough things in my life, Inspector, and every one of them was wrong."

Dev looked at the notebook in her hands.

"Mataji, I can't—"

"You can," said Sharda Devi. "You already have. You decided on my roof. I saw your face when you came down." She did not lower her hands. "Take it. Please. *Main roungi bhi nahi.*"

He took it.

---

She would not let him walk her home. She went out through his mother's gate with her shawl over her head and her empty cloth bag folded under her arm, and he stood at the gate and watched her go down the lane in the fog, very small and very upright, until she turned the corner by the peepal tree and was gone.

His mother came out of the kitchen and stood in the door of the front room. She looked at the untouched tea and at the notebook in his hands, and she did not ask him anything. She picked up the gold-rimmed cups and took them away.

Dev sat down under the light and turned the notebook over.

On the front, in the felt-pen capitals, slightly crooked, was written:

*PROPERTY OF PARI SETHI. CLASS 6-B.*

And underneath, in smaller letters, squashed in at the bottom where she had run out of room:

*Agar mile toh lauta dena, warna Rahu-Ketu ko bata dungi.*

If found, return it. Otherwise I'll tell Rahu-Ketu.

He did not know he was smiling until he felt it, and it was the first time in nineteen days. It did not last long. He sat for a long time with the notebook closed on his knees, under the light in his mother's front room, and did not open it.
