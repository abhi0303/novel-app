@@ a2-scream

**Sunday, 14 June. 6:30 in the morning.**

You are out of bed and running before you are properly awake. The scream came from the end of the verandah.

Tara is in the study doorway, a tray of tea smashed at her feet, both hands over her mouth.

Your uncle is lying on the daybed under the window, on his side, one arm hanging down. He is still wearing yesterday's kurta. There is a basin on the floor beside him, and a bucket, and the room smells sour.

You have seen a great many dead people. You know before you touch him.

Behind you, doors are opening all down the house. Footsteps. Nandini's voice: "What is it? What's happened?" Sid, half-dressed. The Commander, limping, pulling on a shirt. Farida, last, in a white dressing gown, very pale.

Outside, the storm has not stopped. If anything it is worse. The sea beyond the window is a heaving white field, and the waves are breaking over the end of the jetty.

No boat is coming today.

@@ a2-study

You hold up a hand. "Nobody come in. Please. Nobody touch anything."

They stop in the doorway. For a moment, because you said it like you say it in the ward, they obey.

You are the only doctor for seven kilometres of open sea. Whatever happened in this room, you are going to have to see it now, before the house wakes up properly and people start moving things and making tea and crying on things.

Look carefully. Start with him.

@@ h-body

You kneel by the daybed.

He is cold, and stiff all through: rigor mortis is complete in the jaw, the arms and the legs. The blood has settled in the side he is lying on, a dark purple that does not blanch when you press it. Together that says he has been dead for four or five hours. Two o'clock, perhaps half past. Three at the latest.

He vomited several times in the night: the basin, the bucket, the floor. There are no marks on his neck, no bleeding in the whites of his eyes, no bruises on his arms. Nobody held a pillow over his face. His lips have a faint blue tinge.

You think about what you know. A heart patient. Nausea, vomiting, then a heart that slows and stumbles and stops. It is a textbook picture of one thing: an overdose of the heart drug digoxin. He was on digoxin.

But it is a picture, not a diagnosis. You need to know what he took.

@@ h-glass

On the desk, beside a lamp, stands a tall glass with a saucer next to it. Madhav's own solkadhi, the one Tara made for him. It is about a third full.

You lift it to the light without touching the rim. The pink drink has separated. At the bottom there is a thick sediment, and in the sediment, pale gritty specks, like ground nut or seed.

You sniff it. Kokum, coconut, garlic, a lot of green chilli. And under all that, something else: sharp and bitter.

You cover it with its saucer, wrap it in a clean pillowcase from the cupboard, and set it aside where nobody will knock it over. If anything in this room matters, it is this.

@@ h-spoon

A small silver tray on the desk, with a folded napkin and a silver spoon. He stirred his drink with it; Tara mentioned that at dinner, as part of the ritual.

The bowl of the spoon is not clean. A thin dried film of pink, and caught in it, the same pale grit you saw in the glass.

Whatever was at the bottom of that glass, someone stirred it in.

@@ h-pills

A plastic pill organiser with seven days and four times a day. Sunday morning is still full. Saturday's 10:30 PM box is empty: he took his night tablets.

Beside it, a strip of digoxin, 0.25 milligrams. Someone has written on the foil in marker: *started 1 June*. One a day. Thirteen days, thirteen tablets gone. Seventeen left.

It is exactly right. He did not take an extra dose of his own pills, and nobody slipped him a handful. Whatever slowed his heart, it was not the digoxin in this strip.

@@ h-typewriter

An old green Olivetti Lettera 32, the kind your father had in his office at the harbour. Beside it, face up, is one typed sheet:

:::doc
A STATEMENT BY MADHAV KAMAT
For release on 14 June 2026
:::

There is nothing under it. Whatever he meant to read out at lunch today, it was more than one page. Where are the pages?

Then you look at the machine itself. It has a cloth ribbon, and it is new, bright and black, used only a little. Your father used to say *the ribbon remembers everything*: every key that strikes it presses its letter into the cloth, back to front.

Carefully you unhook the two spools, roll the ribbon up and put it in your pocket, along with the cover page.

@@ h-drawer

The top drawer of the desk is unlocked. Inside, among bills and spectacles, is a hospital envelope.

Patient: Madhav R. Kamat, 66 M
Date: 18 May 2026
Impression: Adenocarcinoma of the pancreas, with liver metastases. Stage IV.

So that is the yellow in his eyes. He was dying, and he knew it, and he told no one.

Which makes you think. Who would murder a man who had only months left? Someone who could not wait for months. Someone for whom tomorrow's lunch was too soon.

@@ h-safe

The steel safe in the corner stands a few inches open.

Inside, the top shelf is empty except for two loose paper bands, the kind banks put round bundles of notes. On the lower shelf is a velvet tray of heavy gold jewellery, Nandini's, and a stack of property papers. Neither has been touched.

Someone took the cash and left the gold. Someone in a hurry, or someone who only needed cash.

@@ h-door

The study has a second door, a half-glass one that opens straight onto the side verandah, the same verandah your room is on.

It is unlatched.

Just inside it, on the polished floor, is a footprint. Half dried, as if the shoe was wet when it came in. A flat sole, quite small, with a thin strap mark: a woman's sandal. There is sand in it, pale beach sand, the kind on the path up from the jetty.

The verandah outside is soaked. Any other prints out there have been washed away by the rain.

@@ a2-assess

You stand up slowly and look round the room once more.

A man who was dying anyway. A pill count that is exactly right. A glass with something in it that does not belong. A spoon that stirred it in. A statement with no pages. A door onto the verandah, left open, and a small wet footprint.

You know enough medicine to know what else does what digoxin does. Foxglove. Oleander. And on this coast, a tree with glossy leaves and green fruit, whose seed the old women of Kerala called the suicide seed.

You write it all down, while it is fresh, in the notebook you always carry.

When you look up, they are all still in the doorway, watching you.

@@ a2-ann-yes

"I don't think this was his heart," you say. "Not on its own. I think something was put in his drink. I think he was poisoned."

For a moment nobody breathes.

Nandini sits down on the floor of the corridor as if her legs have been cut.

Sid says, "Are you serious? Are you actually serious?"

Tara starts to cry. "I made it," she says. "I made it at half past nine and I tasted it, like always, and it was fine. I left it on his desk at a quarter to ten under the saucer. I didn't, I would never..."

The Commander has gone very straight and very still, like a man on parade.

Farida speaks last. Her voice is calm and level.

"Doctor, be very careful," she says. "There will be a post-mortem. Until then, what you have just said is an accusation against every person in this house. Including you."

They are all looking at each other now. Whoever did this knows that you know. From now on, they will be careful.

@@ a2-ann-no

"It looks like his heart," you say. "I'm sorry. I think it happened in the night."

It is not a lie, exactly. It is just not all of it.

Nandini closes her eyes. Sid swears softly and walks away down the corridor. Tara is crying. The Commander puts a hand on her shoulder.

And Farida lets out a long breath, almost a sigh. "He knew it was coming," she says. "He told me he didn't have long. Thank God it was in his sleep."

She is very quick to be relieved, you think.

Let them believe it for now. If the person who did this thinks they have got away with it, they may not bother to cover their tracks.

@@ a2-radio

In the radio room the set hisses and crackles. Coast Guard Malvan has to be told: a sudden death on the island, and a doctor who wants the police.

"I should do it," the Commander says. "I know the procedure. And they know my voice."

"I can do it," Sid says, too fast. He is grey and shaking. "Let me do something. Please."

@@ a2-cg-sid

Sid sits at the set with the handset in both hands, and it does not go well. He forgets the call sign. He says "my father" and then "the deceased" and then "Baba" and then stops. The Coast Guard operator asks him to repeat, twice.

In the end the Commander puts a hand on his shoulder and takes the handset gently out of his fingers. "Give it here, boy." Sid goes and stands at the window with his back to all of you, and his shoulders shake.

@@ a2-cg

The answer comes back through the static, slow and clear.

The sea state is too high. No vessel can come out until the storm passes. Earliest arrival: Monday, twelve noon. A police officer from Malvan will come with the Coast Guard. Until then: do not move the deceased more than necessary. Preserve the scene. Keep everyone together.

Monday noon. That is more than a day away.

You look round the radio room, at the five other faces. One of them, you are almost sure, killed your uncle last night.

And for the next thirty hours, there is nowhere for any of you to go.
