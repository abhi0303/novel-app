@@ a4-start

**Sunday night.**

The generator thuds away behind the house. The storm screams round the roof. You sit on your bed with your notebook open, and your casebook, and go through it all again.

Somebody in this house put something in your uncle's glass between a quarter to ten, when Tara left it on his desk, and half past ten, when he drank it. Somebody wanted the statement he was going to read today never to be read.

The Coast Guard will be here at noon tomorrow. A tired police officer from Malvan will walk into this house, look at a dying man on digoxin who died of his heart, and ask you a simple question: *Doctor, what happened here?*

You had better have an answer.

@@ a4-fetch

Then you remember the typewriter.

You kept the study key all day. Nobody has been in. You take your torch along the dark verandah, unlock the door, and there it is on the desk, just as it was: the old Olivetti, with its ribbon still wound on the spools. The ribbon that remembers everything.

You unhook it, roll it up, and take it back to your room, with the cover page. You lock your door.

@@ a4-ribbon

You sit at the little desk in your room with the lamp pulled close, the ribbon in your lap, and the shaving mirror from the bathroom propped against the wall.

It is slow, terrible work. Every letter is pressed into the black cloth back to front, and you have to unwind it an inch at a time, hold it up to the mirror, and read it like a child learning letters. Sometimes the strokes overlap. Sometimes you have to guess a word from the shape of the ones around it.

It takes you more than three hours. By the end your eyes are burning and your hands are black with ink.

But you read all of it.

It is your uncle's voice. It is the statement he meant to read today, at lunch, on the twentieth anniversary of the night your father died. It is the truth about the *Neelkanth*.

You copy every word into your notebook. Then you sit for a long time in the lamplight, not moving.

@@ a4-commander

At eleven there is a soft knock on your door.

The Commander stands in the corridor in a dressing gown, leaning on a stick, looking older than he did this morning. "You asked me a question today," he says. "I said I would answer it tonight. May I come in?"

He sits on the only chair, with his bad leg out straight, and for a long time he looks at his hands.

Then he tells you. All of it. What happened in the office at Kamat Marine in June 2006, and what happened after. Who signed what, and who was paid, and who lied.

When he has finished he stands up with difficulty.

"I have been a coward for twenty years," he says. "Your father was the bravest man I ever sailed with, and I let them say he was a drunk. I will say all of this to the police tomorrow, Doctor. Whatever it costs me. I owe him that."

He goes out and closes the door quietly behind him.

@@ a4-watch

It is past midnight. The storm is at its worst.

Whoever did this knows you have been asking questions all day. They know you have been in the rooms. They know the police are coming at noon.

If you were them, what would you do tonight?

You could protect the evidence in the study. You could watch over the person most likely to be blamed. Or you could get some sleep and be sharp in the morning.

You cannot do all three.

@@ a4-guard-tara

You take a chair and a blanket and sit in the dark corridor of the east wing, across from Tara's door, with your torch switched off in your lap.

It is a long, cold wait. The house groans. The lighthouse beam sweeps the windows every eight seconds. Twice you nearly fall asleep.

At twenty to two, something moves at the end of the corridor.

A figure, barefoot, without a light, coming very quietly along the wall. It stops at Tara's door. A hand reaches for the handle.

You switch on the torch.

Farida.

She is in a white nightdress, and her face in the sudden light is a white mask. In her other hand, wrapped in a handkerchief, is a green fruit, cut open with a knife, the husk split and the kernel scooped out.

For one second neither of you moves.

Then you shout, and doors bang open: Sid, then the Commander, then Nandini. Tara opens her door and sees Farida standing there with the fruit in her hand, and screams.

@@ a4-caught

"I heard someone in the corridor," Farida says. Her voice is almost steady. "I came out to look. I found this on the floor outside her door. I was going to show you in the morning."

Nobody believes her. You can see it on every face. But she does not stop, and she does not raise her voice, and she does not look away from you.

"I will speak to the police, Doctor," she says at last. "Not to you."

She walks back to her room. The Commander and Sid take turns sitting outside her door until dawn.

You do not sleep at all. You sit with the cut fruit on a saucer in front of you, and think about where she was taking it, and why.

@@ a4-guard-study

You let yourself into the study and sit in your uncle's chair in the dark, with the glass and everything else you have found close by.

It is a long, cold wait. The room still smells of him. The lighthouse beam sweeps across the walls every eight seconds.

At about twenty-five to two you see a light through the half-glass door: a small torch, shielded with a hand, moving along the verandah towards the staff rooms at the end of the wing. You hold your breath. It does not stop at the study.

Ten minutes later it comes back the other way, quickly, and is gone.

You do not dare leave the room to follow it. When you do finally look out, the verandah is empty, and the rain has washed it clean.

@@ a4-sleep

You lock your door, push a chair under the handle, and lie down in your clothes.

You dream about your father. He is standing on the deck of the *Neelkanth* in the rain, and he is laughing, and he is saying something to you that you cannot hear over the wind.

You wake at seven with your heart pounding. The storm has dropped. Grey light, and gulls.

@@ a4-morning

**Monday, 15 June. Breakfast.**

The storm has blown itself out. The sea is still running high, but the sky is lifting, and the radio says the Coast Guard has left Malvan.

Everyone is in the dining room. Nobody is eating.

Farida puts down her coffee cup. "In fairness to all of us," she says, "the doctor searched only three rooms yesterday. When the police come, they will search everything. I suggest we open our bags now, here, together. Then nobody can say anything was hidden."

It is reasonable. It is so reasonable that nobody can refuse.

The bags come out onto the dining table, one by one. Sid's, with the cash in it, which makes him go red and then white. The Commander's. Nandini's. Farida's, very neat.

Then Tara's cloth tote bag. Sid tips it out.

Two phone chargers. A power bank. A cardigan. A diary.

And a green fruit, cut open with a knife, the husk split and the kernel scooped out. The cut is still white and wet with sap.

Tara stares at it. "That's not mine," she whispers. "That's not mine. I've never..."

Everyone looks at her. Then, one by one, they look at you.

@@ a4-planted

"No," you say. "It isn't hers."

They all turn.

"I searched that bag yesterday afternoon," you say. "Every pocket. There was nothing in it but chargers and a cardigan and a diary. And that fruit was cut in the last few hours. Look at it. The sap on the cut is still wet."

You pick it up on a napkin and hold it out so they can all see.

"Someone came into this wing last night and put it in her bag. Someone who knew her father drowned on the *Neelkanth*. Someone who needed the police to find a reason to look at Tara and not at them."

There is a long silence in the dining room.

You watch their faces. Sid, open-mouthed. Nandini, frowning. The Commander, slowly nodding. Tara, crying with relief.

And one of them who says nothing at all, and does not look surprised.

@@ a4-tara-blamed

Nobody says anything for a long moment.

Then Sid says, "Her father was on that boat. Wasn't he? I heard you talking. Her father drowned on the *Neelkanth*." He looks round the table. "She made the drink. She took it in there. She's the only one who touched it."

"I tasted it," Tara says. "I tasted it, I would never..."

"You would say that," says Nandini quietly, and moves her chair a little away.

The Commander puts up a hand. "Enough. That is for the police." But he does not look at Tara.

Farida says nothing at all. She only folds her napkin, very neatly, and puts it beside her plate.

You look at the cut fruit on the table. You did not search Tara's bag yesterday. You have no way of knowing how long it has been in there.
