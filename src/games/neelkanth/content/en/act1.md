Before the island, there is you.

You were fourteen when your father drowned. Anant Kamat was the skipper of the trawler *Neelkanth*, and on the night of 14 June 2006 she went down in a storm off the Vengurla rocks with four men on board. The inquiry said he had been drinking at the wheel. Two crewmen who survived said so.

Your father did not drink. Nobody believed you.

His elder brother, Madhav, paid for your school and your medical college, and at nineteen you stopped answering his calls. That was fifteen years ago.

@@ a1-boat

**Saturday, 13 June 2026. Malvan jetty, 3:30 in the afternoon.**

The sky over the Arabian Sea is the colour of an old bruise. Every fishing boat in Malvan is pulled up and tied down for the monsoon ban, and the only thing moving on the water is a white launch called *Saagarika*, rocking at the end of the jetty.

"Dr. Kamat." A tall old man with a Navy cap and a bad knee holds out a hand to help you aboard. "Vinod Rane. Commander, retired. I worked for your uncle for twenty-two years, and I knew your father. We had better go. This weather is coming in faster than they said."

The engine roars. Malvan falls away behind you.

You take the letter out of your bag and read it again, though you know it by heart. Folded inside it, as always, is the yellowed newspaper cutting you have carried in your wallet since you were fourteen.

"He's not well, you know," the Commander says, watching the sea, not you. "Whatever he wants to tell you. Let him say it."

Forty minutes later the island comes up out of the grey: a hump of black rock and green jungle, a white lighthouse on its highest point, a long low house with a red-tiled roof, and a wooden jetty with a boathouse beside it.

"Neelkanth Dweep," the Commander says. "He bought it in 2010 and named it after the boat. Neelkanth, the one who drank the poison so the world could live." He cuts the engine. "Everyone else came over yesterday. The caretakers took their boat back to Malvan this morning, to be with their families for the storm. So it's just us now. Seven." He counts on his fingers, as if to be sure. "Seven, with you."

@@ a1-jetty

The boathouse smells of diesel and wet rope. You haul on lines with Sid and the Commander while the wind picks up and the women stand under the eaves.

Farida is looking at the tree by the path.

"Othalanga," she says, half to herself. "I haven't seen one since I was a girl. We had one behind my grandmother's house in Kozhikode. We were told never, ever to touch the fruit."

"Why?" Tara asks.

"Because the seed stops your heart. They call it the suicide tree in Kerala. My grandmother used to say that more than one unhappy wife had put it in her husband's food, and the doctors wrote *heart attack* every time." She laughs. "Old women's stories."

"Not entirely," the Commander says, tying off a line. "Cerbera odollam. They taught us about it in the Navy in 1979. It grows all along this coast."

"Sir won't let anyone cut it down," Tara says. "He says it was here before the house."

You look at it. Three green fruits hang on its lowest branch, in easy reach of anyone walking past.

At ten to five, Farida pulls her dupatta tight. "I'm going to change before the rain," she says, and walks back up the path alone.

The rest of you stay until a quarter past five, when the first heavy drops start hitting the boathouse roof.

@@ a1-storm

The storm arrives at six.

It comes in like a train. The sea goes from grey to white in ten minutes. The palms bend flat. Rain hits the windows so hard that the house seems to shake, and the lights flicker and hold, because the generator is new and Madhav paid a lot for it.

You stand at the window of your room and watch it, and you are fourteen again.

The night the *Neelkanth* sank there was a storm like this. You remember your mother sitting by the phone. You remember the knock at four in the morning. You remember the newspaper three days later, and a word that your father would have hated: *drunk*.

He did not drink. He did not even drink at weddings. He used to say the sea was the only thing he needed to keep his head clear for.

Somewhere in this house is a man who says he owes you the truth.

@@ a1-dinner

Dinner is at eight, in the long dining room, with the storm hammering at the shutters.

The caretakers left everything ready: fish curry, rice, fried bombil, and a big jug of pink solkadhi, the cool coconut-and-kokum drink of the Konkan coast. Everyone has a glass from the jug. Madhav does not. "Mine comes later," he says. "Tara makes it the way I like it. Extra chilli, no sugar. With my pills at half past ten, every night, like a schoolboy."

"I'll leave it on your desk at a quarter to ten, Sir, as usual," Tara says. "Under a saucer."

You find yourself noticing things, the way you do on a night shift.

Nandini, in her green silk, eating almost nothing.

Sid, in his hoodie, drinking too fast. "So what's this big lunch about, Baba? Are you selling the company? Am I finally fired from the family?"

Tara, in a mustard-yellow kurta, watching your uncle the way a nurse watches a monitor.

The Commander, in a pressed shirt, cutting his fish with Navy precision.

And Farida, still in white, with a fresh plaster on her left thumb. She sees you looking at it. "Paper cut," she says lightly, before anyone has asked.

Then your uncle puts down his spoon.

"Tomorrow at lunch," he says, "I am going to read a statement. Farida has the papers for what comes after. It is about the *Neelkanth*." The table goes very still. "After that, all of you can decide what you think of me. {{name}} first."

Nobody says anything. The Commander's fork stops halfway to his mouth. Nandini looks at her plate. Sid laughs once, a short ugly sound.

Farida dabs her lips with her napkin and says, "Madhav, you are tired. Let's talk about it in the morning."

"No," he says. "We have talked enough."

The front porch is sheltered from the worst of the wind. Nandini sits in a cane chair with a glass of turmeric milk, wrapped in a shawl, watching the lighthouse beam sweep across the rain.

"Sit," she says. "I can't bear the house tonight."

You sit with her from about half past nine. She talks, more than you expect: about the hotels she runs in Goa and Ganpatipule, about Sid, who has never forgiven her for not being his mother, about your uncle, who has not been himself since April.

Later, as the house goes quiet, you lie awake and listen to the storm, and think about your uncle's face at dinner. *Twenty years.* What could he possibly say tomorrow that would change anything?
