**Monday, 15 June. Twelve noon.**

The Coast Guard boat comes in round the point, grey and white, pitching in the swell, and ties up at the jetty with some difficulty.

Two people come up the steps. Assistant Commandant Meera Shirodkar of the Coast Guard, small, brisk, in a dark-blue uniform. And Police Sub-Inspector Ajay Gawde of Malvan police station, in a wet raincoat, who looks as if he has been seasick for most of the way.

They hear the bare facts in the hall. They look at the body. They look at the six of you.

Then PSI Gawde sits down heavily at the dining table and takes out a notebook.

"Doctor," he says. "You are the only medical person here. You have been here the whole time. They tell me you have been asking questions since yesterday morning." He waits. "The deceased had a heart condition. He had cancer. On paper this is a natural death. So I will ask you once, and I will write down what you say. What happened in this house?"

Everyone is looking at you.

"Farida Shaikh killed him," you say.

There is a silence in the dining room so complete that you can hear the sea.

Farida smiles slightly. "Doctor, you are upset..."

"He was going to read a statement today," you say, "about the *Neelkanth*. About what really happened in 2006, and who covered it up. You covered it up. And in a few months you were going to be a judge of the High Court."

You put it in front of the policeman, piece by piece. The glass, with the bitter grit at the bottom. The tree by the jetty that she named before anyone else could, and the stone mortar where the seed was ground. The reason, in your uncle's own words. And the proof that it was her hand, and not anybody else's.

You watch it land on PSI Gawde. You watch him look up from his notebook at her, and keep looking.

Farida sits very still for a long moment. Then she does something nobody expects. She laughs, quietly, and puts her hands flat on the table.

"Twenty years," she says. "Do you know what I was in 2006, Doctor? A junior lawyer, thirty-two years old, with a mother to support, at a shipping company that paid well. Your uncle called me at midnight and said, *Farida, the certificate on the Neelkanth expired in March, and the insurance won't pay, and the families will sue, and we are finished.* So I fixed it. I found a surveyor who would put a February date on a renewal for fifty thousand rupees. And I sat with the two boys who survived, frightened village boys, and I told them what to say. *The skipper was drinking.* It was the only story that saved the company."

She looks at you. "I built my whole life on top of that night. Every case, every client, every year of it. And then in April he finds out he is dying and decides he wants to be forgiven. On the twentieth anniversary. At lunch. With his lawyer sitting at the table." Her voice does not rise. "I begged him. On the lighthouse. In the study. He wouldn't listen."

"So you picked the fruit," you say.

"I had already picked it," Farida says. "I'm a careful woman."

She tells the rest without being asked, as precisely as if she were giving evidence. The fruit, split in the boathouse with a knife from the kitchen, and the knife slipping on the husk. The kernel ground in the stone mortar at twenty to ten, while Nandini sat on the porch and Madhav sat in his lighthouse. The paste, carried along the verandah and stirred into his glass under its saucer, with his own silver spoon. The last visit at a quarter past ten, to beg one last time, and to take the typed pages from his desk when he turned away. The bamba, burning in the yard. And last night, the second fruit, for Tara's bag.

"He drank it at half past ten," she says. "He always did."

PSI Gawde closes his notebook. "Farida Shaikh," he says, "I am arresting you for the murder of Madhav Kamat."

**Fourteen months later.**

The memorial is on the Malvan jetty, where the *Neelkanth* used to tie up: a slab of black Konkan stone with four names cut into it.

*Anant Kamat, skipper. Joseph Fernandes. Santosh Tandel. Ibrahim Mulla.*

Underneath, in smaller letters: *They were not to blame.*

The 2006 inquiry was reopened in January. Surveyor P. Naik, now seventy-four, confessed to backdating the certificate. The two survivors, middle-aged men now, wept in the witness box and said they had been told what to say. Cdr Vinod Rane testified for two days and did not spare himself. Kamat Marine paid the families what it should have paid twenty years ago, and more, under the will your uncle signed on the last Saturday of his life.

Farida Shaikh was convicted in March. She never became a judge.

Tara stands beside you at the memorial, holding a garland of marigolds. "He did say it," she says. "In the end. Through you."

You put your hand on the cold stone, on your father's name.

The sea is calm today. The monsoon is coming, but not yet.

"Farida Shaikh killed him," you say.

Farida does not even look surprised. She folds her hands on the table and turns to the policeman, and for twenty minutes she takes your case apart, calmly, like a senior advocate in a courtroom she has stood in a thousand times.

What exactly is the doctor saying went into the glass, and how? What is the doctor's evidence that anyone ground anything? Why would a respected lawyer, about to become a judge, throw all of it away? What actually links her, and not Tara, or Sid, or anyone else in this house, to that study at that time?

You answer. But there are gaps in what you can show, and she finds every one of them. You watch PSI Gawde's face go from interested to tired to embarrassed.

"Doctor," he says at last, kindly. "I understand you have lost a family member. But I need evidence, not a story."

The body goes to the district hospital at Kudal. The post-mortem takes a week, and finds what a busy government pathologist expects to find in a sixty-six-year-old man with stage-four cancer and a heart condition: *cardiac arrest*. The glass is sent to a laboratory in Pune and the report comes back *inconclusive*. The case is closed in September.

In August, Farida Shaikh is sworn in as a judge of the Bombay High Court. There is a photograph in the newspaper.

The statement your uncle meant to read is never read.

You think about it for years: whether you were right, and couldn't prove it, or whether you were wrong all along. Somewhere in what you found, there was an answer. You did not put it together well enough to make anyone listen.

@@ end-tara

"Tara Fernandes," you say.

Tara makes a small sound, like something breaking.

You lay it out. Her father drowned on the *Neelkanth*. She took the job under her own name, waiting. She made the drink. She carried it into the study. She was the only person who touched that glass. She was next door all night.

It fits. It fits terribly well. PSI Gawde writes it all down, and when he looks up at her his face has changed.

"I tasted it," Tara says. "I tasted it, Doctor. You know I did. I wanted him to say it. I wanted him *alive* to say it."

They take her to the Coast Guard boat at two o'clock. She does not struggle. At the top of the jetty steps she turns round and looks at you, and you will remember that look for a long time.

Behind you, on the verandah, the others watch her go, and nobody says a word.

The boat pulls away. You stand on the jetty in the thin rain, and something cold and certain turns over in your stomach.

You will never be quite sure.

@@ end-sid

"Sid Kamat," you say.

Sid goes white. "What? No. No, no, no..."

You lay it out. The debts to dangerous men. The threat on his phone. The eight lakh rupees from his father's safe, at the bottom of his bag. The email about the new will that would give half of everything away. He lied about where he was. He was alone, and close to the study, in the hour that mattered.

"I'm a thief!" he shouts. "I told you I'm a thief! I'm not a murderer! I saw someone, I *told* you, I saw a woman go in..."

But a man who lied once about where he was can be made to look like he is lying again. PSI Gawde has the cash in a plastic bag on the table in front of him. That is the kind of evidence a policeman understands.

They take Sid to the boat in handcuffs, still shouting.

The others stand on the verandah and watch him go, and none of them will meet your eye.

Later, you will lie awake and think about the woman in white that he swore he saw. Whether he made her up. Or whether he didn't.

"Nandini Kamat," you say.

She looks at you for a long moment, and then she laughs, very softly, without any humour at all.

You lay it out. The marriage that ended in April. The divorce papers, drawn up in May. The new will that would have given half of everything away, and a death that came just in time to stop it. The kitchen where she ground and washed and cleaned.

"You too, {{name}}?" she says. "Of course. The second wife. It's always the second wife."

PSI Gawde writes it down. The divorce papers go into a plastic bag.

She walks to the Coast Guard boat by herself, upright, her grey saree whipping round her ankles in the wind, and does not look back at the house.

The others watch from the verandah. Nobody goes down to the jetty with her.

Weeks later, at a hearing in Sindhudurg, you watch Nandini's lawyer ask you very politely how you could be so sure. And you realise you are not.

@@ end-commander

"Commander Vinod Rane," you say.

The old man straightens in his chair, the way a man does on parade.

You lay it out. The poison manual on his shelf, marked at the page. The five per cent of the company that came to him three weeks after the *Neelkanth* sank. The statement that would have told the world what he knew and kept quiet about for twenty years. A man who knew that tree better than anyone.

PSI Gawde writes it all down.

The Commander does not argue. He only says, quietly, "I deserve something, Doctor. For 2006, I deserve a great deal. But not this."

He limps down to the Coast Guard boat on his stick without any help, and salutes the Coast Guard officer, out of habit, before he steps aboard.

Behind you the others watch in silence. Tara is crying.

You wanted somebody to pay for 2006. Somebody is paying. You tell yourself that is something.

It doesn't feel like anything at all.

@@ end-none

You look round the room at the five faces.

"I don't know," you say. "I'm sorry. I can't prove anything."

PSI Gawde nods, as if this is what he expected, and writes something short in his notebook. Natural causes, in a sick old man, on an island, in a storm.

The body goes to Kudal. The death certificate says *cardiac arrest*. Your uncle is cremated in Malvan on Wednesday. Half the coast comes. Everyone says what a generous man he was.

The statement he meant to read is never read. The *Neelkanth* stays where the inquiry left it in 2006, at the bottom of the sea off Vengurla, with a drunk at the wheel.

Your father's name stays where it was.

Some nights, at the end of a long shift, you think about the glass on his desk, and the tree by the jetty, and the six of you on that island in the storm. Somebody there knew exactly what happened.

It was not you.
